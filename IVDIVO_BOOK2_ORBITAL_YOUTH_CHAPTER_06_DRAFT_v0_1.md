# ORBITAL YOUTH
## BOOK TWO
### CHAPTER SIX — THE RING PEOPLE WANT

**Status:** WORKING DRAFT v0.1 / NOT CANON

Ring Six did not look unfinished.

That was the first problem.

Ollie had spent two mornings entering it through service access: unfinished panels, tagged conduits, open diagnostic bays, people in maintenance layers carrying tools and arguing about verification records.

From that side, the ring was obviously a project.

From the residential side, it was a home waiting for people to catch up.

The corridor opened into a broad commons under full local gravity. Not Earth gravity exactly, but close enough that Ollie's body stopped thinking about every step. Light came from a ceiling surface that made no attempt to imitate a blue sky. It was warmer near the seating areas, cooler along the transit edge, and shifted slightly as more people entered.

Future residents were already everywhere.

A little girl was lying on the floor under a table, measuring something with both hands.

Two adults stood inside an empty kitchen module arguing about where a storage wall should go.

Someone had placed three shoes outside a bedroom door that did not officially belong to them yet.

Ollie looked at the shoes.

Iri noticed.

"Commissioning violation," they said.

Ollie looked at them.

"Seriously?"

"No."

They kept walking.

Mara Saye led the resident tour without behaving like a person leading a tour.

No speech about the future.

No welcome presentation.

She carried a list and stopped whenever somebody had a real question.

"Laundry circulation?" one resident asked.

Mara pointed down the branch corridor.

"Shared node there. Household units in sections C and D. If you use the shared system, dry-cycle return depends on load."

"School route?"

"East connector until the bridge schedule changes next month. After that, central spine."

"Can the kitchen partition stay open during quiet cycle?"

"Household choice unless your acoustic boundary starts affecting the next unit."

Nobody wrote any of this down because the residence system already knew who had asked.

Ollie followed behind the group with an apprentice tablet he was not supposed to use for anything except approved observations.

He kept trying to see systems.

Air distribution above the kitchen wall.

Partition tracks.

Floor access.

Emergency isolation markers.

The route of the environmental loop behind the plant wall.

People kept getting in the way.

A family stopped in front of him to argue about bedrooms.

Not politely.

"You said I could take the outer room," a girl said.

She looked maybe sixteen. Tight dark curls pulled into a knot. Oversized local team jacket. She was standing in the middle of an empty residential module like she had already lived there long enough to resent the architecture.

An older man—her father, probably—held up both hands.

"I said we'd look at it."

"We're looking."

"Your brother's environmental profile makes the inner room easier."

"The inner room has no exterior view."

"He does not care about the view."

"Exactly."

A younger boy sat on the floor between them and said, "I care a little."

The girl turned on him.

"Traitor."

He grinned.

Mara stopped at the doorway.

"Lea."

The girl looked over.

"What?"

"You are blocking the circulation check."

Lea moved exactly one step.

"Better?"

"Enough."

Mara entered and checked the environmental boundary indicator while the family kept arguing.

Ollie stayed outside.

Iri leaned against the wall beside him.

"That's Lea."

"I gathered."

"Her family has had three temporary housing assignments since the overhaul schedule started."

Ollie looked at the empty room again.

Lea had opened one storage panel and was testing how far it extended.

"Why three?"

"Compatibility. Timing. One host household changed. One temporary module got reassigned when another family needed a narrower atmosphere range."

"They were moved?"

"Everybody was moved. That's what temporary means."

"I know what temporary means."

Iri looked at him.

Ollie looked back.

"Fine."

Inside, Lea's father was trying to explain that the outer bedroom had a longer route to the household environmental fallback.

Lea did not care.

"It has the view."

"You can use the common view deck."

"That is not my room."

Ollie understood that sentence more than he wanted to.

On Earth, his room had not been special. A desk, storage, maker parts in places they were not supposed to be, one window facing another row of houses.

He had never had to argue that the window belonged to the meaning of the room.

In Vela, a private exterior view was not impossible.

It was just not universal.

The station could fabricate wall surfaces, furniture, dishes, clothes and a thousand decorative things without anybody worrying about whether a teenager could pay for them.

It could not fabricate another exterior edge at the same place in the ring.

Geometry remained rude.

Mara finished the check.

"Environmental boundary is good. You can continue arguing now."

Lea's father looked at her.

"Thank you."

"I contribute where I can."

The tour moved on.

Lea followed last, still looking back at the outer bedroom.

She noticed Ollie watching.

"You maintenance?"

"Apprentice."

"Earth?"

"Yes."

"Can you make the inner room have the outer view?"

Ollie looked at the ring curve.

"No."

"Then I don't need you yet."

She walked past.

Iri waited until she was out of earshot.

"Welcome to resident relations."

Ollie smiled.

But the line stayed with him.

Not because Lea had been rude.

Because she was right.

The ring did not exist so he could understand it.

---

The school route was six minutes from Lea's future module if the east connector was aligned.

Eleven if it was not.

The youth commons was one level below, beside a shared repair space and two adaptable courts whose floor geometry could change between sports.

A food area opened onto the same corridor, and people kept stopping there even though none of the services were officially active yet.

An empty place for people to eat still made people stand around and imagine eating there.

That seemed to Ollie like evidence of something.

The tour crossed a narrow environmental threshold into a section designed for residents with different humidity and atmospheric needs.

The transition was almost invisible.

No airlock.

No dramatic door.

A soft light marked the boundary, and the pressure in Ollie's sinuses changed just enough for him to notice.

One of the residents beside him did not cross.

They stayed on the other side while their partner continued.

Ollie looked back.

The resident waved.

"I can visit after final calibration. Today I'm still on temporary limit."

Their partner rolled their eyes.

"Which means I get to choose the kitchen without them."

"I will remember this betrayal."

The group laughed.

Ollie did too.

Then he wrote down the threshold number.

Iri saw.

"You know the system has that."

"I know."

"Then why write it?"

Ollie thought about Aru asking whether he wanted the eleven-second delay to be interesting.

"Because I noticed it."

Iri accepted that.

The tour ended in a commons overlooking the inner structural spine.

Not the best view on the station.

Ollie had already learned that saying "best" about a view was a reliable way to start an argument.

This one showed transfer vehicles crossing between rings and service frames moving along the central structure. Farther beyond, Vela's outer construction units caught sunlight in hard white edges.

Lea sat on the floor by the transparent boundary with her younger brother.

Her father was talking with Mara.

Ollie could hear only fragments.

"—if the date moves again—"

"—not promising what certification hasn't cleared—"

"—we've already reorganized school twice—"

Mara did not soften the answer.

"I know. I still won't give you a date the ring hasn't earned."

Lea's father rubbed both hands over his face.

Then nodded.

Ollie looked away.

For the first time, the opening schedule felt less like a line on a project board.

Someone's school route was sitting inside it.

Someone's bedroom argument.

Someone's ability to live with the same people for more than a few weeks.

He had wanted real work.

Real work had people waiting at the other end.

That part had not been in the fantasy.

---

They left the residential sector through a staging corridor used by households preparing for transfer.

Storage modules lined one wall.

Not packed with precious possessions exactly.

Most ordinary objects could be remade.

The modules held things people did not want remade.

Hand-marked art.

Old physical books.

Sports equipment worn into a particular shape.

A child's badly repaired model vehicle.

A coat with patches from three cities.

Things whose value was not scarcity but history.

Ollie slowed beside one open module.

A man was trying to fit a large curved instrument case into it.

It did not fit.

He rotated it.

Still no.

"It collapses," Iri said.

The man looked over.

"It should not collapse."

"Then it does not fit."

"That is why I am rotating it."

Iri nodded respectfully.

"Good luck."

They kept walking.

"Who is he?" Ollie asked.

"Tomas. Service coordinator on Outer Three. Lea's cousin. Or uncle. Something."

"You don't know?"

"I know what he does. Family trees are optional."

Tomas caught up with them three minutes later, carrying the instrument case separately.

"You," he said to Iri. "You cursed it."

"I described geometry."

"Same thing."

He looked at Ollie.

"Earth apprentice?"

"Ollie."

"Tomas. You're on commissioning?"

"Supervised."

"Good. Tell them to open the ring on time."

Iri said, "That is not how commissioning works."

"Then improve commissioning."

Tomas shifted the case to his other hand.

Ollie asked, "You're moving here too?"

"Eventually."

"Temporary housing?"

Tomas laughed once.

"Temporary sleeping. Housing implies I see it."

Ollie waited.

Tomas pointed toward the central spine.

"My current module is on Outer One. My contribution cycle starts on Outer Three. My sister's children are temporarily on Residential Two. I spend enough time in transfers that the station should assign me a seat with my name on it."

"Can't your role move?"

Tomas looked at him.

Not offended.

Just tired.

"It could. I don't want it to."

That answer stopped Ollie.

"Why?"

"Because I chose it."

Simple.

No survival requirement.

No wage calculation.

Tomas did the work because it was his work.

The problem was not that he could not afford to stop.

The problem was that temporary housing had made the life he had chosen take hours longer to live.

"How much longer?" Ollie asked.

"Depends on alignment. Good day? Forty minutes each way. Bad day, over an hour."

"That's not terrible."

Tomas looked at him.

Ollie immediately regretted speaking.

"For a week," Tomas said, "no. For six months, while every family schedule is built around it, tell me again."

"Right."

"My partner disagrees," Tomas added. "She wants Ring Six delayed until every mixed zone has another full run."

Ollie looked at him.

"And you don't?"

"I want it safe. I also want people to stop saying delay has no cost because nobody gets injured by waiting."

He shifted the case again.

"Sometimes nothing dramatic happens. Your life just becomes stupid for longer."

Iri smiled faintly.

"That should be on a civic wall."

"I have submitted it repeatedly."

Tomas turned toward another transfer lane.

"Open it safely," he told Ollie.

Then he was gone.

Ollie looked at Iri.

"Everybody says that like those are two separate instructions."

"Sometimes they are."

"That's not helpful."

"Correct."

They reached the commissioning entry.

Above the door, the status changed from RESIDENT TOUR to OCCUPANCY TEST — CONTROLLED.

Iri scanned in.

Ollie followed.

He had spent the morning wanting a technical problem.

Now he wanted the test to be boring.

---

At first, it was.

That was the best part.

Thirty-two participants moved through the test sector under normal residential instructions.

Not in formation.

Not like a drill.

They carried bags, stopped to talk, changed routes, entered kitchens, opened partitions, used shared facilities and crossed environmental thresholds in patterns designed to resemble ordinary life.

A child asked if they were allowed to run.

Mara said, "In the same places you will be allowed to run after opening."

The child ran immediately.

Ollie sat at an apprentice observation station behind the main commissioning line.

He could see fixed environmental sensors, partition state, thermal load, airflow response and movement density.

No control authority.

No hidden layers.

Exactly what Mara had promised.

Aru was at the next station, monitoring structural damping.

Iri stood closer to the resident flow with a handheld interface and local route assignment.

The first twenty minutes were almost insultingly normal.

People moved.

Systems adjusted.

Indicators stayed green.

Ollie started to relax.

Then zone twelve settled eleven seconds late.

He noticed because he had been waiting for it.

Not the same eleven seconds as the previous run.

Ten point eight.

Close enough to be irritating.

He marked the timestamp.

No threshold violation.

No warning.

The pressure difference corrected itself.

"Again?" Aru asked without looking over.

Ollie nodded.

"Ten point eight."

"Still inside tolerance."

"Yes."

Aru turned their head slightly.

Ollie added, "I know."

The next group crossed the zone boundary.

Normal settling.

Next one.

Normal.

The delay vanished.

Ollie frowned.

He checked occupancy density.

Similar.

Partition sequence.

Different.

Movement path.

Different.

He marked both.

On the floor, Iri stopped beside one resident.

Not an emergency.

Just a conversation.

The resident touched the side of their neck and said something Ollie could not hear.

Iri checked their handheld.

Then looked toward the control line.

A small note appeared in the shared observation channel.

RESIDENT REPORT — BRIEF AIRFLOW / PRESSURE DISCOMFORT BEFORE SETTLING

Ollie read it.

Fixed sensors had shown no unsafe condition at the reported time.

He opened the trace.

The complaint came seven seconds before the measurable drift.

Interesting.

He marked it:

SUBJECTIVE REPORT — CORRELATE IF REPEATED

Aru saw the tag.

"Why subjective?"

"Because it is."

"So is your interest."

Ollie looked at them.

Aru returned to the structural display.

"The report is a report. Your confidence in it is separate."

Ollie changed the label.

RESIDENT REPORT — CORRELATE IF REPEATED

Better.

Mara appeared behind them.

"Status?"

Aru said, "Structure normal."

Ollie said, "Zone twelve settling delay repeated once. Ten point eight seconds. One resident reported discomfort seven seconds before fixed-sensor drift."

Mara looked at the trace.

"Threshold?"

"No violation."

"Repeatable?"

"Not yet."

"Cause?"

"Unknown."

Mara nodded.

She did not say good.

She did not say bad.

She tagged the affected sector for pause after the current participant group cleared.

"Pull the logs. No new occupancy there until comparison."

Housing liaison beside her looked over.

"Static safety still green."

"I can see that."

"We lose the afternoon sequence if you stop twelve."

"Then we lose the afternoon sequence."

No argument beyond that.

The affected residents exited normally.

A soft light closed the next route into zone twelve.

People complained because they had to detour.

Nobody panicked.

That made the anomaly feel smaller.

And more real.

Ollie watched the trace flatten after occupancy changed.

No broken component.

No obvious failure.

A home that passed every static safety check and occasionally took too long to become what it was supposed to be after people moved through it.

He thought of Lea standing in the bedroom doorway.

Tomas carrying the instrument case.

The little girl's shoes already outside a room.

Mara's pause meant another test sequence.

Another day, maybe more.

That should not matter if safety required it.

It still mattered.

Iri came into control after the residents cleared.

"The person who reported it is fine," they said. "They said it felt like the room pushed back before the air changed."

Ollie looked at the fixed-sensor trace.

"That's not physically precise."

"People are terrible instruments."

"Yes."

"They are also inside the room."

Ollie looked at them.

Iri did not make the point again.

Mara assigned log review positions.

A notification appeared on Ollie's wrist.

His afternoon schedule still contained an optional orientation rotation in a higher-visibility planning team.

He had wanted it when he arrived.

A broader view of commissioning. More people. More responsibility eventually.

He looked at zone twelve.

"Can I stay on diagnostics?" he asked.

Mara did not answer immediately.

"You'll lose the planning rotation."

"I know."

"It will be reassigned."

"I know."

"Why stay?"

Ollie looked at the trace.

He almost said because something is wrong.

He did not know that yet.

"Because I want to know whether the delay has a condition."

Mara considered him.

Then moved his name from PLANNING ORIENTATION to DIAGNOSTIC SUPPORT.

"Observation only."

"Understood."

Iri looked at the updated assignment.

"You gave up the visible one."

Ollie turned.

"What?"

"Nothing."

They walked back toward the resident channel.

Ollie watched them go.

Then looked again at the eleven-second delay.

The ring still looked finished.

That was no longer reassuring.
