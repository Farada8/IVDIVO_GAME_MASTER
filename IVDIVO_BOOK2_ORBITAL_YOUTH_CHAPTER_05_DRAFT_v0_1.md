# ORBITAL YOUTH
## BOOK TWO
### CHAPTER FIVE — FIRST SHIFT

**Status:** WORKING DRAFT v0.1 / POST-MONEY CORRECTED / NOT CANON

Ollie learned what orbital maintenance looked like at 06:42, when Mara Saye gave him a cloth.

Not a diagnostic rig.

Not a system map.

A cloth.

"Bay four," she said. "Inspection surfaces first."

Ollie looked at the cloth.

Then at Mara.

She was already checking something on a wall display.

"Inspection surfaces," he repeated.

"Yes."

"As in clean them."

"You're adapting quickly."

That was the entire conversation.

Ring Six commissioning service bay was awake before most of the residential sections it was supposed to serve.

The bay curved along the inner maintenance side of the ring, a long strip of open workstations, sealed tool storage, diagnostic frames and service access points. Half the ceiling was missing in the deliberate way ceilings disappeared in places designed to let people reach what was behind them.

Crew members moved through the space with the quiet speed of people who had already begun before Ollie arrived.

Some were human.

Some were not.

Aru was not.

Ollie had met them at orientation the evening before and had spent most of the introduction trying not to stare at the wrong things.

Aru's body was narrow, taller than Ollie by maybe half a head, built around a pale composite frame with dark flexible joints and hands that had four long fingers instead of five. Nothing about the body tried very hard to imitate human skin.

They wore a maintenance vest anyway.

Tool loops remained useful across civilizations.

Aru was currently arguing with another technician about a missing inspection stamp.

"I signed it," Aru said.

"You signed the replacement."

"The replacement replaced the original."

"The record doesn't know that."

"Then the record is emotionally unprepared for maintenance."

The other technician pointed at the console.

"Fix its feelings."

Aru opened the log.

Nobody found this remarkable.

Ollie liked that.

He had spent years hearing people on Earth ask what robots would do when they became conscious, as though consciousness were an event scheduled for a future conference.

In Vela, one was annoyed at paperwork before seven in the morning.

That seemed more convincing.

"You planning to clean that with philosophy?" a voice asked.

Ollie turned.

Iri stood beside him holding two inspection tags.

They were older than Ollie by maybe a year. Same gray maintenance layer Ollie had seen on local apprentices, sleeves rolled back, dark hair tied out of the way.

"I was observing," Ollie said.

"The dirt?"

"The workflow."

Iri handed him one of the tags.

"The workflow is dirty."

Then they walked away.

Ollie looked down at the cloth again.

Fine.

He started cleaning.

---

The first hour was almost aggressively unimportant.

Ollie cleaned inspection surfaces.

Checked fastener markings.

Matched tool IDs to custody records.

Confirmed that sealed access panels were actually sealed.

Verified that somebody else had verified a pressure sensor mount.

He had imagined orbital maintenance as the place where civilization stopped being abstract and became engineering.

He had not imagined civilization contained this many labels.

At 07:31 he asked Mara why a panel needed both a physical tag and a local system record when either one could identify it.

"Because one can fail without the other," she said.

At 07:38 he asked why two environmental control housings used different connector standards when they performed nearly identical functions.

"Different generation."

"Why not replace the older one?"

"Because it works."

At 07:44 he asked why a service robot had not automated the cleaning pass.

Mara looked up.

"Which robot?"

Ollie hesitated.

Aru, three stations away, turned their head.

Ollie felt his face warm.

"I meant non-conscious automation."

"Then say automation."

"Right."

Mara returned to the display.

"And because cleaning is part of visual inspection. The point is not the cloth."

Ollie looked at the panel in front of him again.

Aru said from across the bay, "For the record, I also decline to clean your station."

"I wasn't asking."

"Good."

Mara's mouth almost moved.

Almost.

Ollie went back to work.

The point was not the cloth.

He slowed down.

The panel surface had looked clean the first time.

Now, wiping along the connector seam, he noticed a faint gray line beside one mounting point.

Not dirt.

Wear.

He leaned closer.

The connector housing sat flush.

The fastener marker aligned.

The digital record showed:

ENVIRONMENTAL DISTRIBUTION NODE E4-17

INSTALL VERIFIED

VIBRATION TEST PASSED

He ran one finger lightly along the edge.

The wear mark stopped halfway around the mount.

That was odd.

Ollie checked the neighboring node.

No line.

Next one.

No line.

He crouched.

From below, the connector looked correct.

"Iri."

No answer.

Ollie looked over.

Iri was on a ladder frame halfway inside an access recess.

"Iri."

"If this is another connector-standard question, no."

"Wear mark."

That got them down.

Iri walked over, looked once and shrugged.

"Install variance."

"Maybe."

"It passed vibration."

"I saw."

Iri touched the housing.

"No movement."

"The mark isn't even."

"Things are often not even."

Ollie looked at the record again.

"Can we see the install image?"

Iri gave him a long look.

"You have been here one hour."

"One hour and nine minutes."

"That's worse."

But they opened the image.

The housing had been installed six days earlier.

Ollie enlarged it.

There was no wear mark.

Iri looked at the current mount.

Then back at the image.

"Still could be harmless."

"I know."

That seemed to matter.

Iri tagged Mara into the record.

Not urgent.

Not failure.

CHECK PHYSICAL SEATING — LOCAL WEAR AFTER INSTALL

Mara appeared three minutes later.

She did not congratulate Ollie.

She did not ask what he thought it meant.

She put a load gauge on the housing and ran the same test the experienced technicians had already run.

Nothing.

Then she changed the load direction.

The connector shifted by less than a millimeter.

A faint gray line appeared under the opposite edge.

Mara stopped the test.

Iri leaned closer.

"That's annoying."

Mara opened the mounting history.

"Very."

Ollie tried not to smile.

Mara saw anyway.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You found a seating problem. You did not save the ring."

"I know."

"Good."

She tagged the node for correction and called over the technician responsible for that section.

The technician examined it, frowned, then checked two related mounts.

One showed the beginning of the same pattern.

Not dangerous.

Not dramatic.

Real.

Ollie's chest tightened with a satisfaction he did not want anyone to notice.

This was better than an evaluation score.

There had been a thing in the world that was wrong.

He had looked carefully enough to see it.

Now somebody would fix it.

That was all.

It was enough.

Mara closed the work record.

"After break, you can help compare occupancy-state diagnostics from yesterday's run."

Ollie looked at her.

"Really?"

"No, I enjoy inventing false assignments."

Aru passed behind them carrying a diagnostic frame.

"She does not. We checked."

Mara ignored them.

"You observe. You do not alter models. You do not publish conclusions. You ask before accessing a new layer."

"Understood."

"And you finish bay four first."

Ollie looked at the remaining inspection surfaces.

There were twelve.

"Right."

Iri handed him the cloth.

"Important work."

Ollie took it.

This time he did not argue.

---

At 10:18 Ollie stopped being bored.

This was not entirely positive.

The occupancy-state diagnostics came from a low-density commissioning run in a finished section of Ring Six.

The data itself was ordinary enough: air handling, pressure stabilization, thermal load, partition states, transit entry counts, local environmental requests.

What made it interesting was the way the systems overlapped.

Every time a residential group crossed from one compatibility zone into another, several systems made tiny corrections.

Airflow shifted.

Temperature changed.

Doors changed priority.

A local environmental buffer adjusted.

None of the corrections mattered alone.

Together, they made the ring feel less like a building and more like a conversation conducted by machines too fast for people to notice.

Ollie sat at a permitted apprentice station while Iri worked beside him on a separate review.

Aru stood across the table examining a structural-damping log.

"Why does zone twelve settle slower?" Ollie asked.

Iri did not look up.

"It doesn't."

"It does here."

"Different occupancy."

"Same head count."

"Head count is not occupancy."

Ollie looked at them.

Iri finally glanced over.

"Bodies are not identical heat sources. Movement isn't identical. Equipment isn't identical."

"I know."

"Then why did you say same?"

Ollie hated this question because he had no good answer.

Aru reached across and rotated the display toward themselves.

"Show me."

Ollie highlighted two runs.

Same section.

Same nominal density.

Different settling time by eleven seconds.

Aru examined the environmental requests.

"Nothing outside tolerance."

"I know."

"Then what are you asking?"

"Why eleven?"

Aru looked at him.

Their face did not move like a human face. Expression came partly through eye brightness, head angle and the timing of stillness.

Ollie was learning that the stillness mattered.

"Because you want it to be a problem?" Aru asked.

"No."

"Because you want it to be interesting?"

Ollie hesitated.

Iri made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

"Maybe," Ollie admitted.

Aru turned the display back.

"Good. Know the difference."

Then they returned to their own work.

Ollie stared at the two runs.

Eleven seconds.

Within tolerance.

No failure.

No one else cared.

He saved the comparison to his permitted notes anyway.

Not as a finding.

As a question.

---

The group's call started at nineteen thirty.

Ollie remembered at nineteen forty-six.

He was still in the service bay.

Not because Mara had kept him.

She had left twenty minutes earlier after handing over commissioning control to the next team.

Ollie had stayed because Iri had shown him a visualization of occupancy transitions and he wanted to understand one thing before going home.

Then one thing became four.

His wrist vibrated against the bench.

ETHAN: alive?

Ollie checked the group thread.

Maya had sent a picture from a social level.

Samir had posted an image of a curved sport court.

Aoife had sent twelve seconds of audio from somewhere crowded and refused to explain it.

Ethan had written:

ETHAN: 19:30 call if free

Not mandatory.

Not even really scheduled.

Ollie had missed it anyway.

He typed:

OLLIE: yes. still at ring

ETHAN: problem?

Ollie looked around.

Iri was across the bay talking with a technician.

Aru had gone.

The displays showed ordinary green states.

OLLIE: no

There was a pause.

ETHAN: good problem or bad no

Ollie smiled.

OLLIE: good no

ETHAN: call when you leave if you want

That was all.

No pressure.

Ollie put the wrist away.

He should have left.

Instead he opened the eleven-second comparison again.

Iri appeared beside him.

"You are still looking at that."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"Excellent technical basis."

Ollie ignored that.

"The correction is too slow for the state change."

"It remains inside tolerance."

"I know."

"You keep saying that."

"Because everybody keeps telling me."

Iri leaned one hip against the table.

"What do you think it is?"

Ollie looked at the trace.

"Nothing yet."

That answer seemed to satisfy them more than a theory would have.

Iri closed the display.

"Then go home."

"You can't close my station."

"I just did."

Ollie reopened it.

Iri closed it again.

Ollie looked at them.

"This is abuse of local knowledge."

"Yes."

They started walking toward the exit.

Ollie followed.

At the door, a status notice appeared on his wrist.

APPRENTICE CONTRIBUTION — FIRST CYCLE COMPLETE

ELIGIBLE:
— EXTENDED WORKSHOP ACCESS
— OPTIONAL DIAGNOSTIC OBSERVATION
— COMMUNITY PROJECT PARTICIPATION
— RECREATION / SPORT / ART ACCESS AS AVAILABLE

No balance.

No payment.

No number telling him what the morning had been worth.

The message asked what he wanted to request next.

Iri glanced at his wrist.

"Don't choose workshop."

Ollie looked at them.

"Why?"

"Because everybody from Earth chooses workshop."

"That's not true."

"You were about to."

Ollie looked back at the list.

They were right.

"What would you choose?"

"I already have workshop access."

"That's not an answer."

Iri started walking again.

"There is a night court reservation open tomorrow."

"I don't play."

"That is why it would be interesting."

Ollie frowned.

"What kind of court?"

Iri looked over their shoulder.

"You really are new."

Ollie stopped.

The workshop request still waited on his wrist.

A specialist fabrication bay on Vela could make things his maker team on Earth had only simulated.

Not because materials were expensive.

Because some tools existed only here, under specific safety responsibility, with people who knew how to use them.

It was exactly the access he would have wanted six months ago.

Exactly the access he wanted now.

He selected:

EXTENDED WORKSHOP ACCESS — REQUEST

Iri saw the confirmation.

"Of course."

"I like building things."

"I didn't say it was bad."

"You made a face."

"I have many faces."

They kept walking.

Ollie followed.

"What is the night court?"

"Too late."

"That's not how invitations work."

"It wasn't an invitation."

Ollie considered arguing.

Then his wrist buzzed again.

Mina, from the Earth maker team.

A picture loaded.

The rescue rover stood on a test platform, one wheel assembly removed. Beside it, three members of the team were grinning at the camera.

MINA: replacement geometry worked

A second message:

MINA: semifinal qualification run next week

Ollie looked at the rover longer than he meant to.

He had not designed the replacement geometry.

They had.

It looked good.

Very good.

He typed:

OLLIE: looks clean. check lateral load on left mount before run

Then stopped.

Deleted the second sentence.

Typed instead:

OLLIE: looks clean. nice work

Mina reacted with a wrench and a heart.

Iri had reached the transit junction and was waiting.

"You coming?"

Ollie put the wrist down.

"Yeah."

They stepped into the transfer lane.

For once, Ollie did not open the diagnostic trace while walking.

He lasted almost two minutes.
