## 1. OPENING — PASS STRONG

Chapter 1 gives short legible spoken units, immediate physical objects, social movement and dialogue. `Ethan lost the presentation slot because Samir looked uncomfortable.` works aloud because it gives person, action and consequence before world explanation.

The emphasis sequence `Not terrified. Not in danger. Just uncomfortable.` is conspicuous but purposeful and immediately grounded. Preserve rather than explain.

## 2. CHAPTERS 20–22 — AUDIO WATCH / MEDIUM

Necessary material, but highest concentration of archival terminology, privacy rules, institutional distinctions, permission scopes and long adult explanations.

Chapter 20's `Ethan had approximately sixty questions...` list risks losing individual distinctions in audio. WATCH only; if human listeners lose the thread, split into two physical beats rather than deleting information.

Chapter 21 archive/funding explanations contain several 45–60 word spoken blocks. If confirmed problematic, use local cadence repair: physical reaction, follow-up question, or one clause moved to another turn. Preserve substantive privacy/provenance logic.

Chapter 22 policy/restriction dialogue is precise but risks an acoustic pattern of policy-statement following policy-statement. Test whether Ethan/Aoife/Maya remain emotionally present while rules are explained.

## 3. SHARED SKY — PASS STRONG

Chapter 28 has short sentence units, physical movement, wonder, sensory change and dialogue without immediate threat swallowing the experience. Protect from compression motivated only by total word count.

## 4. WCF 30–32 — MANUSCRIPT PASS / AUDIO-ADAPTATION WATCH

The action reads clearly but the chapters contain high concentrations of uppercase status blocks. On page these function as interface objects; in audiobook form, identical delivery could become exhausting.

This is not automatically a manuscript problem.

Audio adaptation rule: differentiate interface delivery; use pauses/hierarchy; preserve materially consequential numbers; do not flatten every list item into identical delivery; avoid decorative effects that obscure comprehension.

## 5. CHAPTER 33 — WATCH, PROBABLY KEEP

The long incident reconstruction built around repeated `It says...` creates procedural rhythm. That rhythm is dramatically useful because Ethan hears his action translated into neutral incident language. Keep unless listeners report losing causal order.

## 6. CHAPTER 34 — YOUTH-VOICE WATCH

Ethan's acceptance of limited permissions is earned but unusually controlled for seventeen. Human teen/read-aloud test must answer: does this sound like Ethan after this book, or the author summarizing the book through Ethan? Only the second result opens a local dialogue patch.

## 7. CHAPTER 35 — ENDING AUDIO WATCH

Late concentration of framework/staged-access/program language may briefly move emotional foreground away from Ethan. Test whether listeners still track Aaron getting Ethan's desired door, Ethan's envy, his acceptance of his own future, and the unfinished Ethan/Aoife line. If framework language blunts payoff, repair cadence/placement only, not architecture.

## 8. DIRECT WORD-STUMBLE QUERY

`One one-way door.` is the clearest likely mouth-stumble. Grammatically valid but acoustically resembles duplication.

Severity: POLISH.

Possible future local patch if human listening confirms: `A single one-way door.` or equivalent. Do not patch automatically from heuristic alone.

## 9. HIGH-PRIORITY HUMAN LISTENING CHAPTERS

21 → 22 → 33 → 35 → 34.

Long dialogue itself is not a problem. It becomes a problem when tactic does not change, no physical/social interruption occurs, several nested distinctions must be retained at once, or multiple speakers deliver equally complete formulations in sequence.

## 10. BLIND LISTENER QUESTIONS

Do not prime listeners with diagnoses. Ask afterward:
1. Where did attention drift?
2. Was there a sentence you had to mentally replay?
3. Was speaker identity always clear?
4. Did any teenager sound like an adult policy memo?
5. Did interface information become impossible to retain?
6. What did you emotionally want next?
7. What one fact mattered most?
8. Would you keep listening immediately?
