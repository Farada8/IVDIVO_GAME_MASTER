# PHASE A — PERCEPTION, KNOWLEDGE, BELIEF — PROMPTS 01–08

## 01 — EVIDENCE LADDER PER LINE
For every important assertion in the scene, classify the speaker’s basis as OBSERVED / TOLD / REMEMBERED / INFERRED / SUSPECTED / BELIEVED / KNOWN / WRONG. Flag any wording more certain than its evidence basis.

## 02 — PERCEPTION CONFIDENCE
For every judgment about another person, assign confidence and identify disconfirming evidence the character could plausibly notice. Detect “writer omniscience disguised as empathy.”

## 03 — ACTIVE ATTENTION VS AVAILABLE KNOWLEDGE
Map what each character technically knows but is not attending to because of fear, task load, attraction, fatigue, status pressure or competing goals. Test whether dialogue unrealistically retrieves every relevant fact on demand.

## 04 — NOTICE / INTERPRET / ACT SPLIT
For each important cue, separate what was objectively noticed, what meaning the character assigned, and what action followed. Generate alternative plausible interpretations without changing canon, then verify why the chosen interpretation wins.

## 05 — MEMORY ACCESS UNDER PRESSURE
Audit whether a character recalls facts with implausible speed/precision under emotional or physical load. Determine whether delayed retrieval, partial recall or confidence uncertainty would better fit the established person.

## 06 — FALSE BELIEF PRODUCTIVITY
Identify one or more current false beliefs that should actively shape phrasing, questions and omissions. Reject false beliefs that exist in a bible but have no conversational consequences.

## 07 — INFORMATION ASYMMETRY STRESS TEST
Remove one shared assumption at a time and test whether the exchange still makes sense. Detect lines that silently rely on audience knowledge rather than participant knowledge.

## 08 — MYSTERY-SAFE SPEECH AUDIT
In mystery/thriller scenes, test every inference for clue-order safety. Flag dialogue that accidentally solves, names or frames the answer earlier than the evidence permits.

# PHASE B — LISTENING, REACTION, LATENCY — PROMPTS 09–16

## 09 — CAUSE-TO-LINE TRACE
For each suspect line, output: stimulus -> perceived meaning -> immediate want -> risk -> tactic -> wording -> intended effect. Mark the first missing link.

## 10 — LISTENING GRAPH
Build a directed graph of which word/gesture/silence triggers each major reply. Flag responses that attach to theme, author knowledge or prior outline rather than the live partner.

## 11 — RESPONSE LATENCY PROFILE
For every emotional stimulus classify response as immediate / delayed / displaced / suppressed / action-before-words / private-processing. Test whether the distribution belongs to the character rather than the scene writer’s convenience.

## 12 — MISREAD SIGNATURE
Derive what this character repeatedly misreads in this specific relationship: pity as contempt, silence as judgment, competence as control, protection as possession, etc. Test whether the scene uses that signature without contrivance.

## 13 — REPAIR ATTEMPT DYNAMICS
When one person realizes a misread or overstep, map how they attempt repair: apology, joke, factual correction, physical help, retreat, deflection, gift, silence. Verify repair style is character-specific.

## 14 — FAILED REPAIR
Identify where a believable repair should fail because trust/status/history makes the correct sentence insufficient. Prevent instant emotional reset through perfect wording.

## 15 — ACTION BEFORE LANGUAGE
For emotionally high-pressure beats, test whether the character would move, freeze, leave, touch an object, change task, look away, protect evidence, call someone or perform another action before verbalizing meaning.

## 16 — DELAYED MEANING PAYOFF
Identify a line/gesture that should be understood differently later because the listener lacked context at first hearing. Verify fair-play evidence exists and the later reinterpretation does not require retroactive invention.

# PHASE C — VOICE, REGISTER, RHYTHM — PROMPTS 17–24

## 17 — SPEECH PATTERN MATRIX
Compare principal speakers across sentence frames, correction habits, question types, certainty markers, metaphor domains, humor structures, apology/refusal forms and stress compression. Quantify interchangeability qualitatively.

## 18 — REGISTER SWITCH TRIGGERS
For each character map when they switch between professional, intimate, defensive, public, family, peer and private registers. Test whether the switch is triggered by person/status/stakes rather than author convenience.

## 19 — VOCABULARY PROVENANCE
For unusual vocabulary, identify where the character plausibly acquired it: profession, class, education, hobby, family, region, online culture, institution, obsession. Flag orphan vocabulary with no life source.

## 20 — METAPHOR DOMAIN OWNERSHIP
Map each recurring character’s natural metaphor sources. Detect when multiple speakers suddenly draw metaphors from the same authorial domain.

## 21 — SENTENCE-LENGTH UNDER PRESSURE
Compare sentence complexity in calm vs stressed states. Identify characters who become shorter, faster, more precise, more fragmented or more formal under pressure; verify consistency.

## 22 — QUESTION STYLE IDENTITY
Audit who asks open, closed, leading, defensive, rhetorical, testing, permission-seeking or procedural questions. Make question design part of voice separability.

## 23 — DISAGREEMENT STYLE IDENTITY
Compare how each speaker says no: contradiction, reframing, silence, evidence, sarcasm, withdrawal, status appeal, concession-then-resistance. Flag shared writerly correction syntax.

## 24 — APOLOGY / FORGIVENESS LANGUAGE
Model what each character can and cannot apologize for directly, what they substitute, and what form of apology the other person actually believes. Prevent generic emotionally literate repair dialogue.

# PHASE D — RELATIONSHIP, POWER, GROUP DYNAMICS — PROMPTS 25–32

## 25 — RELATIONSHIP SHORTHAND LEDGER
Build the mutually known facts, private references, known defenses, repeated fights, taboo subjects and gestures that allow the pair to say less. Identify exposition that should disappear because both already know it.

## 26 — RELATIONSHIP DEBT ACCOUNT
Track unresolved favors, betrayals, protection, humiliation, attraction, dependency and sacrifices. For each dialogue turn, identify which debt changes how words are heard.

## 27 — PERMISSION GEOMETRY
Map who can ask, refuse, interrupt, leave, touch, joke, accuse, command, disclose, forgive and define the relationship. Flag dialogue that pretends formal/emotional equality where none exists.

## 28 — STATUS REVERSAL MOMENT
Identify one beat where formal status and emotional status diverge or reverse. Test how language changes before and after the reversal.

## 29 — GROUP ATTENTION GRAPH
For 3+ speakers map gaze/listening/task attention over time. Mark who misses information, who notices side-signals, who performs for the group, and who speaks to one person while ostensibly addressing all.

## 30 — ALLIANCE MICRO-SIGNALS
Identify small choices that reveal alliance without explanation: who answers for whom, who corrects whom, who passes an object, who protects silence, who changes subject. Use behavior before alliance-label dialogue.

## 31 — THIRD-PERSON PRESSURE
In a two-person relationship scene with a third person present/absent, test how reputation, jealousy, duty or loyalty alters what can be said. Prevent isolated “pure relationship” dialogue divorced from social context.

## 32 — EXIT POWER
Who can end the conversation, physically leave or force continuation? Test how exit power shapes pacing, concessions and disclosure.

---

# PHASE E — SCENE STRUCTURE, SUBTEXT, TURN — PROMPTS 33–40

## 33 — DIALOGUE BURDEN CHECK
Temporarily remove the most explanatory speech. If the scene’s causality disappears, identify which structural beat is missing upstream. Repair structure before dialogue.

## 34 — BEAT DEPENDENCY TEST
Number major dialogue/action beats. For each N+1 state exactly how N changed available choices or interpretation. Flag reorderable activity.

## 35 — ENTER-LATE TEST
Find the earliest line/action that can be cut while preserving orientation and required structure. Continue until further cutting damages comprehension or pressure. Compare energy before/after.

## 36 — EXIT-AFTER-TURN TEST
Identify the scene turn, then test whether post-turn dialogue adds consequence/action or merely explains what just happened. Cut/relocate explanatory tail candidates.

## 37 — SUBTEXT CAUSE AUDIT
For every important unsaid truth specify why it cannot be said directly: fear, shame, strategy, status, uncertainty, loyalty, self-deception. Reject subtext with no cause.

## 38 — HIDDEN DESIRE / INDIRECT PLAN
For each major participant define hidden desire and indirect plan. Test whether their lines actually execute the plan rather than merely hinting at emotion.

## 39 — VALUE SHIFT THROUGH BEHAVIOR
State opening and closing relationship/value charge. Prove the shift through choices, reactions and consequences before citing thematic dialogue.

## 40 — SILENCE COMPETITION
At three important beats, compare current line against a silence/action alternative. Keep the line only if it does more necessary dramatic work than the silent option.

---

# PHASE F — AUTHORIAL LEAKAGE, EXPOSITION, COMPETENCE — PROMPTS 41–48

## 41 — HOUSE-STYLE LEAKAGE MAP
Separate narrator patterns from dialogue patterns. Identify rhetorical forms used by unrelated speakers beyond plausible cultural/relationship sharing.

## 42 — CLEVERNESS CLUSTER STRESS TEST
Find clusters of 3+ polished reversals, aphorisms or semantic corrections. For each line state character ownership, cost/reveal, and whether another principal could say it unchanged.

## 43 — HUMOR OWNERSHIP
Define each character’s humor mechanism and forbidden/unnatural humor zone. Audit every joke for owner, target, motive and social risk.

## 44 — INSTITUTIONAL REGISTER QUARANTINE
List all procedural/legal/scientific terms in the scene. Assign natural owners, paraphrasers, avoiders, misusers and status-users. Rewrite only where formal language leaks into unsupported speakers.

## 45 — EXPOSITION AS AMMUNITION
For every necessary fact, transform the diagnostic question from “how to explain?” to “who can use this fact against/for whom, and why now?” Identify facts better delivered through action, document, consequence or narration.

## 46 — COMPETENCE / PERSONHOOD SPLIT
For each competence-heavy character scene, identify the task solution and the private human cost running simultaneously. Flag scenes where all emotional identity disappears into efficient problem-solving.

## 47 — THEME-NAMING DETECTOR
Mark lines that state the book’s theme, lesson or moral too cleanly. Test whether the same meaning can emerge from choice/consequence without explicit naming.

## 48 — TRAILER-LINE DETECTOR
Identify lines that sound designed for quotes, teasers or episode endings rather than spontaneous scene behavior. Keep only if the character/tactic genuinely earns the compression.

# PHASE G — AUDIO, PERFORMANCE, TRANSLATION — PROMPTS 49–56

## 49 — TEXT-ONLY BLIND BELIEF TEST
Present a scene without performance notes. Ask readers to identify speaker, want, risk, subtext and any line that feels written rather than spoken. Preserve disagreements as human-signal variance.

## 50 — PERFORMANCE-ONLY BLIND BELIEF TEST
Play properly directed audio without showing text. Capture belief breaks, speaker confusion, boredom, writer-talk feeling and emotional truth. Do not reveal prior text diagnosis first.

## 51 — SOURCE / LOCALIZATION / PERFORMANCE TRIAGE
For each flagged moment compare original source wording, translated/localized wording and performed delivery. Assign earliest failed layer with evidence.

## 52 — RHYTHM WITHOUT REWRITE
Before changing prose, test whether pause, overlap, interruption, breath, speed, underplay, proximity or listener reaction solves the credibility problem while preserving exact text.

## 53 — JOKE LOCALIZATION TEST
For humor/wordplay, test semantic meaning, social function and timing separately across languages. If literal translation preserves words but destroys relationship function, classify LOCALIZATION_DEFECT rather than SOURCE_DEFECT.

## 54 — REGISTER LOCALIZATION TEST
Check whether formal/informal address, age, class, profession and intimacy survive translation. Flag localized speech that collapses distinct English registers into one Russian register or vice versa.

## 55 — AUDIO OVERACTING RISK
Identify lines whose credibility depends on underplay and prior relationship. Test whether actor emphasis turns subtext into announcement or wit into performed cleverness.

## 56 — PERFORMANCE CANARY SELECTION
Select the smallest 10–15 minute sample that maximally stresses voice separability, intimacy, institutional speech, humor, group attention and high-stakes response before spending on long-form render.

# PHASE H — VALIDATION, REPAIR, SCALE — PROMPTS 57–64

## 57 — EARLIEST FAILED LAYER
For each defect choose among STRUCTURE / HUMAN STATE / KNOWLEDGE-PERCEPTION / RELATIONSHIP-POWER / TACTIC / WORDING / LOCALIZATION / PERFORMANCE. Prohibit downstream fixes for upstream failures.

## 58 — MINIMAL PATCH CONTRACT
Generate A=current / B=minimal repair only for proven wording defects. State exactly which causal failure B repairs and which story/voice facts must remain unchanged.

## 59 — NEGATIVE CONTROL
For every accepted rewrite, create one intentionally over-naturalized banal version and one over-written clever version. Verify the accepted B sits between both failure modes.

## 60 — SPEAKER-SWAP TEST
Swap names on principal lines. Measure which lines remain plausible. Any high interchangeability triggers voice-pattern review, not automatic rewrite.

## 61 — CHAPTER-LEVEL PATTERN REGRESSION
After local repair, scan the chapter for repeated frames, humor mechanisms, apology structures, correction syntax and narrator leakage. Confirm the patch did not merely move the house-style problem.

## 62 — BOOK-LEVEL VOICE DRIFT
Sample each recurring character at beginning/middle/end. Determine which changes are earned arc evolution versus uncontrolled model/style drift.

## 63 — HUMAN SIGNAL DISAGREEMENT
When readers/listeners disagree, classify whether disagreement tracks taste, context, language, performance or a recurring credibility break. Do not convert one opinion into FATAL or average away repeated independent discomfort.

## 64 — PROMOTION GATE
After pilots, decide which mechanisms become mandatory engine law, which remain project overlays, which need more human signal, and which are rejected. Require evidence, application target, regression result and rollback path for every promoted mechanism.
