# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 2 — DUBLIN VERTICAL

Aoife’s first useful recording in Dublin was a man swearing about a twelve-minute orbital delay.

Not the skyline.

Not the transfer architecture.

Not the pale structure rising beyond the old roofs where the morning cloud broke around it.

The man.

He stood three people ahead of her at a city platform with a work bag between his boots and one hand pressed to his ear.

“No, I know what it says,” he told whoever was on the call. “What I’m saying is, if they hold the eight-ten again, I miss the spine transfer and then I’m twenty-five minutes late before I’ve even left the planet.”

He listened.

“No. Because freight has priority until nine.”

Another pause.

“Because freight always has priority until nine.”

Aoife angled her recorder down so it caught the voice without looking as if she had aimed it at him.

The city around it did the rest.

Rain ticking against the shelter.

A bus braking in the street.

A bicycle bell.

Two tourists arguing softly over whether they were on the right platform.

A maintenance alarm somewhere behind the wall, followed by the flat announcement that one vertical freight channel was operating at reduced capacity.

Nobody looked up.

That was better than the skyline.

Aoife marked the clip.

DUBLIN 08:14 — COMMUTER / FREIGHT DELAY / ORDINARY

Then she deleted ORDINARY.

If she had to write ordinary under it, she had probably already failed.

Maya leaned over her shoulder.

“Did you just record a guy being late?”

“Yes.”

“We came all the way here and your first Dublin clip is a man complaining about transport.”

“Exactly.”

Maya looked past her through the wet glass.

The transfer structure was still visible above the buildings, too straight to belong to weather and too large to understand from one angle. It did not look like a tower. Not really. Parts of it disappeared into the city. Other parts rose through the cloud. Freight lights moved along one edge in a pattern Aoife could not translate.

Maya pointed.

“That is objectively more interesting.”

“To you.”

“To everyone.”

The commuter in front of them said, “Finally,” as their platform indicator changed.

Aoife lifted the recorder.

Maya sighed.

“You’re impossible.”

“Professional commitment.”

The phrase came out before Aoife could stop it.

For half a second she was back at a table in Virginia, Ethan glaring at her over evidence cards while everyone else pretended not to enjoy it.

Maya heard the echo too.

She smiled.

“Recycling material already.”

“Efficient production.”

“That sounded like Ethan.”

Aoife lowered the recorder.

“Take that back.”

Their transport arrived.

It was smaller than Aoife expected and dirtier than anything in the arrival materials.

That helped.

One side carried an orbital transfer symbol. The other had a scrape through an advertisement for a theater festival.

Inside, a woman in a blue freight jacket slept with her forehead against the window. Two school-age kids argued over a seat. A synthetic-bodied passenger stood near the rear door in a narrow maintenance configuration, one hand fixed to a ceiling grip, scrolling through messages with the bored precision of someone who did not care whether anybody else found their body interesting.

Maya noticed.

Aoife noticed Maya noticing.

Neither said anything.

Good.

The doors closed.

Dublin moved past in wet pieces.

Brick.

Glass.

Old stone.

Delivery trucks.

A church wall half-hidden by new service conduits.

People carrying coffee.

A public square with a transfer-status board mounted beside something much older that Aoife could not identify before they were past it.

The extraordinary part kept refusing to stay separate from the rest.

She recorded the bus motor.

A child complaining that somebody had taken his charger.

The freight worker snoring.

A city announcement switching from Irish to English and then into an orbital-route identifier with no change in tone.

Aoife had planned an opening narration on the flight.

She had deleted it before landing.

Every version sounded like a person arriving somewhere and announcing what the place meant.

Dublin clearly already had enough people for that.

Her recorder display showed nineteen minutes of audio.

Most of it would be useless.

That was fine.

She was beginning to suspect useful was not the same as true.

The thought sounded suspiciously like something she would later regret saying aloud, so she did not.

---

Ethan found her at the second vertical staging queue.

He had acquired coffee.

This was not surprising.

The fact that he had acquired two was.

He held one out.

Aoife looked at it.

“Is this an apology for something you haven’t done yet?”

“It’s coffee.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“I had enough time to get two.”

“Also not an answer.”

“Do you want it?”

She took it.

“Yes.”

The queue moved three steps.

Their staging bands had changed twice since the city platform.

Aoife’s now read:

VERTICAL TRANSFER — PASSENGER  
PREP BAND D  
RECORDING RESTRICTED AFTER GATE V-3

Ethan’s read:

PREP BAND B  
MIXED ADAPTATION  
TRANSFER GROUP 4

For the moment, they were standing beside each other.

That had become less automatic in the last twenty-four hours.

“What are you using?” he asked.

“For what?”

“The opening.”

Aoife drank the coffee.

It was too hot and not very good.

Also useful.

“I don’t know yet.”

“The commuter complaint is good.”

She looked at him.

He shrugged. “I heard it.”

“You were behind us?”

“For half of it.”

“And?”

“You could start with the complaint, stay on city sound, then cut the first visual reveal until after someone says freight always wins.”

Aoife hated that immediately.

Which was inconvenient because it was good.

Ethan saw her face.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That was the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where I’ve said something correct at the wrong time.”

“That implies a level of rarity I don’t want to validate.”

The queue moved again.

Ahead, the staging corridor narrowed into a bank of white gates set into older dark stone. The contrast looked designed until Aoife noticed three patched cable covers along the floor and a maintenance worker on one knee with an open panel.

Good.

The city was helping.

“You can use it,” Ethan said.

Aoife turned back.

“Use what?”

“The structure.”

“I know I can.”

“I wasn’t saying you needed permission.”

“It sounded structurally similar.”

He stared at her.

Then laughed.

“That’s Ollie’s line.”

“It has applications.”

“Okay. Forget I said anything.”

“That’s not the problem.”

“What is the problem?”

Aoife looked ahead.

The queue had split into four.

Freight workers went left.

Two families moved through a medical-prep lane.

A group of orbital commuters barely looked at the signage.

The five of them had crossed an ocean together and had spent less time together since landing than they usually did getting lunch.

“The problem,” she said, “is that I was trying to figure out what I think the opening is.”

“And I gave you an idea.”

“Yes.”

“A good idea.”

“Probably.”

“So?”

“So now if I use it, I have to decide whether I thought of it because it works or because you put it in my head.”

Ethan frowned.

“That happens every time someone talks to someone else.”

“I know.”

“Then what am I supposed to do?”

“Nothing.”

“That is not actionable.”

Aoife smiled into the coffee.

“There you are.”

Ethan exhaled.

“I was helping.”

“I know.”

“That somehow made it worse.”

“A little.”

He considered that.

Then his band buzzed.

TRANSFER GROUP 4 — REPORT GATE V-2

Aoife looked at her own.

Still V-3.

“How long?” she asked.

“Seven minutes.”

“Until you move?”

“Until prep.”

“And after?”

He checked.

His expression changed by less than most people would notice.

Aoife noticed.

“Different route?”

“Different pressure prep.”

“So we don’t meet after the gate.”

“We meet at upper staging.”

“When?”

He opened the shared schedule.

Of course he did.

“Twenty-eight minutes after—”

Her band changed.

PREP BAND D — REPORT GATE V-3  
UPPER STAGING ROUTE ASSIGNED AFTER TRANSFER  
GROUP MERGE NOT GUARANTEED

Aoife held it out.

Ethan read it.

“That’s new.”

“Yes.”

“It still might merge.”

“Might.”

“I can check the route logic.”

“No.”

“I’m not changing it. I’m checking.”

“No.”

He looked at her.

She took another drink of coffee.

“This is exactly what I mean.”

“What?”

“You cannot let a separation exist for ten seconds without trying to reorganize it.”

“I’m looking at the route.”

“You’re trying to make the route agree with you.”

“I don’t need the route to agree with me.”

“Ethan.”

He stopped.

That was the thing about him.

Sometimes the correction took five minutes, three people and a damaged system.

Sometimes it took his name.

His gate flashed.

FINAL CALL — GROUP 4

He looked at it.

Then at her.

“I really was just trying to help.”

“I know.”

Aoife wished he had said something easier to dismiss.

He lifted one hand, coffee still in the other.

“Upper staging.”

“Maybe.”

“That is unnecessarily hostile.”

“Context-dependent.”

He laughed once, shook his head, and went toward V-2.

Aoife watched him disappear through the gate.

She wanted to call after him.

Not because there was anything to say.

Because they had not finished arguing.

That was worse.

She turned the recorder on.

Then off again.

No.

Not that.

---

Gate V-3 took the recorder away from her.

Not permanently.

That would have been simpler.

A staff member scanned it, sealed it into a soft gray pouch, and clipped the pouch to a transport rack with the rest of the restricted electronics.

“You get it back after upper transfer,” she said.

“How long?”

“Depends on traffic.”

That answer had become Dublin’s unofficial motto.

Aoife surrendered the recorder.

Her phone went into the same pouch.

The small camera she had not used all morning followed.

Then the gate opened.

The first part of the Vertical was a corridor.

That was almost disappointing.

No transparent floor.

No dramatic countdown.

No view of Earth falling away.

Just a long white passage descending slightly under the city while passengers moved in groups toward prep rooms.

The walls carried route numbers, body-prep warnings and freight priority marks.

A cleaner pushed a machine along the opposite side.

Someone had left a paper cup on a bench.

Aoife smiled.

The future had litter.

Then the corridor changed.

The floor became segmented underfoot.

The air cooled.

A low vibration entered through her shoes, too steady to be machinery she could locate.

Staff checked bands again.

Seats.

Harnesses.

A restraint fit that took longer than expected because the person beside Aoife had a shoulder configuration the standard guide had not recognized correctly.

Nobody treated this as extraordinary.

A technician swapped one support panel, reran the fit, and moved on.

Aoife had no recorder.

At first she kept trying to remember sounds.

The click of the harness.

The soft double tone before pressure adjustment.

The way voices changed when everyone became quieter at once.

She caught herself building the edit in her head.

Opening on the commuter.

Freight delay.

City noise.

Then—

No.

She forced herself to stop.

The strongest part began after the lights dimmed.

Not because anything spectacular happened.

Because the room lost its relationship to the city.

The vibration deepened.

Pressure changed around her ears.

Her body became aware of motion before her eyes had anything to contribute.

There was no window.

Nothing to prove they were rising.

For several seconds Aoife could have been underground.

Then gravity shifted.

Not gone.

Changed.

Her stomach moved half a beat behind the rest of her.

Someone two seats away laughed once in surprise and then stopped.

The harness took more of her weight.

The floor felt less certain.

A staff member said something calm about transition phase and breathing normally.

Aoife did not need the instruction, but she obeyed it anyway.

Another change.

This one stranger.

Her body had spent seventeen years assuming down was not negotiable.

Now down had become a service being provided.

That thought arrived cleanly enough that she distrusted it immediately.

Too neat.

Too narratable.

She let it go.

The room tilted without tilting.

Or she did.

There was a stretch of maybe ten seconds—maybe thirty; she had no device to check—when every sound seemed to separate from its source.

Harness.

Ventilation.

Someone breathing.

A faint mechanical impact far away.

Her own sleeve brushing the restraint.

Then the movement settled into a new condition.

Not normal.

Stable.

Aoife realized she had both hands clenched.

She opened them.

Across the aisle, an older woman in work clothes had already fallen asleep.

Aoife nearly laughed.

That was the shot.

Of course it was.

The impossible transition.

The body learning a new down.

And somebody commuting through it with her eyes closed.

Aoife had no camera.

No recorder.

Nothing.

For one stupid second she felt cheated.

Then she felt something else.

Relief.

The moment belonged to nobody.

Not yet.

Maybe not ever.

She sat back.

The technician walked the aisle checking restraints.

The older woman slept.

The city was gone.

Not disappeared.

Gone in the specific way a place vanished when your body could no longer use it to answer where down was.

A small display above the aisle changed.

EARTH SURFACE REFERENCE — TRANSITION COMPLETE

Aoife read it twice.

Around her, people unfastened nothing and waited for the next instruction.

She imagined telling Ethan about the line.

He would probably say it was a good ending for the first sequence.

She would probably tell him to stop helping.

The thought made her smile.

When her recorder came back, she knew exactly what she was not going to do.

She would not fake the lost sound.

She would not rebuild the transition with effects.

She would not ask somebody else to describe it so she could pretend she had captured it.

The strongest thing that had happened to her that morning would remain absent.

That absence could stay.

For once, not recording something did not feel like losing it.
