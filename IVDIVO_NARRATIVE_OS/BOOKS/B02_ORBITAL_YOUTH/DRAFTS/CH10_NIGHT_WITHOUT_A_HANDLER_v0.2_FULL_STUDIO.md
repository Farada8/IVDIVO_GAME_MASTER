# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 10 — NIGHT WITHOUT A HANDLER

Maya won the argument at 18:07 and immediately tried to improve the victory.

“No check-in.”

Her host kept working at the kitchen counter. One pan hissed beside a covered container that pulsed softly with heat. A strip along the wall announced that a food surface had finished sterilizing and could now be touched by humans, which Maya considered unnecessarily specific.

“We already removed continuous location,” the host said.

“That is not the same thing.”

“No. It is more freedom than you arrived with.”

Maya had one boot on. The other sat beside the table, where she had abandoned it six minutes earlier in order to negotiate properly.

Her host finally looked over.

“Zones Three and Four. Lea is the local contact. You either make the late-cycle return or use the agreed sleep backup at her household. If the route or environmental conditions change enough to affect either plan, you message.”

Maya pointed at the agreement floating above the counter.

“And no location stream.”

“Correct.”

“No escort.”

“Correct.”

“Then no scheduled check-in.”

The host set down the utensil they were holding.

“I asked for one message at twenty-two thirty.”

“If nothing changed, what would I be reporting?”

“That you are still inside the plan.”

“That is what the plan means.”

“That is also what seventeen means.”

Maya leaned on the counter. “Not a technical reason.”

“It is the reason I’m awake if you miss the last transfer.”

The younger member of the household crossed the room carrying a plate, heard that, and reversed direction without slowing.

“Coward,” Maya called.

“Survivor,” came the reply from the corridor.

Her host waited until the corridor was empty again.

“Condition-based message,” they said. “The route changes. The permitted zone changes. Lea leaves. You miss the return and activate the sleep backup. Any of those happen, tell me. Otherwise I expect to hear nothing.”

Maya read the agreement again.

No location stream. No escort. No scheduled check-in. Two permitted late-event zones. Lea as local contact. One return condition. One approved sleep backup. Message only if something relevant changed.

She scanned the terms a third time and found no new demand hiding between the lines.

“Fine.”

Her host closed the agreement before she could improve it again.

“Find your other boot.”

Maya looked down.

It was still beside the table.

She pulled it on.

Her family thread buzzed before she reached the door. Someone had reacted to the photo she had sent ten minutes earlier while trying to choose between two shirts.

FAMILY: you chose the black one

Then:

FAMILY: good

Then, before Maya could enjoy the miracle of uncomplicated approval:

FAMILY: who is the person behind you in the mirror

Maya locked the thread.

Her host glanced at her face.

“Earth?”

“Worse. Family.”

“That remains true in orbit.”

Maya left before the next message arrived.

* * *

Lea was waiting three transfers away and pretending she had not been waiting.

“You’re late.”

“Six minutes.”

“Eight.”

“The transfer held.”

“You took the long route.”

“The short route has the stupid handrail.”

Lea stared at her. “You chose an extra transfer because of a handrail.”

“It is on the wrong side.”

“For you.”

“For civilization.”

Lea started walking.

Maya caught up.

They had met properly during the Ring Six resident tour, although properly was generous. Lea had corrected two things Maya said, disagreed with Ethan without bothering to learn his name, and disappeared halfway through because her household had another allocation appointment.

Three days later Maya had found her in a youth event thread and sent a message.

Lea’s entire answer had been:

maybe

Apparently that counted as an invitation.

Tonight Lea wore a loose jacket clipped short at one side for the low-gravity transition ahead. Maya had copied the arrangement badly. Her own jacket kept catching under her arm.

Lea watched her fight it once, twice, then a third time.

“You can move the clip.”

“I know.”

Maya kept walking for another five steps, lost the argument with the fabric and fixed it.

Lea did not comment.

“That silence is hostile.”

“I’m conserving energy.”

The route dipped toward a lower effective-gravity band.

Maya no longer hated the first lightness in her knees. She still noticed it before every transition, a tiny warning from a body that had spent seventeen years assuming down would remain loyal.

People ahead changed pace before the floor geometry made the reason obvious. A local boy shifted to the inside rail without interrupting his conversation. Two children launched themselves down three steps, landed cleanly and kept running.

Maya copied the timing half a beat late.

Lea caught the back of her jacket before Maya completed a graceful introduction to the wall.

“I corrected.”

“You were going to correct with your face.”

Maya got both feet under her. “Still a correction.”

Lea laughed and let go.

That helped more than being caught.

Maya had spent the first days in Vela trying not to look new. It turned out being obviously new around people who found it funny was easier than pretending to be local around people who could tell anyway.

The late event occupied three public volumes that had not been designed to become one room and had therefore become one room very well.

A food strip ran along the highest-gravity side. Music came from somewhere below and reached Maya before she could see the performers through an opening between decks. A flexible mesh crossing had become seating until somebody needed to cross it, at which point everyone sitting there moved with the irritated efficiency of people who had done this all year.

No Youth Cohort marker lit when Maya entered.

Nobody asked what Earth thought of Vela.

Nobody asked what First Contact had felt like.

For almost ten minutes, nobody cared who she was.

It was excellent.

Then Lea reached a crowded table and said, “This is Maya. Planet.”

Maya stopped beside her.

“That is not my name.”

A boy leaning against the wall looked up. “It can be two things.”

Maya pointed at him. “Your name is jacket.”

He looked down at his shirt. “I’m not wearing one.”

“Exactly.”

Lea sighed. “Davi, do not encourage her.”

Davi smiled anyway.

Maya noticed.

Then noticed Lea noticing that she had noticed.

Maya took the nearest food token from the shared order so she would have something else to do.

Lea reached for her wrist.

Too late.

Maya bit into something hot enough to remove Davi from the list of immediate concerns.

She stopped chewing.

The table watched.

Davi lifted his eyebrows.

Maya swallowed because dying was preferable to performing weakness for an audience she had known for forty seconds.

Her eyes watered.

Lea pushed a drink toward her.

“You can stop competing with dinner.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You are crying.”

“Unrelated.”

Davi laughed.

Maya drank. The drink made everything worse for three seconds and then helped.

She put the food token back.

A girl at the far end raised a hand. “That was mine.”

Maya looked at her. “I respect you less now.”

“You stole my food.”

“That sounds less educational than what happened.”

The conversation moved on without waiting for her.

For the next hour Maya got exactly the kind of night she had wanted and had been unable to describe to adults without making it sound like a programme objective.

Someone complained about an assessment. Someone else had been dropped from a rehearsal after missing two bookings. Arel, who seemed to know everybody and own nothing large enough to justify it, was trying to trade a fabrication slot for help moving temporary furniture.

Lea argued with a friend about whether temporary housing counted as near the event if the final direct transfer left before midnight.

Davi claimed a lower-band route would save eleven minutes.

Lea said it would save four and cost dignity.

Maya took Davi’s side because he was smiling at her.

The route saved three minutes.

It also required a crowded transition where Maya misjudged her momentum, caught a handhold with both arms and swung halfway around it while two locals stepped around her.

Davi said, “That was not on the route map.”

Maya dropped back to the floor. “Improvement.”

“You added a circle.”

“Free upgrade.”

Lea passed them without touching the handhold.

“Planet.”

Maya shoved her lightly.

Lea shoved back harder.

A route chime changed overhead.

Maya checked it first.

“Your direct return just moved six minutes earlier.”

Lea stopped and pulled up the service board. Davi checked too.

Maya folded her arms while they read.

Lea looked up. “Do not enjoy this.”

“Too late.”

Davi pointed at the board. “Planet education works.”

Maya’s opinion of him briefly declined.

They reached the second event volume after twenty-one hundred. The music was louder there and worse according to Lea, which meant everyone stayed.

Maya’s family thread buzzed twice more.

She ignored it.

Then Aoife sent a private message.

AOIFE: alive?

MAYA: locals have failed to kill me

AOIFE: disappointing

Maya sent a photo from the table. Lea was making a face at somebody outside the frame. Davi had leaned into the edge at the exact moment Maya took it.

Aoife replied almost immediately.

AOIFE: the boy is looking at you

Maya zoomed in.

He was.

MAYA: he is looking at the camera

AOIFE: sure

Maya put the device away before Aoife could become more useful.

Lea leaned over. “Was that one of your Earth people?”

“Aoife.”

“The one with the recorder?”

“Yes.”

Lea nodded toward Davi. “Did she diagnose him?”

Maya nearly choked on a much safer piece of food.

“No.”

“Good. We charge for local consultation.”

Davi returned carrying drinks and handed one to Maya without asking.

Lea saw that and smiled into her own cup.

Maya decided not to ask what the smile meant.

That decision lasted almost a full minute.

* * *

At 23:18, the plan changed.

Not dramatically enough to make anyone stop having fun.

A late service adjustment moved one of the convenient return transfers. Lea could still reach her temporary housing. Maya could still make the return condition she had negotiated, but only if she left in twelve minutes.

Nobody wanted to leave in twelve minutes.

Arel lived two public volumes away and had enough common-room floor for five people if nobody objected to sleeping badly.

Three people objected.

All three agreed to go.

Davi said he was coming only until the next transfer.

Nobody believed him.

Lea checked the route. “Arel’s is still Zone Four.”

Maya checked too.

Same permitted zone. No environmental restriction. No additional transfer risk. Eight minutes from the event. Four from an all-night food line.

Her approved backup sleep location was Lea’s household.

Arel’s was not.

Lea saw her stop scrolling.

“You need to message.”

“The zone didn’t change.”

“Your sleeping place did.”

Maya opened the agreement.

APPROVED SLEEP BACKUP — LOCAL CONTACT HOUSEHOLD

Lea tapped the line with one finger. “That means mine.”

“I can read.”

“Then stop trying to make the words tired enough to give up.”

Maya looked toward the corridor. Davi was holding the door open with one foot while arguing with Arel about food.

“If I message now, they might tell me to go back.”

“They might say okay.”

“Or they might not.”

“That is generally why people ask.”

Maya closed the agreement and reopened the transit board instead.

The return countdown dropped to eleven minutes fifty-nine.

Maya swiped back to the agreement. The condition-based message line sat exactly where she had negotiated it.

She locked the screen.

Davi called from the corridor. “Are you two coming?”

“Go,” Lea said. “We’ll catch up.”

Maya’s device buzzed before Davi could argue.

HOST: route notice came through. return condition still valid?

The message was almost offensively reasonable.

Maya typed:

Still with Lea. Staying inside approved Zone Four. No route or environment change.

Her thumb hovered over send.

Every sentence was true.

She was with Lea.

Arel’s was in Zone Four.

The service adjustment did not create a hazard.

What she had not written was where she intended to sleep.

She sent it.

Lea read the message from beside her.

“That sounds like you’re coming back with me.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You knew what they were asking.”

“They asked about the return condition.”

“And you answered everything except the part that changed.”

Maya locked the screen. “If they need an address, they can ask for one.”

Lea’s mouth tightened.

“If they ask me, I’m telling them.”

“Fine.”

“And I’m not doing this all night.”

“I’m not asking you to.”

“You put my name in the message.”

“Because I’m with you.”

“Exactly. Do not make my name do the work.”

Maya opened her mouth, then stopped.

The host reply appeared.

HOST: understood. message if condition changes. enjoy.

There was no demand for a location, no argument and no order to return.

Maya’s shoulders dropped before she could stop them.

Lea saw.

“Once,” she said.

Maya frowned.

“I’m letting that stand once. If they ask where you slept, you answer. I am not building a whole story around you.”

“There is no story.”

“Good.”

Lea started after the others.

Maya followed, muted the host thread and slipped the device into her pocket.

At Arel’s there were already six people in a common room built for maybe four.

Someone had stolen the only wall cushion. Davi was losing an argument about music volume to a twelve-year-old who did not appear to live there. Arel handed Maya a folded thermal sheet and pointed at two narrow strips of floor.

“Near the vent or near the door.”

“Which is better?”

“Neither. That is why they’re available.”

Maya chose the vent.

Lea dropped her bag near the door.

“You took the worse one,” Maya said.

“Bathroom is closer.”

Maya considered the route and nodded. “Advanced orbital planning.”

“Years of training.”

Davi threw a soft packet at them because they were talking over the music.

Maya threw it back.

Someone dimmed the lights.

Her family thread buzzed again. The preview showed:

FAMILY: okay but seriously who—

Maya muted that thread too.

The twelve-year-old reclaimed the wall cushion from behind her. Maya grabbed for it, missed as the room’s slight gravity shift changed her reach, and landed sideways on the thermal sheet.

Lea laughed so hard she had to sit down.

Maya threw the sheet at her.

The host thread stayed quiet in Maya’s pocket while the argument about the cushion became much more important.
