# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 3 — ARRIVAL WITHOUT A FLOOR

Maya knew exactly what the floor was doing.

This did not help her feet.

The arrival lane curved gently ahead, its surface apparently level, while the rest of her body insisted that something had shifted sideways and down at the same time.

She had trained for rotational transitions.

She had watched diagrams.

She had done the adaptation room in Virginia until she could explain Coriolis effects to someone who had not asked.

None of that stopped her from planting her left foot half a second too early.

A woman behind her swore.

Maya moved right.

Wrong choice.

Two commuters adjusted around her without breaking stride.

Someone caught her elbow.

“Not there.”

The voice belonged to a local teenager with a black work bag strapped close to one hip.

Maya pulled her arm free on instinct.

“I’m fine.”

“You’re blocking two lanes.”

Maya looked down.

The floor markings made sense now that she was not standing on top of them.

A narrow pale strip curved toward the outer ring.

A darker path cut across it toward a radial transfer.

She was occupying the exact place where both flows expected people to keep moving.

“Okay.”

The teenager pointed at the wall.

“Take the grip before the turn.”

“I don’t need—”

The floor changed under her.

Not dramatically.

Just enough.

Maya’s hand found the grip.

The teenager waited one beat.

Maya hated that beat.

Then the teenager nodded once and kept walking.

No smile.

No welcome to Vela.

No explanation of orbital life.

Just traffic correction.

Maya released the grip too quickly and her shoulder drifted farther than she expected.

She caught herself before anyone else had to.

Good.

She had arrived.

Excellent.

A synthetic-bodied commuter passed on the opposite side in a narrow maintenance configuration, long forearms folded close to avoid the crowd. One outer panel carried scratches and a yellow service mark. The commuter stepped onto a low-g transfer strip without changing pace, used a ceiling rail for two pulls, and vanished through a service gate.

Nobody looked.

Maya did.

Then stopped looking because that was worse.

Her arrival band buzzed.

HOST TRANSFER — ROUTE 18  
ESTIMATED: 27 MIN  
CURRENT: 36 MIN  
CAUSE: PHASE / QUEUE LOAD

Thirty-six minutes.

The route map showed the host zone less than a third of the city away.

Maya zoomed out.

Still close.

She looked at the flow beside her, then at the map again.

There had to be a better path.

---

The first rule of Vela transit, according to Maya, was that the transit system had not been consulted during the design of Vela.

This was unfair.

The system had probably been consulted extensively.

That only made it worse.

She stood at a junction where three routes met and watched her estimated arrival time increase by four minutes while doing nothing.

The official route wanted her to continue inward, wait for a synchronized radial lift, cross a low-g section of the spine, then transfer outward into a different rotating band.

The map showed a shorter path around the ring.

Much shorter.

It even looked like a normal path.

Maya selected it.

ROUTE AVAILABLE  
ESTIMATED: 44 MIN

“No.”

She selected it again as if the system might be embarrassed.

Forty-four.

Her recommended route remained thirty-two.

A local commuter beside her glanced at the display.

“Don’t take ringline south.”

Maya looked up.

“Why?”

“Because you’ll miss the radial window.”

“The line is shorter.”

“Yes.”

“That usually helps.”

“On planets.”

The commuter walked away.

Maya stared after him.

That was unnecessarily satisfying on his part.

She chose ringline south anyway.

Not because he had annoyed her.

That would be childish.

She chose it because the map was clearly wrong in a way she intended to understand.

The ringline car arrived almost immediately.

Point for Maya.

She boarded.

The car curved along the inhabited structure with enough gravity that standing felt ordinary again. The windows faced inward through layers of city rather than out toward space. She caught glimpses of service corridors, stacked public spaces, a green volume full of trees under artificial light, three levels of housing, a food market, something that looked like a basketball court until the playing surface curved up one wall.

The city was not one wheel.

It was pieces.

Rings and connectors and volumes braided around the spine, some clean and new, some patched enough to look older than First Contact.

People were going to work.

A child slept across two seats.

Two teenagers in school clothes argued about whether somebody had cheated in a game.

An older man carried a box that smelled aggressively of fried onions.

Maya had expected arrival to feel like entering the future.

Instead it felt like she had boarded public transport in a city whose geography had decided to become personal.

Her band buzzed.

TRANSFER WINDOW: MISSED  
REVISED ETA: 51 MIN

Maya closed her eyes.

The commuter had been right.

This did not prove anything about the system.

It proved one person had known one thing.

She got off at the next stop.

The floor pitched subtly as the platform geometry changed.

This time she took the grip before the turn.

Nobody applauded.

Good.

A group of local teenagers moved past, talking too fast for Maya to follow because they kept switching between route names and people she did not know.

One of them pointed toward a service shortcut.

“Faster.”

Another said, “Not today.”

“Why?”

“Loop closure.”

“It reopened.”

“When?”

“Like ten minutes ago.”

The first teenager grinned. “See?”

They ran for it.

Maya watched them go.

Thirty seconds later the transfer board changed.

SERVICE LOOP 4 — TEMPORARY HOLD  
NEXT CONNECTION: 19 MIN

The same teenager reappeared at the far end of the platform at a full sprint.

The others were laughing.

He reached the closing gate one second late.

It sealed.

He put both hands on the transparent panel and stared through it.

One of his friends bent over laughing.

Maya smiled before she could stop herself.

The teenager turned.

Saw her.

Recognized the smile.

“Don’t.”

“Wasn’t going to say anything.”

“You have the face.”

“What face?”

“The planet face.”

“That is not a thing.”

“It is now.”

His friends laughed harder.

Maya’s arrival band updated.

RECOMMENDED ROUTE: RESTORED  
ESTIMATED: 24 MIN

She looked at him.

“Need help?”

“No.”

“Good.”

She started toward the radial lift.

Behind her, somebody said, “That was cruel.”

The teenager answered, “She’s been here forty minutes.”

Maya smiled all the way to the lift.

---

Her host residence had a view of nothing.

This was apparently considered normal.

The room itself was better than the photos had suggested: narrow but private, built into a residential section where gravity felt close enough to Earth-normal that Maya stopped noticing it after ten minutes.

The window faced an interior pedestrian space.

Across it, two levels down, someone was hanging bright fabric over a balcony rail despite a sign telling residents not to obstruct airflow sensors.

A small food place occupied the corner below.

Three people stood outside it arguing over an order.

No stars.

No Earth.

No majestic curve of the habitat.

Maya liked it immediately.

Her host showed her where to put shoes, where not to put wet clothes, which cabinet was hers, and which door was not a closet.

“Environmental service access,” the host said.

Maya moved her bag away from it.

“That would have been useful on the label.”

“It is labeled.”

Maya looked.

A small symbol sat beside the frame.

“Oh.”

“You’ll see it everywhere.”

“Great.”

Her host smiled. “You’re doing well.”

“That sounded dangerous.”

“It wasn’t meant to.”

“Still.”

The host had already explained the kitchen, the shared laundry allocation, the building quiet period and the fact that Maya’s room wall did not block all sound despite appearing thick enough to stop artillery.

Then they reached the part Maya had been waiting for.

“Late movement.”

Maya sat on the edge of the bed.

“I have the Youth Cohort rules.”

“These are house rules.”

“Okay.”

“If you’re staying in this zone, I don’t need to know where you are every minute.”

“Good.”

“If you leave the zone late, tell me.”

“How late?”

“After the last reliable direct connection.”

“That changes.”

“Yes.”

“So I report based on the route, not a time.”

Her host looked mildly surprised.

“Yes.”

“That makes more sense.”

“If you sleep somewhere else, I need the host or contact.”

Maya’s shoulders tightened.

“I’m not giving you live location.”

“I didn’t ask.”

“My travel packet said hosts may request location sharing.”

“I may request it. You may refuse unless a specific safety condition requires it.”

Maya waited.

That had been easier than expected.

Her host continued.

“If the route changes while you’re out, tell me what zone you’re in and what your return condition is.”

“What does return condition mean here?”

“Whether you’re coming back on the next reliable connection, staying with someone, or waiting out a closure.”

“So you need the plan.”

“I need enough of the plan that if the city closes a route, nobody spends an hour looking for you in the wrong ring.”

Maya leaned back on her hands.

“That seems reasonable.”

“It usually does before midnight.”

“Do I have a curfew?”

“Yes.”

Maya sat up.

Her host smiled.

“You looked disappointed by how well this was going.”

“What time?”

“Weeknights, host-zone return by twenty-three thirty unless the plan changes and we agree.”

“That is a curfew.”

“That is why I used the word.”

“What about Vela Night?”

“We negotiate.”

“What about weekends?”

“We negotiate.”

“What about if everyone else is staying—”

“We negotiate.”

Maya exhaled.

“You really like that word.”

“I host teenagers.”

“Fair.”

Her band chimed.

It had finally recognized the residence.

HOST ZONE CONFIRMED

Maya looked at it.

“Does that send you my position?”

“No.”

“What does it send?”

“That you arrived.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s all I asked for.”

Maya turned the band over in her hand.

The privacy controls were already visible.

Zone acknowledgment.

Emergency contact.

Route disruption permission.

No continuous location stream enabled.

She had spent most of the flight prepared to argue against surveillance.

It was difficult to win an argument nobody was having.

Her host pointed toward the cabinet.

“Food is shared unless marked. The red shelf is not.”

“Why?”

“Environmental compatibility.”

“With what?”

“Dinner.”

Maya stared.

Her host laughed.

“You’ll learn.”

That was the first time anyone in Vela had said something to her that sounded remotely like a guide.

She forgave it because it involved food.

Her phone reconnected to the local network.

Messages flooded in.

ETHAN: everyone arrived? confirm host zones when possible

AOIFE: no

ETHAN: that was directed at everyone

AOIFE: still no

SAMIR: arrived

OLLIE: arrived. route estimate was wrong

MAYA: mine too

ETHAN: by how much?

MAYA: enough

OLLIE: +24 min

MAYA: traitor

Aoife reacted to that with a laughing icon.

Ethan sent:

DINNER TOMORROW? 19:00 local. I found a central point.

Maya looked out through her completely unimpressive interior window.

A teenager across the atrium was trying to carry two food containers and open a door with one elbow.

Somebody called down from a balcony.

The teenager shouted something back.

The fabric over the airflow sensor flapped.

A service light turned amber.

Nobody panicked.

The city kept moving.

Maya typed:

define central

Ethan replied immediately.

GEOMETRICALLY OR BY TRANSFER TIME?

Maya smiled.

He was learning.

Then another message appeared.

ETHAN: actually never mind. transfer time.

AOIFE: historic moment

OLLIE: verify before publication

SAMIR: please stop

Maya put the phone down.

The room was hers for six weeks.

Not her home.

Not yet.

But hers enough to make a mess in.

She opened the bag.

The first thing she took out was the outfit she had packed for Vela Night.

The second was the host information sheet she had promised herself she would read only if forced.

She looked from one to the other.

Then she put the information sheet on top.

Not because the rules were winning.

Because she intended to know exactly which ones she was breaking.
