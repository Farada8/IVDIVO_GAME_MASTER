# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 20 — THE WRONG CAUSE

By 07:40 the next morning, Ring Six had acquired laundry.

Ollie had not expected laundry to matter.

This was becoming a pattern.

A drying rack had appeared outside Lea’s assigned residential volume because the wall unit inside was still waiting for a service cartridge. Two shirts and a narrow towel moved slightly in the corridor airflow every time the local door cycled.

Someone had taped a handwritten note to the rack.

DO NOT MOVE. SERVICE KNOWS.

Under it, in different handwriting:

SERVICE DOES NOT KNOW.

And below that:

SERVICE NOW KNOWS.

Ollie stopped long enough to read all three.

Iri came up behind him carrying an environmental kit.

“You are blocking the route.”

“I am reading the maintenance record.”

“That is laundry.”

“It contains a service history.”

Iri stepped around him.

“You have been here less than two months and already think everything is documentation.”

Ollie followed.

The ring sounded different occupied.

Doors did not cycle on test timing. They opened because somebody had forgotten food, because a child had gone back for a school case, because two residents were trying to carry a wall panel through the same opening from opposite sides.

The common volume smelled faintly of heated grain and sealant.

A service cart waited beside an access panel while its operator argued with a resident over whether a storage wall could be connected before the second household module arrived.

Lea’s younger brother was sitting on the floor with one shoe off.

Ollie stopped.

“Why are you missing a shoe?”

The boy looked up.

“I know where it is.”

“That was not the question.”

“It is in the room.”

“Then why—”

Lea appeared in the doorway.

“Do not investigate him.”

“He is in a public corridor with one shoe.”

“He does that on planets too, probably.”

“I do not,” her brother said.

Lea pointed inside.

“Find it.”

He went.

Ollie looked at the drying rack.

“Your wall unit still down?”

“Waiting on the cartridge.”

“Ticket?”

“Already open.”

“Priority?”

“Ollie.”

He stopped.

Lea leaned against the doorway.

“We moved in twelve hours ago. You are allowed to see something broken without adopting it.”

“I was asking.”

“You were three questions from tools.”

“That is speculation.”

“Local knowledge.”

Iri, already halfway down the corridor, said, “She is right.”

Ollie ignored both of them.

He checked the time.

His occupied-commissioning shift started in six minutes.

At the diagnostic bay Mara was already there.

So was the environmental safety lead from the previous day’s review, along with two technicians and a resident-support liaison.

The main display showed:

R6-A OCCUPANCY: 31% PLANNED LOAD.

Below it:

LIMITED PROFILE ACTIVE.
NEW INFLOW WINDOW: 08:30.

The cap remained forty-two percent.

Mara pointed at Ollie’s station.

“Today you watch sequence and route state.”

“I know.”

“What do you not do?”

“Change operating constraints.”

“What else?”

“Interpret resident reports into medical categories.”

“What else?”

Ollie looked at her.

“Have you added something?”

“No. I am checking if you remember yesterday.”

“I remember yesterday.”

“Good.”

She handed him the shift record.

He opened the live model.

The prohibited overlap rule sat at the top of the transition monitor.

SECOND PARTITION CYCLE BEFORE CORRECTION CLOSURE: LOCKED OUT.

Green.

The reviewed route combinations were loaded beneath it.

Green.

Environmental fixed sensors were inside normal bands.

Green.

The unresolved event rail remained visible on the right.

Empty.

Ollie checked all of it twice.

Mara said, “Anything change the second time?”

“No.”

“Then save the third check for when something does.”

He did not tell her he had been about to do a third.

At 08:06 a household food delivery entered through the approved service route.

At 08:11 one resident reported that a room felt cooler than the setting indicated.

The local sensor showed a normal two-tenths degree variation during door use.

The report went into the resident rail anyway.

At 08:17 a child activated the common-volume ocean wall and discovered that the repeating wave from the Ch15 test had finally been fixed.

The child then complained that it was less interesting.

At 08:24 Lea’s drying-rack ticket updated.

SERVICE CARTRIDGE DELAYED — 4 HRS.

Ollie did not go fix it.

He noticed that he did not go fix it.

He disliked noticing that too.

The first new household block was staged outside the residential boundary for the 08:30 window.

Eight adults.
Three children.
Two service carts.
One folded table that looked too wide for the assigned route and had already generated an argument.

Nadia stood with them checking allocation order.

Mara opened the intake readiness panel.

“Fixed?”

“Nominal,” the safety technician said.

“Route?”

“Reviewed profile.”

“Partition lock?”

“Holding.”

“Resident event rail?”

“One low-severity temperature report at 08:11. No cluster.”

Mara looked at the safety lead.

The safety lead nodded.

“Open first block.”

The boundary changed to controlled entry.

The first three residents crossed.

One child immediately stopped to stare at the ocean wall.

Nadia said, “Keep moving.”

The child kept staring while moving badly.

A parent caught the back of the child’s shirt and redirected them.

Ollie watched the occupancy sequence feed.

The first environmental correction opened.

Counterflow rose.

Air curtain compensated.

Second partition remained locked.

Exactly as designed.

The correction began to settle.

Then Iri stopped walking.

They were inside R6-A near the common-volume service access, carrying the environmental kit toward a routine check.

Ollie saw the pause before the event rail did.

Iri’s hand went to the regulator at their side.

A soft pulse.

PERSONAL INTERFACE ADAPTATION — COARSE EVENT.
08:31:44.2

Ollie looked at the fixed trace.

Normal.

Iri looked toward the diagnostic bay.

Not at him specifically.

At the staff.

They raised two fingers.

Mara said, “Log context.”

The resident-support liaison was already doing it.

“No raw data request. Location and subjective category only.”

Iri spoke through the local channel.

“Dry shift. Maybe pressure. Fast.”

The safety lead said, “Severity?”

“Low.”

“Continue outbound capable?”

“Yes.”

“Hold your position.”

Iri stepped to the side of the route.

Ollie checked his model.

First correction settling.
Second partition locked.
No disputed overlap.

Projected state:

RECOVERY / NORMAL COMPENSATION.

Eleven seconds later a resident near the next connector rubbed both ears.

They kept walking two steps, then stopped.

“Pressure?” the liaison asked.

The resident nodded.

“Bad?”

“No. Just quick.”

The liaison logged it.

RESIDENT-REPORTED PRESSURE CHANGE — LOW SEVERITY.
08:31:55.7

Two events.

Ollie felt the room narrow around the unresolved rail.

Fixed sensors remained nominal.

The first correction closed.

Second partition remained locked.

His model still showed recovery.

The safety lead said, “No new intake after current crossing.”

Nadia answered from staging.

“Copy. I have six still outside.”

“Keep them outside.”

The folded table stopped at the boundary with a family behind it.

Nobody complained yet.

Inside R6-A, one of the service-cart operators said, “I’m getting a dry-mouth report from resident support at A-17.”

The liaison looked over.

“Independent?”

“Yes.”

“Time?”

“Now.”

The third event appeared.

RESIDENT-REPORTED ENVIRONMENTAL CHANGE — LOW SEVERITY.
08:32:08.1

The lower stop protocol activated before any fixed-system warning.

EARLY EVENT CLUSTER — REVIEW HOLD.

Mara said, “Freeze intake.”

Nadia changed the boundary control.

INBOUND HOLD.
OUTBOUND AVAILABLE.

The safety lead said, “Suspend discretionary service cycles. Maintain ventilation baseline. Fixed monitoring to high resolution.”

The technicians moved.

No alarm.

No emergency lights.

The six residents outside the boundary stayed outside.

The people inside kept breathing.

Ollie watched the fixed trace.

Nothing.

A return line shifted by less than one percent and came back.

Normal occupied compensation.

His model still projected recovery.

Iri’s regulator returned to baseline.

The resident with the ear pressure said it had passed.

The A-17 report did not escalate.

Thirty seconds.

Forty-five.

The safety lead said, “Any severe event?”

“No.”

“Any fixed deviation outside occupied band?”

“No.”

Mara asked Ollie, “Sequence state?”

“Explicit overlap absent. Partition lock holding. First correction closed before any secondary partition action.”

“Prediction?”

Ollie looked at the model.

“Current model predicts no disputed fixed lag.”

“Confidence?”

He hated the question now.

“Under the encoded transition classes, high.”

Mara looked at him.

“Encoded.”

“Yes.”

She nodded.

The safety lead said, “Hold continues. We do not reopen intake on a prediction.”

“Agreed,” Mara said.

Ollie did not object.

That mattered later.

At the time, it only felt slow.

* * *

The first operational problem arrived ninety seconds into the hold.

Not from the environment.

From breakfast.

A chilled food-service cart had entered R6-A before the inbound hold and was now stopped near the common-volume service access because a household had temporarily parked two relocation containers in the designated service clearance zone.

The containers were allowed there for unloading.

The food cart was allowed there for delivery.

The timing was bad.

A service worker looked at the corridor, then at the route panel.

“Can I take S-two around?”

The local supervisor checked.

S-2 was a reviewed alternate route under the Ch19 profile.

“Yes. Outbound side stays clear.”

The safety lead heard the exchange.

“No new environmental service cycle with it.”

“None requested.”

The cart reversed.

Ollie saw the route state change on his display.

SERVICE CART — PRIMARY PATH ABORTED.
ALTERNATE S-2 — ACTIVE.

Green.

Reviewed.

The cart moved toward the service throat.

At the same time, the common-volume access door stayed open longer than its ordinary cycle because two residents were carrying the folded table back out after deciding it would not fit through the residential connector.

Outbound remained open by rule.

The door controller extended the local air curtain.

ACCESS AIR CURTAIN — EXTENDED HOLD.

Green.

Allowed.

The environmental system compensated for the extended opening by shifting a small amount of return draw to the adjacent return path.

RETURN BALANCE — A3 INCREASE / A2 DECREASE.

Green.

Automatic occupied-state compensation.

Ollie’s model accepted all three events.

Separately.

The service cart entered S-2.

The alternate throat had its own local pressure buffer so service traffic did not disturb the residential corridor every time a door opened.

BUFFER COMPENSATION — ACTIVE.

Green.

Four green events.

No prohibited partition cycle.

No red state.

No disputed overlap.

Ollie watched the return trace move half a division.

Then another.

Still inside occupied band.

He enlarged the sequence window.

The model classified:

SERVICE ROUTE SUBSTITUTION.
ACCESS HOLD.
RETURN COMPENSATION.
LOCAL BUFFER RESPONSE.

Four event families.

No causal warning.

Iri’s voice came through the local channel.

“Again.”

Ollie looked up.

“What?” Mara asked.

Iri stood beside the service access with one hand against the wall rail.

“Same wrong shift. Stronger.”

Their regulator pulsed twice.

The resident-support liaison logged it immediately.

PERSONAL INTERFACE EVENT — MODERATE.

The safety lead said, “Begin controlled outbound from A corridor. No panic language. Service supervisor, stop the cart where it is if safe.”

The supervisor answered, “Cart is inside S-2. Stopping.”

Mara said, “Ollie. Fixed trace.”

“Still inside band.”

“Sequence?”

“Explicit overlap absent.”

He said it too quickly.

Mara did not correct him.

She was looking at the return paths.

Ollie looked too.

A3 return draw rose.

A2 fell.

The common air curtain was still extended because the table was halfway through the outbound door.

S-2 buffer compensation held the service throat stable.

The first residential correction had technically closed seventy-three seconds earlier.

According to the model, these were unrelated.

The fixed trace moved.

Not much.

A small delayed fall in A2 pressure recovery.

Then A3 over-corrected.

The system compensated.

A2 climbed.

A3 dropped.

Ollie stared.

The pattern was wrong.

Not outside threshold.

Wrong.

He pulled the four event families onto one timeline.

The display stacked them.

SERVICE ROUTE SUBSTITUTION.
ACCESS HOLD.
RETURN COMPENSATION.
BUFFER RESPONSE.

All within nineteen seconds.

His model still showed:

NO DISPUTED TRANSITION OVERLAP.
EXPECTED RECOVERY.

The fixed trace moved again.

This time the environmental safety console gave a low alert.

COUPLED RETURN DEVIATION — WATCH.

Mara said, “Outbound now. Clear R6-A nonessential occupants.”

The safety lead was already issuing the same order.

Nadia’s voice came from staging.

“Inbound remains held. I’m clearing the waiting block away from the boundary.”

The route display changed.

CONTROLLED OUTBOUND — ACTIVE.

Residents began moving.

No sirens.

Staff at the connectors told people to take bags only if they were already in hand.

Lea appeared at the edge of the common corridor with her brother.

He was wearing both shoes now.

She had a food container in one hand and nothing else.

“What happened?” she asked a liaison.

“Environmental review. Keep moving to staging.”

“Are we coming back?”

“Not answering that yet.”

Lea’s mouth tightened.

She kept moving.

Ollie looked back at the timeline.

The four events were still separate categories.

He dragged a bracket around all of them.

The model did nothing.

He added the current return-state values.

A2 low.
A3 high.
S-2 buffer active.
Main access curtain extended.

A topology view opened.

For one second he thought the display had loaded the wrong event.

The return network state looked almost identical to the disputed overlap state from the original four incidents.

Not the same doors.

Not the same sequence labels.

The same relationship.

He said, “Mara.”

She was coordinating with the safety lead.

“What?”

“The partition didn’t do it.”

“I know.”

“No. I mean—”

The fixed trace crossed the first stop threshold.

The room alarm sounded once.

Not loud.

Enough.

COUPLED OSCILLATION — CONFIRMED.

Mara said, “Isolation protocol. R6-A occupied evacuation.”

The safety lead hit the authorization.

The ring changed state.

The common-volume lighting shifted to evacuation guidance.

Outbound route arrows appeared along the floor and wall interfaces.

The first isolation doors began closing behind cleared residential branches.

Mara pointed at Ollie.

“Station C. Route status only.”

He moved.

That was the point.

At Station C he could see three things:

which residential branch was clear;
which isolation boundary was closed;
which outbound path remained open.

He could not change environmental state.

He could not override doors.

He could not fix the model.

“Confirmed clear.”

“A-15 has two residents plus liaison moving outbound.”

“Route open through common north.”

Nobody answered.

The fixed oscillation grew.

Pressure and humidity stayed well inside anything that would injure people directly, but the coupled corrections were now chasing one another instead of settling.

Every compensation produced another compensation.

The building was answering late and then answering its own answer.

Mara and the safety lead isolated the return paths in sequence.

A3 closed.

A2 stabilized for three seconds.

“Tomas and Kevi. Clearing now.”

Tomas.

Forty-six minutes if he missed the direct transfer.

Grandmother twice a week.

The automatic boundary tried to close and stopped at pre-close because the chilled service cart was still partly across the service-compartment line.

OBSTRUCTION — ISOLATION DELAYED.
