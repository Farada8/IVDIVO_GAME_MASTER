# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 26 — THE SECOND MODEL

Ollie was allowed to repair a waste-line sensor and not allowed to see the Ring Six incident record.

The waste-line sensor had at least had the courtesy to be wrong in public.

It lived behind a panel in an occupied module two rings away from Ring Six and had been reporting a fill estimate six percent higher than physical inspection for three mornings.

Nobody had evacuated.

Nobody had called a review board.

A resident had complained because the service warning turned the kitchen wall orange during breakfast.

Ollie replaced the mount.

The reading dropped five percent.

Iri checked the service tablet.

“One percent high.”

“It is still wrong.”

“It is holding waste, not proving a theorem.”

“It is a measurement.”

The resident leaned out of the kitchen.

“Can the wall stop being orange?”

Ollie cleared the warning.

“Yes.”

“Best thing you’ve done all week.”

The resident disappeared back inside.

Iri clipped the panel shut.

“There. Public recognition.”

Ollie put the old mount into the return pouch.

His band vibrated before he could explain why kitchen-wall praise was not the recognition he had meant.

INCIDENT REVIEW — MODELING RESPONSE AVAILABLE.

He stopped.

Iri, already moving to the next ticket, nearly walked into him.

“You picked the wall option.”

Ollie opened the message while walking.

The review had not sent him incident data.

It had sent six bounded outputs, each with its own source label, purpose restriction and confidence note.

No names. No personal route histories. No raw body-support feeds. No full Ring Six replay.

The first was the mature-module comparison Mara had routed through review after his maintenance query.

Six modules. Two control generations. Thirty days.

At the top:

NEGATIVE CASES DOMINATE.

The weak subset remained weak: several reports clustered near combined return-correction and service-transition states, but the relationship changed sharply by module and control generation. Remove one module and most of the apparent timing effect disappeared.

The second output was the source-bound correction Aoife had passed through incident review.

EARLY REPORT WINDOW PRECEDES OFFICIAL RECONSTRUCTION MARKER.

Timing only. No recording, route, accommodation detail or original words.

The third came from Samir’s Service Transfer Four comparison.

HELD / REVERSED MOVEMENT STATE ACTIVE BEFORE FIXED CORRECTION DISPLAY.

No exact subjective onset. No team data.

The remaining outputs were mostly resistance: two negative comparisons and one local-support event class that could not be aligned tightly enough to the others.

At the bottom:

REVIEW QUESTION:

CAN THESE EVIDENCE CLASSES SUPPORT A FALSIFIABLE MODELING PROPOSAL WITHOUT EXPANDING DATA ACCESS?

Ollie read it twice.

Iri glanced at the screen.

“What did they give you?”

“Outputs. Not the record.”

“And the question?”

He showed them.

Iri read it while walking.

“Looks like work.”

Ollie wanted to object to the smallness of it.

Instead he put the band away.

If the model failed, he would not be able to rescue it by opening another dataset.

That was irritating.

It was also cleaner.

* * *

The first model had one center.

Building state in the middle. Everything else waited around it until Ollie could translate it into building language.

This time he began with separate layers.

FIXED ENVIRONMENTAL STATE.

PARTITION / SERVICE STATE.

MOVEMENT ORDER.

PRIVACY-SAFE TIMING ANCHORS.

EARLY RESIDENT / BODY / SUPPORT RESPONSE CLASS.

SOURCE CONSTRAINT.

NEGATIVE CASES.

CONFIDENCE.

At the top of the workroom screen he wrote:

AUTHORIZED TASK: MODELING PROPOSAL ONLY.

NO INCIDENT-REVIEW ACCESS.

NO LIVE RING SIX ACCESS.

The sources did not fit together neatly.

A fixed sensor produced synchronized numbers.

A service log gave commanded or observed machine state.

Samir gave movement order plus an approximate timestamp from a note made at the time.

Aoife’s source correction gave only one bounded earlier-warning relationship.

Resident reports could be dry air, pressure, airflow, a support device adapting, or simply wrong.

Some negative cases contained the same building states and no complaint at all.

Ollie tried one combined confidence score.

The number looked professional and meant almost nothing.

He deleted it.

Ren appeared at the workroom door carrying two sealed food packs.

“That looked painful.”

“Bad score.”

“Historic.”

“I have had bad ideas before.”

“Not voluntarily.”

Ren put one food pack on the opposite side of the table and opened the other.

“Is that mine?” Ollie asked.

“Kalen’s.”

“You are eating Kalen’s food.”

“I am preventing temperature degradation.”

“That is theft.”

“Maintenance.”

Ren stayed long enough to notice that NEGATIVE CASES was the largest layer.

“Why keep the big useless column?”

“Because it might be the useful one.”

“That sounded suspiciously mature.”

Ollie ignored him.

The weak cases did not share a universal lead time. Samir’s case did not supply a body warning. Aoife’s source correction did not supply a route. The mature-module subset did not survive every module.

But they could all be arranged in sequence.

Something local.

Then another state.

Then, sometimes, the fixed representation later.

Not always. Not under most conditions.

Ollie added:

RELATIVE EVENT ORDER.

Then:

STATE EQUIVALENCE UNKNOWN.

He moved the early-response layer into the same horizontal sequence as service and fixed states instead of leaving it in an annotation rail.

Ren looked over again.

“Ugly.”

“Yes.”

“Probably healthier.”

Kalen arrived.

“You ate my food.”

Ren held up the empty pack.

“It was degrading.”

Kalen did not argue the science. He grabbed Ren by the shoulder and pulled him toward the door.

The two of them left discussing compensation at a volume that made the workroom less useful.

Ollie kept the ugly model.

* * *

Tomas was supposed to be on light-duty coordination.

This had apparently evolved into standing in a service passage with a braced forearm while preventing other people from creating heavy-duty problems.

“Not there,” he told a worker pushing a cleaning cart.

The worker moved it half a metre.

“Still not there.”

Another half metre.

Tomas pointed at the floor seam.

“If inspection opens that panel, your cart becomes everybody’s new route.”

The worker sighed and pushed it to the opposite wall.

Ollie stopped beside Tomas.

The scene had already answered half the question Mara had authorized.

Tomas noticed him.

“What?”

Ollie showed the request header.

SERVICE SUBSTITUTION / MATURE OCCUPIED MODULES — ONE BOUNDED QUERY.

Tomas read it, then looked at the cart.

“You want to know how often routes change without a route event.”

“Yes.”

Tomas pointed at three people walking around the cleaning cart’s new position.

“There.”

“The system still calls the corridor open.”

“Because it is open.”

“But movement changed.”

Tomas shifted the tablet to his unbraced hand.

“Cleaning. Deliveries. Inspection stands. Slow mobility. Somebody opens a panel and owns half the corridor for ten minutes. Service stays technically available, so nobody creates a transit event.”

A maintenance pair approached with a long equipment case. They saw the cleaning cart, turned sideways and took the opposite edge of the passage.

Tomas watched them go.

“That one will change the next door timing too.”

Ollie looked at the service display.

Nothing registered as reroute.

“What if I put service substitution in the model?”

“Do not make it yes or no.”

“Because?”

Tomas lifted his braced arm slightly toward the corridor.

“Walking around a cart is not crossing a climate boundary with a delivery. Same label, different load.”

Ollie opened a note, then stopped.

Tomas saw him.

“You can write that. I am not a protected source.”

Ollie entered:

SERVICE SUBSTITUTION — MAGNITUDE / LOCAL STATE EFFECT UNKNOWN.

Tomas glanced at it.

“Looks ugly enough.”

The cleaning worker called his name from farther down the passage.

Tomas went back to his actual job.

Ollie left with the question changed.

* * *

Maya had seven minutes between training and food.

She spent the first one walking fast enough that Ollie had to decide whether asking his question was worth losing lunch.

“You called it route behavior,” she said.

“I corrected that.”

“You thought it.”

“I need to know what makes people take paths the planner does not predict.”

“People.”

“Maya.”

She stepped onto a moving transfer strip without slowing.

Ollie followed.

“Friends. Food. Somebody you are avoiding. Somebody you are trying to find. A queue that looks bad. A route that was faster yesterday. Host cutoff. Carrying something stupid. Everyone coming the other way.”

Ollie reached for his tablet.

Maya caught his wrist and pushed it down without stopping.

“If you turn that into eight boxes while I am still talking, I leave.”

He let the tablet hang at his side.

A group coming off the opposite strip forced them toward the inner edge. Maya moved before Ollie did, slipped behind two local students and emerged one lane over.

Ollie had to wait for a gap.

When he caught up, she was smiling.

“You just deviated from the recommended route.”

“No. I went to food.”

“The planner routed you along the outer lane.”

“The planner does not know Davi blocks that corner after training every day because he waits for people.”

Ollie looked back.

Davi was exactly where she had said, surrounded by four others and narrowing the passage without technically obstructing it.

“The system thinks you took an alternate.”

Maya shrugged.

“I think Tuesday happened.”

That was better than the answer Ollie had been trying to request.

He waited until they reached the food counter before opening the tablet.

Maya saw the blank note.

“Fine. One sentence.”

Ollie typed:

WHOSE ROUTE DEFINES SUBSTITUTION?

She read it over his shoulder.

“That is not terrible.”

“High praise.”

“You used six of my seven minutes.”

“Food is here.”

“Then stop being useful.”

She took her order and left him with the question.

* * *

Aoife chose the transit waiting volume.

Ollie suspected this was because it gave her a departure board she could use to end the conversation.

She was already drinking tea when he arrived.

“You bought one.”

“Yes.”

“So I do not have to.”

“I planned ahead.”

“That feels hostile.”

Aoife pointed at the empty seat.

“What do you actually need?”

“The authorized correction only.”

“You have it.”

“I want to know what kind of statement it supports.”

Aoife’s posture changed before her expression did.

Ollie kept his tablet closed.

“The source allowed de-identified timing to incident review,” she said. “Not the recording, original words, route or accommodation context.”

“I am not asking for those.”

“The correction says the warning happened before the point the first reconstruction used as the start of the event.”

“How much before?”

Aoife shook her head.

“Review does not have authorization for a tighter number.”

Ollie let the question die.

“Cause?”

“Not established.”

“Same environmental variable?”

“Not established.”

“Same physical state as my first model?”

“You do not have the source context to say that.”

The departure board changed above them.

Aoife glanced at it.

Ollie said, “So I can use order and nothing tighter.”

“You can use exactly what review released.”

He nodded.

Aoife watched him for a moment.

“Do not turn the source into the person who solved your model.”

“I won’t.”

Her mouth tightened.

“That is not about your intention.”

Ollie waited.

“If the model works, people will tell the story backwards. One early warning becomes the clue everybody should have understood. It wasn’t that clean.”

Ollie thought about the six-module table.

Thirty-seven personal/interface events with no fixed update.

“Negative cases stay in.”

Aoife picked up her tea.

“That is your job.”

Her transfer appeared on the board.

She stood.

“That was shorter than expected,” Ollie said.

“I chose the location well.”

Then she left.

* * *

The second model still looked wrong.

That was useful because Iri disliked it before they had even sat down.

They were in the older service corner with bad seating and better food. Iri had twenty-three minutes before a rehearsal equipment check.

They scrolled through the layers.

Fixed state.

Service state.

Movement order.

Privacy-safe timing anchors.

Early response class.

Source constraint.

Negative cases.

Service substitution.

Iri stopped at EARLY RESPONSE CLASS.

“Which one?”

Ollie understood the question.

“Right now it contains person-reported environmental change, personal support response and local interface change.”

“And no report?”

“Negative cases are separate.”

Iri pushed the tablet back.

Ollie split the layer before arguing.

PERSON-REPORTED ENVIRONMENTAL CHANGE.

PERSONAL SUPPORT / LOCAL INTERFACE RESPONSE.

LOCAL SERVICE-BOUNDARY OBSERVATION.

NO EARLY RESPONSE OBSERVED.

Iri pulled the screen back toward them.

“That is less dishonest.”

“Encouraging.”

“Do not ruin it.”

They ate while Ollie rearranged the model.

The route field still bothered him.

MAIN PARTITION A.
COUNTERFLOW.
RETURN CORRECTION.
SERVICE TRANSITION B.
LOCAL ADAPT.
FIXED DISPLAY.

Iri tapped the first and fourth labels.

“You still named the doors.”

“They are observed states.”

“They are observed hardware.”

Ollie looked at the service map on the wall.

Iri pointed toward it with a piece of flatbread.

“A door does not care whether six people arrived because the planned connector opened, they followed a delivery cart, cleaning pushed them sideways or somebody forgot food.”

“That is movement order.”

“It is load relationship.”

Ollie zoomed out from the named route diagram.

Maya’s question sat beside it.

WHOSE ROUTE DEFINES SUBSTITUTION?

Tomas’s note sat underneath.

SERVICE SUBSTITUTION — MAGNITUDE / LOCAL STATE EFFECT UNKNOWN.

Ollie removed the door names.

LOCAL DEMAND CHANGING.

RETURN CORRECTION ACTIVE.

MOVEMENT LOAD REDIRECTED.

SECOND LOCAL ADAPTATION ENTERS BEFORE SETTLE.

He stopped before the final line.

FIXED / AGGREGATE REPRESENTATION LAGS LOCAL STATE.

Too broad.

He marked it:

CANDIDATE / MUST FALSIFY.

Iri read the new sequence.

“Different hardware could make that.”

“That is the point.”

“Could. Not does.”

Ollie changed the heading:

STATE RELATIONSHIP / HARDWARE EQUIVALENCE UNKNOWN.

Iri checked the time.

“You have a testable problem now.”

“Maybe.”

They smiled.

“That word improves you.”

Iri stood, gathered the food wrapper and rehearsal bag, and left before Ollie could turn the comment into another discussion.

* * *

Mara read the proposal for eleven minutes.

Ollie counted because pretending not to count made the seconds louder.

They were in a review room beside maintenance coordination. No live Ring Six systems. No privileged display. Mara’s tablet, Ollie’s proposal and a clock he was beginning to resent.

At minute eleven she turned the document toward him.

“What are you asking review to do?”

“Build a controlled reproduction.”

“Of what?”

“Not the incident. A safe low-amplitude test of the candidate state relationship under different visible routes and known negative conditions.”

Mara tapped the source layer.

“And these?”

“Remain separate evidence classes. No conversion into one lead-time variable.”

“The module-generation effect?”

“Preserved.”

“Samir?”

“Movement order only. No body-warning claim.”

“Aoife’s source correction?”

“Order only. No exact lag, cause or variable identity.”

Mara moved to the negative cases.

“They stay in the preregistration?”

“Yes.”

She routed the document.

PROPOSAL → INCIDENT REVIEW / SAFETY TEST DESIGN.

Ollie watched the status change to:

PENDING TECHNICAL REVIEW.

His credential did not change.

REVIEW HOLD.

He had expected the disappointment by now. Expecting it did not improve it.

Mara closed the routing screen.

“If safety accepts the concept, adults design and own the test. You may get a bounded observation or timing role because the hypothesis is yours. The hold remains. No unrestricted Ring Six or incident access.”

Ollie looked at the closed routing screen.

“Understood.”

“Actually?”

“I dislike it and understand it.”

“That is sufficient.”

He stood.

At the door Mara added, “Safety will change the proposal.”

“How much?”

“If we already knew, we would not need them.”

Outside, Iri was waiting with a percussion-frame clamp in one hand.

“You got access?”

“No.”

Iri handed him the broken clamp.

“Hold this.”

“Why?”

“I need both hands.”

“For what?”

“To fix something that is actually broken.”

Ollie held the clamp while Iri opened a tool roll on the floor.

His band vibrated.

INCIDENT REVIEW:

SECOND-MODEL PROPOSAL RECEIVED.

NO ACCESS CHANGE.

SAFETY DESIGN REVIEW PENDING.

Ollie locked the message without reopening the credential.

Iri glanced up.

“Anything?”

“Not yet.”

They tightened the first fastener.

Ollie kept holding the clamp.
