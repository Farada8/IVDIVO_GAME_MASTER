ORBITAL YOUTH
Chapter 21 — SHUT

Ollie spent twenty-three minutes trying to open a file he already knew he was not allowed to open.

The first attempt produced:

RING SIX INCIDENT DATA — REVIEW PARTITION
ACCESS: HOLD

The second produced the same thing.

So did the third.

On the fourth, the system stopped being polite.

YOUR ACCESS STATE HAS NOT CHANGED.

Ollie stared at it.

Ren, at the other end of the service table, said, “I think it knows you now.”

“I am checking whether the review partition has propagated.”

“You checked that six minutes ago.”

“It could have changed.”

“Did it?”

“No.”

“Good investigation.”

Ollie closed the request.

He opened the incident cross-reference instead.

That one he was allowed to read.

It was worse.

The document did not contain a line that said OLLIE CAUSED RING SIX TO OPEN.

He had been looking for one anyway.

Decision inputs appeared as a sequence of attached records.

HOUSING / TEMPORARY ALLOCATION PRESSURE.

RESIDENT ADVISORY — SPLIT.

SCHOOL / ROUTE CONSEQUENCE.

SERVICE STAFFING IMPACT.

ENVIRONMENTAL SAFETY CONDITIONS.

BOUNDED OCCUPANCY TEST — CH15.

TIMING / TRANSITION ANALYSIS.

PUBLIC TECHNICAL POSITION — RESTRICTED PROFILE SUPPORT.

COMMISSIONING BOARD AUTHORIZATION.

Then the occupied incident.

No arrow connected his name to the injured maintenance worker.

No arrow removed it from the chain either.

He opened the board record.

His answer from Ch19 sat exactly where he remembered it.

SUPPORT LIMITED HABITATION UNDER DEFINED RESTRICTIONS: YES.

The next field contained the safety engineer’s lower stop threshold.

The next contained housing’s allocation schedule.

The next contained resident deferral rights.

The next contained the board vote.

The decision had been made by people doing their jobs.

That did not make Ollie feel better.

He switched to the technical appendix he was still allowed to see.

The event reconstruction had been updated since the evacuation.

09:18 — RESIDENT REPORT.

09:22 — PERSONAL-INTERFACE REPORT.

09:25 — THIRD SAME-CLASS REPORT / REVIEW THRESHOLD.

INBOUND STAGING PAUSED.

RESIDENT MOVEMENT REDUCED.

EAST SERVICE — HELD / OCCUPIED.

COMMON RETURN CORRECTION — ACTIVE.

SERVICE C — OPEN.

PARTITION B — LOCKED.

Then the first fixed rise.

Five human or interface reports existed before the fixed threshold crossing.

Ollie knew that.

He had watched them arrive.

What he had not seen was the next diagram.

The review team had overlaid the environmental control dependencies rather than the named route states.

He leaned closer.

East service and service C belonged to separate route categories in his model.

The common return correction belonged to environmental control.

Partition B belonged to residential sequencing.

Separate labels.

Separate rule checks.

Underneath them, however, two airflow controls and the common-volume return path shared the same adaptive environmental loop.

Ollie expanded the timeline.

The east door stayed held around the waste cart.

The common return correction extended because residents reversed direction.

Service C opened to receive the same cart.

No prohibited partition moved.

No single state violated the profile.

The shared loop still received simultaneous demand from three allowed actions.

He moved the timestamps together.

The shape appeared.

Not the old direct overlap.

The same demand relationship produced without the named transition that his model had been watching for.

Ollie sat very still.

Ren looked up.

“What?”

Ollie did not answer.

Ren waited.

That was unusual enough to make Ollie notice.

He rotated the diagram once more.

The result remained preliminary.

The review note said so in three places.

POSSIBLE DISTRIBUTED EQUIVALENCE.

NOT ROOT-CAUSE FINDING.

EMBODIED / PERSONAL-INTERFACE ROLE UNKNOWN.

He read the second line twice.

Ren finally came over.

“Can I look?”

“It is open review material.”

“That means yes?”

“Yes.”

Ren leaned over the display.

Ollie pointed at the three states.

“I treated these as separate.”

“They are separate.”

“Operationally.”

Ren looked at the dependency layer.

“Oh.”

“Yes.”

“That is bad.”

Ollie closed the diagram.

Ren straightened.

“I did not say you were bad.”

“I know.”

“You made the face.”

“I have no idea what face everyone keeps referring to.”

“The one where you start arguing with a sentence nobody said.”

Ollie opened the decision record again.

Ren watched him.

“Are you trying to find your percentage?”

Ollie looked up.

“My what?”

“Of responsibility.”

“That is not a real quantity.”

“You are still trying.”

Ollie did not answer.

Ren went back to his own work.

The silence did more than the argument would have.

Ollie read the record again.

His analysis had not opened Ring Six.

His analysis had removed one reason not to.

The distinction was exact.

It was also not an escape.

* * *

Mara refused him at 11:06.

He had waited until 11:05 to ask because asking earlier would have looked desperate.

He had spent the previous hour being desperate privately.

“I want supervised access to the raw incident logs.”

“No.”

“I do not need system control.”

“No.”

“Read-only.”

“No.”

“Then export the environmental dependency layer.”

Mara kept working.

“No.”

Ollie stood on the other side of her service bench.

“You said the hold was procedural.”

“It is.”

“Then procedure can define supervised access.”

“It can.”

“So define it.”

“No.”

He stopped.

Mara finished tagging a damaged service component before she looked at him.

“You are trying the same tactic with more words.”

“I am narrowing the request.”

“You are trying to make the request small enough that I feel unreasonable saying no.”

Ollie opened his mouth.

Mara held up one finger.

“No.”

He closed it.

She turned the damaged component over.

“Review hold means the people whose work is under review do not alter the evidence environment and do not receive privileged access to the review team’s raw material.”

“I would not alter anything.”

“I did not accuse you of altering anything.”

“Then—”

“No.”

Ollie looked at the component in her hands.

It was a pressure-door track sensor from somewhere outside Ring Six.

Ordinary work.

He disliked it on sight.

Mara noticed.

“Good.”

“What?”

“You have an available task.”

“That is not Ring Six.”

“Correct.”

“I can still be useful to the review.”

“Probably.”

“Then why am I calibrating track sensors?”

“Because this sensor needs calibration and you are authorized to do it.”

Ollie waited for the rest.

There was no rest.

“Mara.”

“What do you want me to say?”

“That the hold is not because you think I will make it worse.”

Mara’s expression changed by almost nothing.

“Do you want reassurance or access?”

Ollie looked away first.

“Access.”

“No.”

He hated the clean answer.

Mara pushed the sensor toward him.

“Track module E-seventeen. Mature ring. Service corridor. Iri is already there.”

Ollie did not touch it.

Mara waited.

He said, “You could give this to Ren.”

“I could.”

“Why me?”

“Because you asked for work.”

“I asked for review work.”

“You asked to be useful.”

Ollie looked at her.

Mara did not look away.

That was the argument.

He took the sensor.

“E-seventeen?”

“Yes.”

“Track module.”

“Yes.”

“Any relation to Ring Six?”

“Not that I know of.”

He heard himself ask the next question before deciding whether he should.

“Will doing this change my access state?”

“No.”

The answer landed harder than he expected.

Mara turned back to the service inventory.

Ollie picked up the calibration case.

At the door she said, “Ollie.”

He stopped.

“Useful and permitted are different questions.”

He waited for a lesson.

She gave him none.

He left.

* * *

Iri was halfway inside a wall panel when Ollie arrived.

Only their legs were visible.

Ollie put the calibration case down.

“Apparently I am your punishment.”

From inside the panel:

“No.”

He waited.

Nothing else came.

“That was a joke.”

“I know.”

“You usually react.”

“I did.”

Ollie looked at the two legs.

This was going badly and the other person had not even become fully visible.

He opened the case.

“Track module E-seventeen.”

“I know where we are.”

“I was naming the task.”

“I also know the task.”

Ollie took out the calibration block.

Iri backed out of the panel.

Their hair had caught dust at one side. They did not fix it.

“Did Mara send you?”

“Yes.”

“Review hold?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Ollie looked at them.

Iri reached for the calibration lead.

He did not hand it over.

“What does good mean?”

“It means the hold is working.”

“I did not cause the defect.”

“I did not say you did.”

“You said good.”

“Yes.”

“About me being removed.”

“About review rules applying to you.”

“That is different.”

“I know.”

Ollie handed over the lead harder than necessary.

Iri caught it.

They connected the block.

The track sensor reported a five-percent drift.

Iri said, “Again.”

Ollie reset the test.

The second reading was 4.8.

“Replace?” he asked.

“Clean first.”

“It is outside preferred calibration.”

“Clean first.”

“That will not fix an internal drift.”

“Then you will be right in three minutes.”

Ollie took the cleaning strip.

They worked in silence.

The silence was not neutral.

Ollie knew because he kept wanting to break it.

He cleaned the contact surface.

Iri checked the seating.

Ollie ran the calibration again.

1.2 percent.

He stared at it.

Iri said nothing.

“You knew.”

“I suspected.”

“You could have said contact contamination.”

“You could have cleaned it.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

Ollie put the cleaning strip away.

Iri started reseating the panel.

He said, “Are you angry with me?”

Iri stopped.

Not dramatically.

Their hand stayed on the panel fastener.

“Yes.”

Ollie had prepared for a less efficient answer.

“For the opening?”

“No.”

“For saying yes?”

“Not exactly.”

“That is not useful.”

Iri turned.

“Do you want useful or honest?”

“Both.”

“Of course.”

They tightened the fastener once and stopped again.

“You heard it.”

Ollie waited.

“The reports,” Iri said. “You heard them.”

“I logged them.”

“That is not what I said.”

“I did not ignore them.”

“I did not say you ignored them.”

Ollie felt himself moving into the argument faster than the words required.

“The reports contradicted each other. Different language. Different onset precision. Some people said pressure, some airflow, some just wrong. They did not all happen before fixed changes. The ones during Ch15 did not escalate.”

“I know.”

“So what exactly should I have done?”

Iri looked at him for a long second.

“Not that.”

“Not what?”

“Turn it into a defense before I finish.”

Ollie closed his mouth.

Iri looked back at the panel.

The corridor carried on around them. A maintenance cart passed. Someone farther down asked for a filter size. A child in the occupied residential section complained that a door was too slow.

Iri said, “The words were messy. Yes.”

Ollie said nothing.

“The timing was less messy.”

He looked at them.

Iri continued.

“Not perfect. Less messy. People kept reporting a change before the fixed system admitted there was one.”

“Not every time.”

Iri turned their head.

Ollie stopped.

“Sorry.”

“That was fast.”

“I am attempting improvement.”

“Suspicious.”

It was almost their normal rhythm.

Almost.

Iri put the panel tool down.

“You treated the reports like annotations until the building gave you something clean enough to call evidence.”

“That is not fair.”

“Good.”

“What?”

“It is not fair. The reports were hard to compare. Some were adaptation. Some might have been unrelated. You needed repeatability.”

Ollie had been ready to resist.

The agreement removed the place he had planned to stand.

Iri continued.

“But repeatability does not have to mean same words. It could mean same order.”

Ollie looked at the maintenance display.

Resident report.

Fixed response.

He saw the Ch20 rail in his head.

Five entries.

Then threshold.

“That still does not make the reports causal.”

“No.”

“Or prove they are sensing the same variable.”

“No.”

“Or tell us whether personal adaptation is part of the loop.”

“No.”

Ollie frowned.

“You are being very difficult to argue with.”

“I am trying something new.”

He almost laughed.

It did not quite happen.

Iri picked up the calibration case and put it in his hands.

“Next panel.”

“That is the end of the argument?”

“For now.”

“You cannot end an argument by assigning maintenance.”

“I just did.”

Ollie followed them down the corridor.

Trust had not returned.

The work had.

* * *

The next panel was not a track sensor.

It was an environmental service interface in an older occupied module, two generations behind Ring Six and apparently designed by people who hated knees.

Ollie knelt on the floor.

“I withdraw all criticism of Ring Six access panels.”

Iri handed him a tool.

“Historic moment.”

The service request was ordinary.

INTERMITTENT LOCAL COMFORT COMPLAINTS.
NO CURRENT FAULT.
CHECK INTERFACE / FILTER / RETURN TIMING.

Ollie opened the complaint history because it was part of the maintenance task.

Four entries.

The first said:

DRY EDGE AFTER SERVICE DOOR. CLEARS QUICKLY.

The second:

PRESSURE FEELS LATE ON RETURN. NO FIXED ALERT.

The third:

AIR CHANGED AFTER DOOR / PERSONAL SUPPORT CORRECTED BEFORE WALL UPDATE.

The fourth was six months later:

SAME THING. SHORT. NO FAULT FOUND LAST TIME.

Ollie stopped scrolling.

Iri was checking the filter housing.

“What?”

He turned the display toward them.

Iri read the four entries.

Their face changed.

Not recognition exactly.

Attention.

“Different module generation,” they said.

“Yes.”

“Different return control.”

“Yes.”

“No Ring Six partition logic.”

“Yes.”

Iri looked at the third complaint again.

PERSONAL SUPPORT CORRECTED BEFORE WALL UPDATE.

Ollie said, “Lead timing.”

“Maybe.”

“Same ordering.”

“Maybe.”

“You are enjoying that word.”

“Very much.”

Ollie opened the fixed service history.

No incident.

No threshold event.

No damage.

The complaints had been closed as intermittent comfort issues after checks found no component fault.

Reasonable at the time.

Possibly still reasonable.

He searched the local task scope for access to other modules.

Denied.

Maintenance ticket only.

He looked at Iri.

“Can we search—”

“No.”

“I did not finish.”

“You were going to ask for broader complaint history.”

“Yes.”

“Not on this ticket.”

Ollie looked at the four entries.

“You sound like Mara.”

“Insulting.”

“What can we do?”

Iri pointed at the open panel.

“Finish the job.”

“That is not what I mean.”

“I know.”

They checked the filter.

Clean.

Ollie checked the interface.

Normal.

Return timing inside mature-module tolerance.

No fault.

He entered the maintenance result.

NO CURRENT COMPONENT FAULT.

Then stopped before closing the ticket.

There was a field for related observation.

He looked at Iri.

“Allowed?”

Iri read the field.

“Yes.”

Ollie typed:

HISTORICAL COMPLAINTS INCLUDE REPEATED USER/PERSONAL-INTERFACE CHANGE PRECEDING FIXED-SYSTEM UPDATE. NO CLAIM OF COMMON CAUSE. FLAG FOR REVIEW IF INCIDENT TEAM REQUESTS CROSS-MODULE COMPARISON.

He showed Iri before submitting.

They read it.

“Good.”

Ollie waited.

Iri looked at him.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You wanted more.”

“No.”

“You made the face.”

Ollie submitted the ticket.

The system accepted the note.

No access expanded.

No mystery solved.

No one called him back to Ring Six.

They closed the panel and moved to the next service request.

Behind them, the older module stayed occupied.

A resident opened the service door.

The local return correction adjusted.

Nobody stopped walking.
