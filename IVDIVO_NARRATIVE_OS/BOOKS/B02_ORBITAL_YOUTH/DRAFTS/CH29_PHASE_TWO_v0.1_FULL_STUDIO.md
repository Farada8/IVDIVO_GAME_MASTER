# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 29 — PHASE TWO

The volunteers were late for ordinary reasons.

One had a class that ended eleven minutes later than the test schedule assumed.

One had been moved to a different shift after Ring Six relocation work changed a service rota.

Two arrived together carrying food and arguing about whether the test room would allow it.

The answer was no.

They finished eating in the corridor.

Ollie watched through the observer glass while the participant coordinator explained this without apology.

Phase Two had been booked for three hours.

It lost twenty-two minutes before anyone entered the rig.

Ollie liked that more than he should have.

It made the test feel less like a demonstration built to prove his model.

Mara stood beside him with the Phase Two protocol open.

“Read your access.”

Ollie looked at his temporary band.

TEST RIG 4 — ANALYSIS PARTICIPANT.

PARTICIPANT IDENTITIES — HIDDEN.

PERSONAL SUPPORT TELEMETRY — NO ACCESS.

EVENT CHANNEL — COARSE TIMING ONLY / AFTER CONTROL-STATE LOCK.

RING SIX — REVIEW HOLD UNCHANGED.

“I know what it says.”

“Read it anyway.”

He did.

Mara waited through the last line.

“Good.”

Ollie looked toward the participant briefing room.

“Iri is not here.”

“I noticed.”

“They were eligible.”

“That is not the same as scheduled.”

“I asked.”

“What did they say?”

“Not today.”

“Why?”

“Rehearsal, then a household thing.”

Mara looked at him.

Ollie added, “I said okay.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

“Immediately?”

“Yes.”

“Congratulations.”

“That sounded sarcastic.”

“It was observational.”

The participant coordinator opened the internal briefing channel.

Six anonymous participant slots appeared on Ollie’s side of the system.

P1.
P2.
P3.
P4.
P5.
P6.

He could not open their identity fields.

He tried one.

ACCESS NOT ASSIGNED.

Mara did not look at him.

“That one was necessary,” Ollie said.

“I said nothing.”

“Exactly.”

The participant coordinator explained the event channels.

Each person could choose one of three forms of participation for each run.

Manual marker: press once if they noticed the kind of environmental or body change described in the consent briefing.

Coarse personal-support event: if they already used a support system that could emit a simple event-time class, they could authorize that event only for the run.

No personal-support feed: manual marker only, or no marker if they noticed nothing.

No raw values.

No diagnosis.

No promise that a marker meant the rig had changed.

No requirement to keep participating after a run.

One volunteer asked through the participant channel, “If I press it and nothing happened, do I fail?”

The coordinator said, “No.”

“If I do not press and something did happen?”

“No.”

“Then what are you measuring?”

“Timing patterns across runs. Not whether you are good at this.”

Ollie looked at his analysis window.

He had been about to create a column called ACCURACY.

He deleted the empty column before anyone saw it.

Mara saw him delete it.

She did not comment.

That was worse.

* * *

The blind structure was simple enough that Ollie distrusted it.

The control team ran the rig.

A separate analysis process estimated the shared-loop state-entry window from control data only.

That estimate locked.

Only then did the participant timing rail become visible to Ollie.

He could not move the window after seeing the markers.

He had argued for that in Chapter Twenty-Six.

He now hated it operationally.

The first run was a negative control.

N1.

Full physical baseline.

Ordinary service transition.

No residual return window.

Prediction:

NO CANDIDATE SHARED-LOOP ENTRY.

The participant coordinator counted down.

The control engineer ran the sequence.

Nothing interesting happened.

A service door loaded.

Return compensation moved.

The shared-loop estimator stayed below entry threshold.

The control-state process locked the result.

NO STATE ENTRY.

Only then did the participant rail open.

P1 — NO EVENT.

P2 — MANUAL EVENT.

P3 — NO EVENT.

P4 — NO EVENT.

P5 — NO EVENT.

P6 — NO EVENT.

Ollie stared at P2.

The event occurred four seconds after the service transition began.

No candidate state.

No later anomaly.

He opened the run note.

The participant had marked the event voluntarily.

No explanation attached.

No identity.

No diagnosis.

Ollie said, “False positive.”

Mara said, “Against what?”

“The candidate state.”

“Then write that.”

Ollie changed the label.

MANUAL EVENT WITHOUT CANDIDATE STATE.

“That is longer.”

“Yes.”

“Less useful?”

“No.”

“Good.”

The control engineer reset the rig.

P4 withdrew from the next run because their shift window had moved again.

The slot disappeared from the participant rail.

Ollie looked at the missing line.

“Will they return for C1?”

“Unknown,” the coordinator said.

“Does that break balance?”

“No. This is not a balanced diagnostic trial.”

“I know.”

“Your face disagrees.”

Everyone was learning that phrase.

* * *

C1 used the same safe residual-return window that had reproduced shared-loop entry in Phase One.

This time the participants were present before the control sequence began.

The control team ran the return correction.

Formal controller closure appeared.

Physical return remained three point two percent from pre-run baseline.

Inside the registered window.

The service load opened.

The control-state estimator moved.

Ollie could see the control traces.

He could not see participant markers.

The state-entry process locked:

ENTRY WINDOW: T+6.4 TO T+7.2.

Fixed display update followed at T+10.9.

Then the participant rail opened.

P1 — MANUAL EVENT T+8.1.

P2 — NO EVENT.

P3 — SUPPORT EVENT CLASS T+8.7.

P5 — NO EVENT.

P6 — MANUAL EVENT T+11.3.

Ollie leaned closer.

Two events sat cleanly between estimated state entry and fixed display.

One came after fixed display.

Two participants marked nothing.

He felt the pattern trying to become a story faster than the data deserved.

Mara said, “Interpret.”

“Two independent coarse events occurred after locked state-entry window and before fixed display.”

“And?”

“One event occurred after fixed display.”

“And?”

“Two participating slots reported nothing.”

“And?”

Ollie looked at her.

“You are going to keep saying and.”

“Yes.”

“It supports possible corroborating lead timing.”

“Necessary?”

“No.”

“Sufficient?”

“No.”

“Good.”

The coordinator released participants for a five-minute break.

P3 asked whether their support event meant anything about their body.

The coordinator answered through the private participant channel.

Ollie could see only the response category:

NO PERSONAL INTERPRETATION PROVIDED.

He did not get the question text.

That annoyed him until he realized the annoyance itself was the point.

The test did not make him entitled to a person.

* * *

C2 was the run Ollie expected to be cleaner.

The residual return state entered at nearly the same percentage as C1.

A different permitted service path loaded the same adaptive return pair.

The control-state estimator locked a candidate entry window.

Fixed display updated four point three seconds later.

The participant rail opened.

P1 — NO EVENT.

P2 — NO EVENT.

P3 — NO EVENT.

P4 — RETURNED / NO EVENT.

P5 — NO EVENT.

P6 — MANUAL EVENT AFTER FIXED DISPLAY.

Ollie read it twice.

Candidate state.

No early human/support event.

One late manual marker.

The pattern he had wanted from C1 disappeared.

His first instinct was to compare participant composition.

P4 had returned.

Same slots except one reporting-mode change.

He opened the participation metadata.

The only visible fields were channel type and whether the participant had completed the run.

No body type.

No support category.

No age.

No identity.

He could not build the subgroup analysis he was thinking about.

Mara watched him.

“You are looking for who changed.”

“I am looking for reporting-mode differences.”

“Are you?”

Ollie stopped.

“Both.”

“What question were you authorized to answer?”

“Whether coarse independent event timing adds useful evidence to a control-defined candidate state.”

“Then answer that before inventing a better population.”

Ollie closed the participation panel.

He wrote:

C2 — CANDIDATE STATE WITHOUT PRE-FIXED PARTICIPANT EVENT CLUSTER.

Then underneath:

EARLY HUMAN/SUPPORT EVENT NOT NECESSARY FOR CANDIDATE STATE.

He did not like the sentence.

That was becoming less important.

* * *

The second negative control happened after lunch.

The volunteers had changed again.

P5 left for class.

P2 returned late from food and missed the run.

Four slots remained.

N2 began from full physical baseline and used a longer ordinary door hold than N1, still outside the shared-loop candidate envelope.

The control-state process locked:

NO CANDIDATE ENTRY.

Participant rail:

P1 — MANUAL EVENT.

P3 — SUPPORT EVENT CLASS.

P4 — NO EVENT.

P6 — NO EVENT.

Ollie stared.

Two independent events.

No candidate state.

If he had seen the participant rail first, he would have expected something in the control system.

Nothing was there.

He opened the fixed trace.

Normal compensation.

No later coupled response.

No abnormal display delay.

He said, “This is bad.”

The participant coordinator looked over.

“For whom?”

“For the signature.”

“Then good test.”

Ollie hated adults who said things like that when they were right.

Mara said, “What does N2 rule out?”

“A participant-event cluster by itself cannot define shared-loop state.”

“Operationally?”

“We cannot use human/support events alone as a trip condition.”

“Was that ever the design?”

“No.”

“Then what did you learn?”

“That the false-positive rate against control state may be nontrivial.”

“May.”

“May be.”

He added N2 to the main result rail.

Not an appendix.

P1 and P3 remained in the test as anonymous events without being labeled wrong.

Ollie looked at the word accuracy in his deleted-history list.

He cleared the recovery entry permanently.

* * *

The final candidate run nearly lost its slot because one volunteer needed to leave.

The coordinator asked whether the participant wanted to withdraw from the run or leave after it.

The answer came back:

WITHDRAW NOW.

The slot disappeared.

Ollie did not ask why.

Three months earlier he would have wanted to know whether the reason mattered to data quality.

It still mattered to data quality.

It did not become his right to know.

C3 began with five participating slots.

Residual return window valid.

Alternate service load.

Shared-loop state estimator rose.

ENTRY WINDOW LOCKED: T+5.9 TO T+6.8.

Fixed display updated at T+10.1.

Participant rail opened.

P1 — NO EVENT.

P2 — MANUAL EVENT T+7.0.

P3 — SUPPORT EVENT CLASS T+7.6.

P4 — MANUAL EVENT T+8.4.

P6 — NO EVENT.

Three independent coarse events before fixed display.

Two no-events.

Ollie looked at C1, C2, N2 and C3 together.

C1: candidate state plus two early events.

C2: candidate state, no early event cluster.

N2: no candidate state, two event markers.

C3: candidate state plus three early events.

It was not clean enough to become a slogan.

That helped.

The control engineer ended the run.

No dangerous oscillation.

No abort.

The participants were released.

One asked whether they would receive their personal timing results.

The coordinator said individual timing could be returned only as the participant’s own raw event record if the protocol allowed it, not as a diagnosis or ranking against others.

Another asked whether they had been early.

The answer was the same.

No performance ranking.

Ollie listened without joining the conversation.

He no longer had an accuracy column to fill.

* * *

The debrief used aggregates.

Six anonymous slots across the session.

Variable participation by run.

Two negative controls.

Three candidate-state runs.

No dangerous event.

No raw personal telemetry.

Mara put the locked state-entry windows on the wall first.

Only after that did she overlay the coarse event rails.

The room could see the mess.

The participant-event rate was higher in the pre-fixed window for some candidate runs.

Not all.

Control-run event clusters existed.

No-event candidate runs existed.

The intervals varied.

The test engineer said, “So what do you want to do with it?”

Ollie opened his draft summary.

His first version said:

PARTICIPANT EARLY-EVENT RELIABILITY — MIXED.

He deleted the line.

Replaced it with:

**COARSE HUMAN/SUPPORT EVENTS ARE NEITHER NECESSARY NOR SUFFICIENT FOR CANDIDATE SHARED-LOOP STATE.**

Then:

**WHEN CONTROL-DEFINED CANDIDATE STATE IS PRESENT, A CLUSTER OF INDEPENDENT COARSE EVENTS MAY ADD CORROBORATING EARLY EVIDENCE BEFORE FIXED DISPLAY UPDATE.**

Mara read it.

“May.”

“Yes.”

“Operational use?”

“Not yet.”

“Why?”

“False positives in controls. Candidate state without reports. Small sample. Reporting modes differ. We do not know dangerous escalation condition.”

The coordinator added, “And people are not obligated to keep participating because your estimator wants continuity.”

“Yes.”

The test engineer said, “What would you validate next?”

Ollie looked at Ch28’s two bad options in his head.

Not the chapter title.

The actual problem.

“If Ring Six reopens under a conservative physical-recovery interlock, run the state estimator and any consented coarse event channels in shadow mode. No control authority. Measure how often the combined signature appears, how often it appears without later fixed response, and how often fixed response occurs without it.”

Mara said, “And if people decline the event channel?”

“Then the system runs without that evidence stream. It cannot require a person to become instrumentation.”

The participant coordinator looked at him.

“That sentence can stay.”

Ollie immediately wanted to rewrite it.

He did not.

Mara opened a new status line.

PHASE 2 — COMPLETED / MIXED TIMING RESULT.

CANDIDATE EARLY SIGNATURE — ACCEPTED FOR SHADOW VALIDATION DESIGN ONLY.

NO OPERATIONAL TRIP AUTHORITY.

RING SIX OCCUPANCY — SUSPENDED PENDING OPERATING-PROFILE DECISION.

Ollie read the last two lines.

Not back.

Not solved.

Still useful.

He could live with that for the length of the corridor, at least.

* * *

Iri was outside the rehearsal volume when Ollie found them later.

They were carrying the percussion frame with the repaired clamp installed.

“How was the human experiment?” Iri asked.

“Do not call it that.”

“That bad?”

“Mixed.”

Iri shifted the frame to the other hand.

“Meaning?”

“Sometimes coarse events led fixed display after control-defined state entry. Sometimes nobody reported anything. One control run had two reports without candidate state.”

Iri nodded.

“You look happier than that result sounds.”

“It means we cannot treat reports as a detector.”

“You wanted to?”

“No.”

Iri looked at him.

Ollie corrected.

“I wanted them to be cleaner.”

“That sounds more true.”

“We can use them as corroboration in shadow mode if the control state is already present.”

“Maybe.”

“Yes.”

Iri smiled.

“You said it first.”

“Do not make this unbearable.”

“I have rehearsal.”

“You just finished rehearsal.”

“I have food.”

“That is not rehearsal.”

“It is more important.”

Iri started walking.

Ollie went with them for one junction.

He did not ask Iri to volunteer for the next phase.

There was no next volunteer phase yet.

That felt like progress too.
