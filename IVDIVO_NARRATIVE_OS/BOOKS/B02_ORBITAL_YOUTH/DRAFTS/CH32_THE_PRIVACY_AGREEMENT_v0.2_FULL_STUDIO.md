# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 32 — THE PRIVACY AGREEMENT

Maya had rehearsed the apology badly enough that Lea noticed before she sat down.

“You have a speech.”

“I do not.”

“You have your speech shoulders.”

Maya looked at her shoulders.

“They are normal.”

“They are higher.”

“That is posture.”

“That is guilt with posture.”

Lea went back to sorting relocation tags.

They were sitting on the floor outside a temporary storage room because every table nearby had become useful to somebody else. Half the corridor was filled with sealed household boxes waiting for the next Ring Six migration window. The other half was filled with people pretending not to look at the boxes.

Maya put her hands flat on her knees.

Lea looked at them.

“Now you have apology hands.”

“Can you stop making this difficult?”

“I’m labeling kitchen equipment.”

“That is not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Maya had planned to begin with the route closure.

That was strategic.

The route closure was concrete.

Then the host call.

Then the part where Lea had to answer questions based on information Maya had deliberately kept incomplete.

Then the apology.

The order made sense.

Which was probably why it was wrong.

She said, “I used you.”

Lea stopped writing.

Maya kept going before the silence could improve her sentence.

“When I changed where I was sleeping and didn’t tell my host, I made you the person who knew. Then when the route closed, everyone planned around what I had said instead of where I was. You had to answer for something I decided.”

Lea looked at the tag in her hand.

“You also told me it would not become my problem.”

“Yes.”

“It became my problem.”

“Yes.”

“You said you weren’t asking me to lie.”

“I wasn’t.”

Lea looked up.

Maya winced.

“Okay. That sentence sounds worse now.”

“It sounded bad then.”

“I know.”

“No, you know now.”

Maya almost argued.

Stopped.

Lea went back to the label.

The storage tag read:

KITCHEN / TEMP HOLD / DO NOT OPEN

Maya said, “I’m sorry.”

Lea nodded once and went back to the label. Maya had wanted something warmer and did not get it.

“I’m not apologizing for wanting privacy.”

“I know.”

“Or for going out.”

“I know.”

“Or for not wanting somebody watching a dot move around Vela all night.”

“I know.”

“Can you let me finish one sentence?”

“You have finished several.”

Maya looked away.

People moved through the corridor carrying ordinary parts of relocation: bedding, chargers, school bags, folded chairs, one plant sealed into a transparent transport sleeve.

She tried again.

“I’m apologizing because I wanted the freedom and made you carry the part I didn’t want.”

Lea put the pen down.

“That is closer.”

“Closer to what?”

“A real apology.”

Maya leaned back against the wall.

“Do I get points?”

“No.”

“Nothing?”

“No.”

“Harsh.”

Lea picked up another tag.

Maya watched her write.

“So are we okay?”

Lea did not look up.

“We are sitting here.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is an answer.”

“You are impossible.”

“You picked me.”

“For what?”

“To be the person who knew.”

Maya stopped.

Lea finished the tag.

Then said, “I’m not doing that again.”

Maya felt the reply before she decided on it.

“I’m not asking you to.”

“Good.”

“What if I tell you something as a friend?”

“That depends.”

“On?”

“Whether it turns into a job.”

“That is vague.”

“You like vague.”

“I like privacy.”

“You liked false information.”

Maya stared.

Lea met her eyes.

“That was mean,” Lea said.

“Yes.”

“I’m still angry.”

“I noticed.”

They sat with that.

The silence did not repair anything for her. Maya wanted to ask whether they were fine and stopped herself.

After a while, Lea handed her a stack of blank tags.

“You can do bathrooms.”

Maya looked at them.

“This is forgiveness?”

“This is handwriting.”

“I have terrible handwriting.”

“I know.”

Maya took the tags.

That was all she got.

It was enough to keep sitting there.

* * *

The host meeting was worse because nobody was angry.

Maya’s host had made tea.

This felt manipulative.

Maya said so.

“It is hot water and leaves.”

“Exactly.”

“You requested the meeting.”

“I requested a conversation.”

“You put it in the calendar.”

“That is how time works.”

Maya sat at the kitchen counter.

Her host sat opposite.

There was no tablet or prepared form between them.

Maya had brought one anyway.

It was only a list.

She opened it.

Her host saw.

“You made rules.”

“Conditions.”

“That word is more dangerous.”

“They are mine.”

“Continue.”

Maya read the first line.

“Truthful zone.”

Her host waited.

“Not exact position.”

“Agreed.”

“Truthful host or reachable contact if I’m sleeping somewhere else.”

“Agreed.”

“Return condition.”

“Define.”

“If I say I am coming back here, I give the last route or time condition that still makes that possible.”

“That is not a sentence.”

“It is if you understand transit.”

“Try again.”

Maya exhaled.

“If I’m coming home, I tell you what has to remain true for that plan to still work. Like: first late ringline, or South Transfer open, or I leave by twelve forty.”

“Better.”

Maya ignored the word.

“If the route or environmental condition changes enough that the plan no longer works, I update.”

“Before or after you improvise?”

Maya stopped.

Her host waited.

“That is hostile.”

“It is the part you failed last time.”

“Before if I can.”

“If you cannot?”

“As soon as it is safe to stop.”

“What counts as safe?”

“Not while crossing. Not while moving through a transfer. Not while somebody is yelling at me to keep the lane clear.”

“Reasonable.”

Maya moved to the next line.

“No continuous location stream.”

“Agreed.”

“No passive tracker.”

“Agreed.”

“No automatic host access to my route history.”

Her host paused.

Maya saw it.

“No.”

“I did not say no.”

“You made the face.”

“What face?”

“The adult version of loading.”

“I was thinking.”

“Same thing.”

Her host smiled despite trying not to.

“I do not need your route history by default.”

“Good.”

“But if there is an actual emergency and transit support needs the last validated point—”

“That is different.”

“How?”

“Emergency access is not the same as you watching me because I am seventeen.”

“Good distinction.”

Maya looked at the list.

“That sounded like praise.”

“It was not.”

“Everyone here does that.”

Her host tapped one finger on the counter.

“What if your plan changes twice?”

Maya had prepared for this.

“I update twice.”

“What if it changes five times?”

“I become annoying.”

“You are already annoying.”

“Then there is no additional cost.”

“Maya.”

“I update the condition, not ask permission.”

Her host considered.

“That is the part I want clear.”

Maya waited.

“If you move from one safe registered host plan to another safe registered host plan, I do not need to approve it in real time.”

Maya relaxed.

Too obviously.

Her host saw.

“But I need truthful information about which plan is current.”

“Yes.”

“And if the route environment changes in a way that makes your plan unsafe?”

“I stop treating it as a privacy argument.”

“Meaning?”

“I follow transit or host safety instruction.”

“And message.”

“If messaging is possible.”

Her host nodded.

Maya looked at the remaining item.

It was the one she had almost removed.

“If I fail the agreement?”

“What happens?”

“No automatic tracker.”

Her host said nothing.

“That has to be explicit.”

“It is.”

“No, say it.”

“Maya—”

“Please.”

Her host leaned back.

“If you give false location or host information again, we reassess late mobility. We do not automatically switch to continuous tracking.”

Maya watched her.

“Okay.”

“Depending on what happened, late mobility may become narrower.”

“Temporary?”

“Probably.”

“That is not a contract.”

“This is not a contract.”

“It is a protocol.”

“It is a household agreement.”

“Same thing with worse documentation.”

Her host almost laughed.

Then did not.

“One more condition.”

Maya narrowed her eyes.

“What?”

“You do not make another teenager responsible for maintaining a false version of your plan.”

Maya looked down at her list.

She had not written that.

“Okay.”

“Say it.”

“I do not use Lea as backup reality.”

Her host blinked.

“That is not how I would phrase it.”

“It is how I would.”

“Fine.”

Maya added it.

NO BACKUP REALITY.

Her host looked at the words.

“That is ridiculous.”

“It is memorable.”

“It is.”

Maya saved the list.

“So?”

“So we try it.”

“For how long?”

“Two weeks.”

“Why two?”

“Because one night proves nothing.”

“That is insulting.”

“It is a trial.”

“And after?”

“We argue again with evidence.”

Maya considered.

“That is worse than a permanent rule.”

“It is more honest.”

“I know teenagers.”

“Worse.”

Her host lifted the cup.

“Relocation support needs late-route volunteers tomorrow. Your current agreement would allow you to take one of the evening windows.”

Maya stopped.

“That was fast.”

“You wanted freedom that works.”

“I did.”

“Use it.”

Maya looked at the saved conditions.

Truthful zone.

Truthful host/contact.

Return condition.

Update if route/environment changes.

No continuous stream.

No backup reality.

It looked irritating.

Which was probably why it might survive contact with her actual life.

* * *

The first test of the agreement was so boring Maya nearly forgot it counted.

Relocation support ended late because one family had packed every charger into the same sealed box and then needed one of them.

Maya spent twelve minutes finding the correct container.

Then seven more explaining why opening three other containers would not make the correct one appear faster.

By the time she left the support corridor, her original plan had already changed.

She had told her host:

ZONE: CENTRAL EAST
HOST: HOME
RETURN CONDITION: SOUTH RINGLINE 00:42 OR EARLIER

At 00:09, South Ringline posted a service delay.

It was a delay rather than a closure.

Estimated twelve minutes.

Maya stood under the transit display with two relocation volunteers and looked at the revised route.

She could still get home.

Probably.

Or she could go with Lea to a registered youth host space in North Central, sleep there, and help with the 07:00 move window without crossing half the city again.

Lea looked at her.

“I am not covering anything.”

“Do not involve me.”

“I have said nothing.”

“You made the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you are about to make your logistics my ethics.”

Maya looked at the route again.

Then opened her host thread.

For three seconds she felt the old resistance.

The plan was safe.

Lea was there.

The host space was registered.

Nobody needed to know every move.

Then she read her own agreement.

Truthful current plan.

That was all.

She typed:

PLAN CHANGE.
ZONE: NORTH CENTRAL.
HOST: SERA HOUSEHOLD / REGISTERED.
RETURN CONDITION: staying overnight; leaving for relocation support 06:35.
SOUTH RINGLINE delay made home-return inefficient, not unsafe.
No help needed.

Her thumb hovered.

Lea watched.

Maya said, “Stop.”

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You’re watching.”

“You made me part of this once. I’m enjoying the sequel.”

Maya sent the message.

The reply came less than a minute later.

HOST: RECEIVED. GOOD NIGHT. MESSAGE IF HOST/ROUTE CONDITION CHANGES.

The reply asked for nothing else.

Maya stared at the thread.

Lea said, “Terrible?”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

“Nothing.”

“Tragic.”

They walked toward North Central.

Halfway there, one transfer lane closed for floor maintenance.

The alternate route added four minutes.

Maya looked at the map.

Lea looked at her.

“No.”

“What?”

“You do not need to update four minutes.”

Maya frowned.

“How do you know?”

“Because your host does not care about your emotional relationship with every corridor.”

“That is not in the agreement.”

“Your condition is still true. Same zone. Same host. Same overnight plan.”

Maya looked again.

Lea was right.

“That feels dangerously like judgment.”

“It is called reading.”

They continued.

At the host entrance, Maya sent one final message.

ARRIVED. PLAN UNCHANGED.

Her host replied with a single check mark.

Maya hated how satisfying that was.

Sera opened the door wearing one sock.

“Why are you both smiling?”

“We are not,” Maya said.

Lea said, “Maya successfully transmitted information.”

Sera looked impressed.

“Should we celebrate?”

“No.”

“Do we have cake?”

“No.”

“Then definitely no.”

They went inside.

Nobody discussed privacy again.

They argued for fifteen minutes about whether the early relocation shift justified eating leftovers marked for morning.

Maya lost.

At 06:31, her alarm went off.

She checked the route.

The connector was slower than predicted but remained open and safe.

The plan still worked.

She did not send anything.

She put the band away and kept walking.

* * *

At 06:52 Maya stood in the migration support bay with a route board clipped to her sleeve.

Nadia Park looked at it.

“You are early.”

“Do not make this a character moment.”

“I was checking a clock.”

“Good.”

Nadia pointed toward the first household staging area.

“We need one person on late-route substitution.”

Maya looked at the route board.

“What does that mean?”

“People whose planned transfer changes after they have already left temporary housing.”

“That sounds familiar.”

“Yes.”

Maya looked at Nadia.

Nadia’s expression remained neutral.

“Was that intentional?”

“What?”

“Giving me this.”

“You know the late routes.”

“So do other people.”

“You also know what happens when the plan in the system and the plan in a person’s head stop matching.”

Maya looked at the route board instead of answering.

Nadia handed her a narrow task tab.

“You do not decide housing.”

“I know.”

“You do not improvise safety closure.”

“I know.”

“You do not tell people a route is open because you think it should be.”

“I know.”

“You confirm current destination, current host/receiver, and current route condition. If one changes, you update the chain.”

Maya looked at the tab.

ZONE.

RECEIVING CONTACT.

ROUTE CONDITION.

She laughed.

Nadia frowned.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That is rarely true.”

The fields on Nadia's task tab looked uncomfortably familiar: zone, receiving contact, route condition. Maya clipped it to her sleeve anyway.

The first family arrived with two bags too many and one child who refused to let go of a broken toy.

Maya checked the route.

Then looked at the people.

“Okay,” she said. “Where are you actually going?”

The migration day began.
