# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 28 — TWO BAD OPTIONS

Ethan was asked to map the consequences.

He heard: find the answer.

Nobody had said that.

The task arrived at 07:16 while he was eating breakfast and trying to stop a piece of fruit from rolling away every time the household floor shifted through its morning gravity adjustment.

RING SIX — OPERATIONAL PATH CONSEQUENCE MAP.

REQUESTED OUTPUT:

OPTION A — FULL-RECOVERY INTERLOCK.

OPTION B — STATE-AWARE CONTROL DEVELOPMENT.

DEPENDENCIES / COSTS / UNKNOWNS / AFFECTED GROUPS.

NO RECOMMENDATION REQUESTED.

The fruit rolled again.

Ethan trapped it under one finger and opened a workspace called:

R6 — OPTION COMPARISON.

He did not add recommendation.

Four minutes later he had built a scoring layer anyway.

He deleted it before leaving for the civic work volume.

On the transfer, he restored it from version history.

Not for submission.

Just so the comparison would mean something.

That was the explanation he used until the doors opened.

* * *

Nadia Park had already covered one wall with household blocks.

Each block showed only what the decision needed: temporary placement, room compatibility, school and care routes, work overlap, transfer burden.

From across the room they looked manageable.

Ethan opened the first one.

A parent’s early shift crossed the child’s temporary school route by eleven minutes.

The second had two adults sleeping in different sectors because only one unit could support a household member’s environmental requirement.

The third was waiting for a room close enough to a care transfer that the care transfer would remain possible after an overnight shift.

Nadia watched Ethan stop moving blocks.

“That is the housing side.”

“Current eligibility order?”

“Current. Change the operating profile and the order changes.”

Ethan looked at the wall again.

“That is worse.”

“For your map?”

“Yes.”

Nadia handed him the engineering release from Ch27.

Only the bounded summary. No raw incident data, no private support records.

Option A first.

FULL-RECOVERY INTERLOCK.

Certain service transitions could not begin until the physical return network reached a deeper recovery threshold.

Technically simple.

Operationally expensive.

Ethan connected A to the first movement window.

A service cart waited longer.

That delayed a maintenance crossing.

The crossing shifted a residential route.

The route collided with a school transfer.

Ethan moved the school block to clear it.

Nadia reached over and moved it back.

“What?”

“That child has a school.”

“I was showing the downstream effect.”

“You were fixing the downstream effect.”

Ethan looked at the wall.

He had been.

“Fine. Leave the conflict visible.”

“That is the assignment.”

Under A, the first plausible occupied profile could be validated sooner because it relied less on a state estimator that was still unproven in occupied operation.

But Ring Six would not initially run as designed.

Capacity fell.

Peak movement lost flexibility.

Some service transitions became scheduled waits.

Some households still fit.

Some did not.

Nadia opened Lea’s household block.

The family’s temporary arrangement remained split across incompatible routes.

Under the reduced A profile they might fit in an early return group.

Might.

Ethan started to open a probability field.

Nadia stopped him with one finger on the screen.

“Do not.”

“I need to represent likelihood.”

“You need to represent eligibility. Allocation depends on who else fits the same capacity.”

“So the queue matters.”

“Of course the queue matters.”

Ethan changed the note.

LEA HOUSEHOLD — EARLY ELIGIBILITY POSSIBLE / NOT GUARANTEED.

He disliked how little that promised.

That was probably why it was honest.

Option B.

STATE-AWARE CONTROL DEVELOPMENT.

Ch27 had shown that a controller might eventually recognize residual recovery state and block only combinations entering the candidate shared-loop window.

If it worked, Ring Six retained more of the flexibility it had been designed for.

If it worked.

Ethan entered the phrase once and refused to let himself hide it in a footnote.

B needed more validation.

Repeated runs.

False-positive measurement.

And the harder problem: they still did not have a complete definition of the dangerous escalation condition, which meant false-negative performance could not yet be validated against the thing everyone cared about most.

He wrote:

VALIDATION TIME — LONGER THAN A / END DATE UNKNOWN.

Nadia read it over his shoulder.

“Housing will hate that.”

“You mean you.”

“I mean the people still living out of storage boxes. I am included.”

Under B, Ring Six remained shut longer.

Temporary housing continued.

School transfers continued.

Service staff continued carrying temporary routing load.

If B passed, more households could eventually return under something closer to the intended operating profile.

If B failed, they would have spent the extra validation time and still need A or another design.

Ethan stepped back.

Two bad options.

At last, something that wanted a comparison.

He reopened the private scoring layer.

SAFETY MARGIN.
HOUSING DISPLACEMENT.
SERVICE DISRUPTION.
ACCESS IMPACT.
VALIDATION UNCERTAINTY.
REVERSIBILITY.

Nadia saw the panel.

“You brought it back.”

“I am not submitting it.”

“That makes me feel much safer.”

“A consequence map without any way to compare consequences—”

“Is a consequence map.”

Ethan looked at the wall full of households.

Then at his blank weights.

He did not close them.

* * *

The affected-use review happened in a room designed for forty people and occupied by nine.

That helped.

No stage. No audience seating. One wall for Ethan’s consequence map, one for the temporary housing routes.

Tomas sat near the service layer with his left forearm still braced.

Lea arrived with her mother, who stayed through the opening and then checked the time twice before saying she had to leave for her younger child’s temporary school transfer.

An older resident from another waiting household sat nearest the door because their care connection left before the meeting officially ended.

Nobody had time to represent an abstract constituency.

The facilitator pointed at Ethan.

“Consequences. Not recommendation.”

He nodded.

Option A first.

“Certain transitions wait for deeper physical recovery. That gives us a simpler path to an occupied profile sooner, but at reduced flexibility and capacity.”

The older resident raised a hand before Ethan could show the next slide.

“Sooner for whom?”

Ethan opened the compatibility layer.

“Allocation is not fixed until the profile is fixed.”

The resident looked at the wall.

Their household class sat outside the first simulated A capacity band.

“So someone else goes home sooner.”

Nadia answered before Ethan could improve the sentence.

“Under this version, yes.”

The resident pointed at Ethan’s heading.

“Then when you say faster, put that beside it.”

Ethan added:

FASTER REOPENING ≠ EQUAL RETURN DATE.

Lea was already staring at her household block.

“Are we in?”

Nadia shook her head.

“Maybe. The profile fits your household better than some of the current temporary arrangements, but allocation depends on the final capacity set.”

“That was a lot of words for maybe.”

Ethan changed his note before she asked.

LEA HOUSEHOLD — MAYBE EARLY / NOT PROMISED.

Lea read it.

“Ugly.”

“Accurate.”

“I can live with ugly.”

Tomas tapped the service layer with one finger.

“What did you assume for maintenance windows?”

Ethan showed him.

Tomas shook his head.

“We lose those two.”

“Why?”

“Because if a service crossing has to wait for deep recovery, something else gets pushed. Those two are easiest to move without immediate failure.”

“That defers maintenance.”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Depends what else breaks.”

“That is not useful for modeling.”

Tomas looked at the brace on his arm, then back at Ethan.

“Neither was this.”

Ethan stopped.

Tomas pointed at the service map.

“Put deferred maintenance on it. Do not invent the duration.”

Ethan did.

Option B.

“Longer validation before occupied use. If the state-aware controller proves reliable, more intended capacity and flexibility remain available. But it currently recognizes a reproduced candidate state, not the complete dangerous condition.”

The older resident frowned.

“So it is smarter and you trust it less.”

Mara, standing beside the engineering summary, answered.

“It is more selective. It also needs more evidence before it can carry occupied safety authority.”

“That is a nicer sentence.”

“It is the sentence I can defend.”

Ethan moved to the uncertainty block.

“We do not yet have validated false-negative performance against the dangerous escalation condition because that condition is not fully defined.”

Lea stared at him.

“Normal language.”

Ethan tried again.

“We know one state we can reproduce. We do not know whether it is the only state that can become dangerous.”

“Thank you.”

Tomas asked how long B left current service reassignment in place.

Ethan gave the range they actually had.

Tomas corrected one staffing assumption because a worker reassigned that morning was due back the next day if medical clearance held.

Ethan changed the wall.

The map got less tidy and more current.

Then he made the mistake of offering the hybrid.

“What if A operates as an interim profile while B validates in parallel?”

For half a second it felt like the answer.

Reopen some capacity sooner.

Keep the higher-capacity control development moving.

Transition later.

Lea said, “Move twice.”

Ethan looked at her.

“If we get a room under A and B changes allocation later,” she said, “do we move twice?”

Nadia answered, “Some households could.”

The older resident leaned forward.

“And some of us wait through A and then keep waiting.”

Tomas was already looking at the staffing layer.

“The same test and maintenance people do work for both.”

Mara added one sentence.

“Parallel is a resource claim, not free time.”

Ethan opened a transition-cost layer.

The facilitator said his name.

He kept typing for two seconds too long.

“Ethan.”

He stopped.

“We need the consequences of the two current directions today.”

“The hybrid is a sequence, not—”

Nadia covered her eyes briefly.

Lea laughed.

Ethan closed the layer.

“Fine.”

“Thank you.”

He left the hybrid in notes with MOVE-TWICE / STAFF-COMPETITION / TRANSITION COST attached to it.

It no longer looked like a free third answer.

* * *

The facilitator did not ask everyone to rank the same list.

Instead she asked a simpler question.

“What makes an option unacceptable for you?”

Lea answered first.

“Another move after we finally get in.”

Then, after a moment, “And the school route. My brother is already leaving before anyone should have to be awake.”

The older resident tapped the access icon on their household block.

“A room we cannot actually use is not a room.”

Tomas pointed at the service layer.

“Do not reopen Ring Six by quietly borrowing reliability from older rings until nobody notices.”

Nadia looked at the temporary housing wall.

“I cannot give you a housing number that turns unknown safety failure into an acceptable exchange.”

Ethan was already translating each statement into categories.

RELOCATION BURDEN.
ACCESS COMPATIBILITY.
DEFERRED MAINTENANCE.
SAFETY UNCERTAINTY.

The words fit beautifully.

Too beautifully.

He opened his private scoring layer under the table.

The first pass favored B.

Not by much.

Enough to make the room feel solvable again.

Safety uncertainty had the largest penalty. Housing displacement had twelve. Service disruption nine. Access impact eight. Validation time seven. Reversibility changed the totals by scenario.

He ran a sensitivity pass.

B remained ahead under most of the weights he considered reasonable.

Reasonable according to Ethan.

He was adjusting housing from twelve to fourteen when Mara said, “What are you doing?”

Ethan looked up.

The private layer had mirrored to the wall.

He had connected the workspace during the review and never disabled secondary display.

CURRENT PREFERENCE — B / MODERATE CONFIDENCE.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Lea stood and walked closer.

“Why is housing twelve?”

“It is a relative weight.”

“I can see that. Why twelve?”

Ethan opened the definition automatically.

“Temporary household-days, route burden, school disruption—”

“Why twelve?”

He stopped.

“Because I set the reference value.”

Lea nodded once.

“So you picked it.”

“Yes.”

Mara pointed at SAFETY UNCERTAINTY.

“And that one?”

“I gave unknown false-negative risk the largest penalty because it is the least bounded.”

“That explains why it worries you. It does not tell you the exchange rate against thirty households waiting.”

Nadia did not point at a number. She pointed at the household wall.

“Your displacement score goes down if fewer total people wait.”

“Yes.”

“What if the same access-needs households are always the ones left behind?”

Ethan opened his mouth.

Distribution constraints.

Sensitivity bands.

Scenario weighting.

He could improve the score.

That was the problem.

He could keep improving it until the choice disappeared inside the machine.

Ethan shut the layer off.

The wall returned to A, B and the consequence blocks.

Lea said, “That does not answer why twelve.”

“No.”

“So?”

“So twelve does not go in the packet.”

“Because we caught it?”

That hurt more than he wanted it to.

“Because I can map what changes. I cannot pretend those weights came from engineering.”

Mara said nothing.

Ethan looked at the blank space where CURRENT PREFERENCE had been.

“I can show sensitivity if the board asks what different priorities do to the map. But the first priorities do not belong to me.”

The facilitator nodded toward the consequence wall.

“Then finish that.”

No praise.

Better.

Ethan reopened only the consequence map.

A no longer turned red.

B no longer turned green.

He attached the statements to the people and systems that had actually produced them.

LEA HOUSEHOLD — RETURN DATE / SCHOOL ROUTE / DO NOT MAKE US MOVE TWICE.

OTHER HOUSEHOLD — ACCESS COMPATIBILITY / PREDICTABILITY / RETURN DATE.

SERVICE — STAFF LOAD / DEFERRED MAINTENANCE / OLDER-RING DEPENDENCIES.

HOUSING — NUMBER STILL DISPLACED / SPLIT HOUSEHOLDS / ALLOCATION FAIRNESS.

SAFETY — EVIDENCE CEILING / REVERSIBILITY / UNKNOWN ESCALATION CONDITION.

Lea read her line.

“Leave that exactly like that.”

“Do not make us move twice?”

“Yes.”

“It is not parallel with the other labels.”

“Survive.”

Ethan left it.

* * *

The review ended without choosing A or B.

Ethan found that deeply unsatisfying.

The facilitator accepted the consequence map into the next commissioning/civic decision packet.

The score was not included.

The hybrid sequence remained only as a longer-term note with explicit transition and staffing costs.

Tomas left first because his light-duty shift restarted in nineteen minutes.

The older resident left for the care transfer they had been watching all meeting.

Lea stayed long enough to read the final wall once more.

“Which one do you think they’ll choose?”

Ethan looked at A.

Then B.

“I don’t know.”

Lea waited as if expecting him to repair the sentence.

He did not.

“That physically hurt you,” she said.

“A little.”

“Excellent.”

She left.

Nadia began clearing the temporary workspace.

Ethan stopped her hand before she removed the affected-priority notes.

“Those go with the packet.”

“They were already going.”

“Right.”

Nadia found the small strip from the private score printout.

CURRENT PREFERENCE — B / MODERATE CONFIDENCE.

She held it out.

“Souvenir?”

Ethan took it, folded it twice and dropped it into recycling.

His band showed three messages from the group.

Maya was still arguing about her return window.

Samir had practice.

Ollie had sent nothing, which was becoming strange enough to count as progress.

Aoife had written:

AOIFE: alive after civic optimization?

Ethan typed:

ETHAN: no optimization

He deleted it.

ETHAN: consequences mapped. no recommendation.

AOIFE: painful?

ETHAN: extremely

Three dots.

AOIFE: food later?

Ethan opened his schedule.

His first reflex was to find a time that worked for all five.

He stopped before running the search.

ETHAN: when are you free?

Aoife sent one time.

It did not work for everyone.

Ethan accepted it anyway.
