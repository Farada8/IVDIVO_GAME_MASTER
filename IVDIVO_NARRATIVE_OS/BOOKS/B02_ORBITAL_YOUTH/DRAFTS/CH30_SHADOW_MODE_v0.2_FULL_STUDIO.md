# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 30 — SHADOW MODE

The incident review ended with a badge.

Not a speech.

Not an apology.

A badge.

Ollie read it in Mara’s office while the review chair finished explaining everything the badge did not mean.

RING SIX — SUPERVISED COMMISSIONING ACCESS.

ANALYSIS / VERIFICATION / ASSIGNED MAINTENANCE.

CONTROL AUTHORITY — NONE.

SHADOW ALERT ACTION — NONE.

PRIVATE PARTICIPANT DATA — NONE.

OPERATING PROFILE CHANGE — NONE.

He looked at the third line again.

The review chair was still talking.

“The panel found no evidence of falsification, unauthorized operation or deliberate suppression of contradictory material.”

Ollie looked up.

“The panel also found your interpretation materially affected the commissioning decision and that your model demoted a class of observations which later proved relevant to incident timing.”

“Yes.”

“The board’s evidence hierarchy did the same.”

“Yes.”

“The opening decision belonged to the board.”

“Yes.”

“Your contribution was valid and incomplete.”

Ollie waited.

The review chair waited too.

Apparently that was the whole sentence.

He had expected more after three weeks of wanting the result.

Mara said, “Do you understand the access?”

“Yes.”

“What can you do if shadow mode marks a candidate state?”

“Observe. Record. Follow assigned procedure. No control action unless someone with authority orders a separate task.”

“What if you are certain it is dangerous?”

“I still do not have control authority.”

“What if I am not in the room?”

“Same answer.”

Mara nodded.

The review chair closed the finding.

Ollie looked at the badge again.

He had wanted Ring Six back.

This was Ring Six with four lines of *no* attached to it.

Mara stood.

“Good.”

Ollie looked at her.

“What?”

“R6-A service check. West return corridor. You have nine panels before first occupied window.”

“That is the first assignment?”

“Yes.”

“After incident review.”

“Yes.”

“Nine panels.”

Mara opened the door.

“Do you want ten?”

“No.”

“Then go.”

That was his reinstatement ceremony.

* * *

Ring Six smelled different after being shut.

Not bad.

Empty.

The air had the flat clean quality of a space that had been maintained without being lived in.

No food.

No damp clothes.

No open household doors changing local temperature.

No child running from a warmer room into a cooler corridor and leaving the door open too long.

No furniture blocking exactly the access line Mara had warned about three times.

Ollie had once wanted the ring to behave like this.

He knew better now.

Iri was already at the third panel.

They saw Ollie’s badge before his face.

“Supervised.”

“Yes.”

“No control.”

“Yes.”

Iri opened the panel.

“No speech?”

“Apparently not.”

“Good.”

That was all.

Ollie had not expected congratulations.

He had expected slightly more visible lack of congratulations.

Iri handed him the meter.

“West return three.”

Ollie took it.

They worked.

Panel three passed.

Panel four had a seal beginning to stiffen from repeated thermal cycles.

Not a failure.

Ticketed for replacement.

Panel five had a resident-facing grille someone had repainted while the ring was closed, and the new coating had dried with a ridge along one edge.

Iri ran a finger near it without touching.

“This will whistle.”

“It is inside tolerance.”

“It will still whistle.”

“That is not a commissioning failure.”

“It becomes your problem at two in the morning.”

Ollie added a service note.

POSSIBLE ACOUSTIC COMPLAINT UNDER NIGHT FLOW — CHECK AFTER OCCUPANCY.

Iri looked at him.

“You wrote it.”

“Yes.”

“No argument.”

“It is measurable after occupancy.”

“Still counts.”

Ollie moved to panel six.

By the ninth panel his knees hurt from crouching and his supervised badge felt less symbolic.

That helped.

Then the first relocation carts arrived.

* * *

Ring Six did not reopen.

R6-A opened under an operating profile so reduced that Nadia refused to call it reopening in front of residents.

“Restricted occupied profile,” she said.

A man carrying two bedding modules said, “That is longer.”

“Yes.”

“Does it mean I can sleep here tonight?”

“Yes.”

“Then I’m calling it open.”

Nadia pointed toward the route marker.

“Call it whatever you want after you keep that cart inside the yellow path.”

He moved the cart.

The new live profile used the conservative recovery interlock from Option A.

Ollie could see its effects without looking at a single engineering display.

A service door opened.

Then waited.

A second service movement that would once have followed immediately held until the relevant return network recovered deeper than the old controller closure required.

People noticed.

One resident noticed in thirty seconds.

“Why is that door still red?”

“Recovery hold,” a service worker said.

“How long?”

“Twenty-three seconds.”

“That is absurd.”

“It is twenty-one now.”

The resident waited, carrying a plant.

The door cleared.

They moved through.

The plant hit the frame anyway.

Ordinary life returned faster than the ring’s elegance.

The first occupied wave was small enough that the corridor still looked underused.

That did not stop it from becoming messy.

A child sat on a sealed storage case because they had decided the room assignment was taking too long.

Two adults argued about which sleeping wall belonged to whom.

A food delivery arrived before the household it belonged to.

The delivery worker left it in a legal holding bay and complained to anyone willing to listen.

A school case was lost for twelve minutes and found under the child who had been sitting on the storage case.

Ollie was checking a return grille when Lea appeared carrying someone else’s lamp.

He recognized the lamp before he understood why she had it.

“You’re moving in?” he asked.

Lea stopped.

“No.”

The answer landed harder than it should have.

She looked at his badge.

“You are.”

“Supervised.”

“Congratulations on supervised.”

The lamp was too wide for the way she was holding it.

Ollie took one end without asking.

“Whose?”

“Imani’s household.”

The name was new to him.

“They got first wave?”

“They fit first wave.”

Lea adjusted her grip.

“My family doesn’t.”

“Because of the room-route combination?”

“Because of the sentence that means we don’t.”

Ollie nodded.

They carried the lamp through the restricted route.

The recovery interlock stopped them at a service crossing.

Twenty-six seconds.

Lea looked at the red indicator.

“This is what we waited for?”

“It is part of what makes this profile acceptable.”

“That was almost a brochure sentence.”

“It was not.”

“It had the tone.”

The crossing cleared.

They continued.

Imani’s household had one room open and three people inside it arguing about shelf height.

A younger child saw the lamp and shouted, “There.”

Everyone else said, “Not there.”

Lea handed over her end.

Ollie did the same.

Imani’s parent thanked both of them and immediately asked Ollie whether the room ventilation could be made less loud at night.

Ollie looked at the grille.

Different grille.

Same problem.

“Maybe,” he said. “I need to hear it under night flow.”

The parent looked disappointed.

“You don’t know?”

“Not yet.”

Lea glanced at him.

Ollie ignored it.

Outside the room, Lea said, “We’re still in temporary Unit Nine.”

“I know.”

“School route changed again.”

“I did not know.”

“My brother’s first transfer is twelve minutes earlier. He is treating this as a human-rights violation.”

“That is a reasonable age-specific interpretation.”

Lea almost laughed.

Then did not.

“When does second wave happen?”

Ollie looked at the supervised badge.

“I don’t make that decision.”

“I know.”

“Nadia said the profile review is after the first occupied data window.”

“That means not today.”

“Yes.”

“Excellent.”

She walked away before he could offer something useless.

Ollie watched her go.

Ring Six had opened sooner.

Lea was still not home.

The consequence looked worse in a corridor than it had on a map.

* * *

Shadow mode started at 14:00.

No light changed for residents.

No route sign mentioned it.

The ring did not know, in any operational sense, that another model was watching.

At the commissioning desk, the shadow layer sat beside the live controller with a thick grey border and a label Mara had insisted on:

ANALYSIS ONLY — NO ACTUATOR / NO TRIP AUTHORITY.

Ollie’s supervised access allowed him to see released shadow states.

Not participant identities.

Not raw personal-support events.

The coarse event rail, where volunteers had chosen to contribute during occupied operation, appeared only as anonymous event times after the consent processor accepted them.

Most resident routes had no human event channel at all.

The ring operated anyway.

The first hour produced nothing.

Ollie checked panels.

Verified a service queue.

Helped a resident understand why a storage wall could not be moved six centimeters without blocking maintenance access.

Lost the argument socially and won it physically because the wall did not move.

At 15:17 the shadow estimator entered the candidate residual/shared-loop envelope.

Ollie saw the grey marker appear.

SHADOW CANDIDATE STATE.

His attention narrowed immediately.

The live controller did nothing.

It was not supposed to.

A service cart had entered a hold while the return network was still recovering inside the new conservative window.

The live interlock blocked the next related transition.

WAIT — PHYSICAL RETURN RECOVERY.

The shadow state persisted.

Ollie looked at the coarse event rail.

Nothing.

Then one anonymous manual event appeared.

E1.

One.

Not a cluster.

Fixed environmental display remained normal.

The live interlock continued waiting.

Residual return imbalance decayed.

The shadow candidate state cleared.

The service transition released.

No anomaly followed.

Ollie watched the traces another thirty seconds.

Nothing.

Mara was two stations away reviewing the first-wave service queue.

Ollie said, “Shadow candidate at fifteen seventeen. One coarse event. No fixed response.”

Mara did not turn immediately.

“Live control?”

“Conservative recovery hold operated normally.”

“Any threshold?”

“No.”

“Then log it.”

Ollie did.

He wanted to call it a false positive.

He stopped.

The shadow state had actually occurred.

The live system had then waited until recovery and nothing escalated.

The early signature had been incomplete.

He labeled it:

LIVE SHADOW CASE 001.

CONTROL-DEFINED CANDIDATE STATE — YES.

COARSE CORROBORATION CLUSTER — NO / ONE EVENT ONLY.

LATER FIXED ANOMALY — NO.

LIVE INTERLOCK — NORMAL RECOVERY HOLD.

OUTCOME — BENIGN UNDER CURRENT PROFILE / CAUSAL INTERPRETATION UNKNOWN.

Mara came over after finishing the queue review.

She read the log.

“You kept it.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Ollie looked at her.

“You are all saying that too often.”

“Then stop doing things worth saying it about.”

“That makes no sense.”

Mara returned to the service queue.

Ollie looked at Shadow Case 001.

The model had noticed something real.

Nothing dangerous had happened.

Both could be true.

* * *

By 17:30 R6-A had twenty-seven arguments, four delayed deliveries, one blocked waste route, two children asleep in rooms their families had spent months waiting for and no incident.

The exact count of arguments came from Nadia, not a system.

She had begun writing them on a small wall as a joke and stopped when residents started adding their own.

SHELF HEIGHT — 6.

SCHOOL ROUTE — 4.

DOOR WAIT — 5.

WHO TOOK MY CUP — 3.

VENT NOISE — 2.

OTHER / PRIVATE / NONE OF YOUR BUSINESS — 7.

Ollie added:

STORAGE WALL SIX CENTIMETERS — 1.

The resident who had argued with him saw it.

“You’re proud of that?”

“No.”

“You wrote it.”

“For completeness.”

“Very planet,” Lea said from behind him.

Ollie turned.

She had come back without the lamp.

“Why are you here?”

“Helping Imani. Then leaving.”

She looked at the argument wall.

“Door wait only five?”

“Residents adapt,” Nadia said.

“They complain while adapting.”

“That also counts.”

Lea added one mark to DOOR WAIT.

“Six.”

“You are not occupied here,” Ollie said.

“I waited at the door.”

“Valid.”

She put the marker back.

Ollie’s band showed his end-of-shift status.

RING SIX — SUPERVISED ACCESS ACTIVE.

No congratulatory message.

No full credential restoration.

One shadow case that had not become a problem.

Twenty-eight arguments now, because Lea had started one about whether the wall categories were insulting.

The ring sounded inhabited again.

Ollie stood in the corridor and listened.

Panel Five had started to whistle.

Iri, walking past with a service pouch, heard it at the same time.

They stopped.

Ollie pointed at the grille.

“I wrote that.”

“You want credit for predicting an annoying sound?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“Supervised credit?”

“Still no.”

Iri opened the panel.

Ollie crouched beside them.

The shadow model continued running without them.
