# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 26 — THE SECOND MODEL

The review packet arrived while Ollie had both hands inside a filter housing.

His band vibrated against his wrist.

He felt it.

He did not pull his hand out.

Iri noticed.

“That was review.”

“Probably.”

“You are not looking.”

“I am holding this seal in place.”

Iri stared at him.

Ollie kept pressure on the seal while the local maintenance worker reset the housing latch.

The latch closed.

The differential settled.

The worker checked the indicator.

“Good. Next one.”

Only then did Ollie wipe his hands and open the notification.

REVIEW-APPROVED COMPARISON RELEASE — PARTICIPANT ACCESS.

No raw Ring Six incident data.

No identities.

No personal-support telemetry.

No private media.

The release contained only the comparison outputs the incident team had cleared for participants whose work was under review.

Ollie read the first line.

Then the second.

Iri was already walking toward the next panel.

“You can read while moving,” Ollie said.

“I can. I am choosing not to.”

“This is relevant.”

“So is the filter you are carrying.”

Ollie looked down.

He was still holding the used filter.

He put it in the service bag and followed.

The next panel was older than the one they had just serviced and had been painted around at least twice without anyone removing the access frame first.

Ollie hated this aesthetically and professionally.

Iri opened the latch.

“What did review send?”

“A new comparison case.”

“From where?”

“De-identified.”

“Good.”

“Movement-order case. Mature transfer. Mixed flow, held transition, return compensation. Fixed correction displayed several seconds after the held/reversed state was already active.”

Iri stopped with one hand on the panel.

“No person report?”

“Not required for the comparison.”

Iri nodded once.

Ollie continued.

“They retained no peer data. No profile context. It is just movement order plus controller timing.”

“Better.”

“You know who it is.”

“I can guess.”

Ollie looked at them.

Iri looked back.

“What?”

“You are not asking.”

“No.”

“That is unusually disciplined.”

“Do you want me to become worse?”

“No.”

“Then open the panel.”

Ollie did.

The inside was ordinary.

Dirty filter edge.

Two service marks.

One resident complaint from six months earlier that turned out to be a loose grille and nothing else.

They replaced the filter, closed the housing and moved on.

Ollie read the rest of the review packet only after the maintenance block ended.

That delay was eleven minutes.

He knew because he checked.

* * *

The meal break volume had three tables and one window that showed a maintenance spine instead of space.

Ollie preferred it.

Nothing outside was pretending to be inspirational.

He put his food on one side of the table and opened the review packet on the other.

Iri sat opposite with a percussion-frame clamp, a tool and something hot in a container.

Ollie pointed at the clamp.

“You are doing that over food again.”

“It is not over food.”

“It is eleven centimeters from food.”

“Different category.”

“Not hygienically.”

Iri moved the clamp three centimeters farther away.

“Resolved.”

“It is not.”

“Review your packet.”

Ollie did.

The released comparison had four sections.

The mature-module sample from the bounded review.

One source-controlled support-timing comparison cleared only as de-identified order and approximate timing.

The new movement-order case from a mature service transfer.

The original Ring Six event family, reduced to review-approved state relationships rather than raw incident logs.

Ollie opened his local model beside them.

The old structure was still there.

PARTITION OVERLAP.

SERVICE TRANSITION.

RETURN CORRECTION.

BUFFER RESPONSE.

RESIDENT / INTERFACE EVENT.

FIXED RESPONSE.

He added the new movement case.

It did not fit.

There was no Ring Six partition.

No resident report in the released comparison.

No personal-support event needed to define it.

Only mixed movement, a held transition, return compensation and a delayed displayed correction.

Ollie tried classifying it under SERVICE TRANSITION.

The model accepted it and made the case almost meaningless.

Too broad.

He tried HELD TRANSITION.

That pulled in dozens of ordinary cases where nothing happened.

He added RETURN CORRECTION.

The match improved.

Then the negative cases multiplied until the result disappeared.

Ollie frowned.

Iri ate.

He changed the filter conditions.

HELD TRANSITION + RETURN CORRECTION + COUNTERFLOW.

The movement-order case stayed.

Some mature-module lead cases stayed.

Most disappeared.

So did most of the negative cases.

The line looked cleaner.

Ollie stared at it.

Iri looked over.

“What did you remove?”

“Cases that do not share the state combination.”

“How many?”

“Most.”

“Put them back.”

Ollie looked at them.

“They are controls.”

“Then show the controls.”

“They make the display unreadable.”

“That sounds like the display’s problem.”

Ollie felt irritation arrive fast enough to be familiar.

“The point is to isolate the candidate class.”

“And the point of the negative cases?”

“To test whether the class means anything.”

“Where are they?”

Ollie did not answer.

Iri returned to the food.

“Put them back.”

Ollie put them back.

The clean line dissolved.

Six mature modules.

Two control generations.

A lot of ordinary transitions.

A lot of reports that led nowhere.

A smaller number of lead-timing cases.

One module contributed too much of the effect.

Remove that module and the cluster weakened sharply.

The movement-order case still sat outside the body/support evidence entirely.

Ollie moved it to a separate rail.

Iri watched.

“That is better.”

“You do not know what I am doing.”

“I know you stopped pretending one line was the whole answer.”

“That is not what I was doing.”

Iri raised an eyebrow.

Ollie looked back at the screen.

“Fine.”

He created four evidence rails.

CONTROL / ROUTE STATE.

FIXED ENVIRONMENTAL RESPONSE.

RESIDENT / SUPPORT EVENT TIMING.

MOVEMENT-ORDER / HELD-TRANSITION TIMING.

Then he tried aligning them to the first fixed sensor change.

That was how the old model had worked.

Everything became BEFORE or AFTER the machine admitted something had changed.

The result looked familiar.

Too familiar.

Iri said, “You did it again.”

Ollie looked up.

“What?”

“Made the fixed system the middle of the universe.”

“It is a timestamp.”

“It is the timestamp you trust most.”

“Yes.”

“That was the old argument.”

“The fixed response is precise.”

“I know.”

“Resident reports are not.”

“I know.”

“Then why are we having this conversation?”

Ollie looked at the four rails.

Because the new movement-order case had a precise control timestamp before the displayed fixed correction.

No person was required to establish that.

The controller itself knew the held/reversed state existed before the fixed environmental display treated it as a correction event.

He opened the dependency layer from the review-approved Ring Six reconstruction.

Not raw incident data.

Only the released state relationships.

East service held.

Common return correction active.

Service C open.

Partition B locked.

Different named actions.

Shared adaptive loop.

Ollie removed the fixed-response timestamp from the center of the model.

Iri stopped eating.

“What are you doing?”

“I do not know yet.”

“Good.”

“Please stop rewarding uncertainty. It is unpleasant.”

“No.”

Ollie created a new marker.

ESTIMATED SHARED-LOOP STATE ENTRY.

He placed it before fixed response.

Then deleted it.

Iri said, “Why?”

“Because I chose the time by looking at the cases that produced a later response.”

“Circular.”

“Yes.”

“That one I know.”

Ollie opened only the control-state rails.

No resident timing.

No support events.

No fixed threshold.

He used the relationships among allowed control states to estimate when more than one path began loading the same adaptive loop.

The estimate was messy.

Some cases had no clear entry.

Some had broad windows.

The old Ring Six partition-overlap cases were easy because the visible transition defined the overlap sharply.

The distributed-equivalence cases were harder.

The movement-order case gave him a separate control sequence with no private body data.

He created an interval rather than a point.

STATE-ENTRY WINDOW.

Then he put fixed response back on the far rail.

Only after that did he restore resident/support timing.

The screen changed.

Not dramatically.

Enough.

Some early reports now sat after the estimated shared-loop entry window but before fixed display update.

Some sat before any plausible state-entry window.

Some had no later fixed anomaly at all.

Some state-entry windows had no resident report.

The model had become uglier.

Ollie trusted it more.

Iri leaned closer.

“What are those?”

“Reports with no later fixed event.”

“You kept them.”

“Yes.”

“What about transitions with no report?”

Ollie turned on another layer.

There were many.

Iri made a small sound.

“Good?” Ollie asked.

“No.”

“Better?”

“Maybe.”

Ollie looked at them.

“That is my word.”

“You do not own uncertainty.”

He returned to the model.

The candidate pattern was narrow.

Under some combined service/return states, a small subset of resident/support events occurred after estimated state entry but before fixed display update.

That did not mean the reports caused the state.

It did not mean they were measuring the same variable.

It did not mean every report mattered.

The movement-order case mattered because it suggested the hidden alignment problem could exist without needing a body report to define the state.

Ollie wrote the hypothesis in the least satisfying form he could tolerate:

**UNDER A NARROW CLASS OF SHARED-LOOP SERVICE / RETURN STATES, SOME RESIDENT OR PERSONAL-SUPPORT EVENTS MAY OCCUR AFTER SYSTEM STATE ENTRY BUT BEFORE AGGREGATE FIXED DISPLAY UPDATE.**

Iri read it.

“May.”

“Yes.”

“Some.”

“Yes.”

“Narrow class.”

“Yes.”

“You hate this.”

“Yes.”

Iri smiled at the food.

Ollie added:

**ALTERNATIVES REMAIN LIVE:**

EARLY RESPONSE TO HARMLESS LOCAL VARIATION.

INTERFACE-SPECIFIC ADAPTATION.

CONTROL-GENERATION ARTIFACT.

REPORTING / TIMESTAMP BIAS.

PARTICIPATION IN FEEDBACK RATHER THAN EARLY SENSING.

UNRELATED COINCIDENCE.

Iri read the fourth line.

“You missed one.”

“What?”

“The people reports might be right about a change and still useless for predicting the dangerous part.”

Ollie stopped.

“That is the first alternative.”

“No. Harmless local variation means they detect something unrelated. I mean they might detect the shared-loop change correctly and that change might happen a hundred times without oscillation.”

Ollie looked at the negative transition cases.

They had exactly that problem.

He added:

SHARED-LOOP ENTRY MAY BE REAL BUT NOT SUFFICIENT FOR LATER OSCILLATION.

Iri nodded.

Ollie said, “You are becoming useful.”

Iri stared at him.

He heard it.

“Bad sentence.”

“Yes.”

“Very bad.”

“I meant the objection.”

“I know what you meant.”

“That does not improve it.”

“No.”

Iri checked the time.

“I leave in twenty minutes.”

“Why?”

“Rehearsal.”

“Today?”

“It has been today all day.”

“I need one more pass.”

“You have twenty minutes.”

“That is not enough.”

“It is exactly how much exists.”

Ollie looked at the model.

For once he did not ask Iri to stay.

* * *

The second pass was worse.

That helped.

Ollie asked the question he had been avoiding.

“What would make this wrong?”

Iri was packing the percussion clamp into a cloth sleeve.

“The whole thing?”

“The useful part.”

“That is your job.”

“I am asking you because you keep finding ways I am cheating.”

“I am not your validation service.”

“I know.”

Iri zipped the sleeve.

“Fine. One thing.”

Ollie waited.

“If you estimate state entry after seeing which cases have early reports, you can make the window fit them.”

“I already separated the rails.”

“Here.”

“Yes.”

“In the test?”

Ollie looked at the model.

Iri continued.

“Someone should estimate the control-state entry without seeing the resident/support timing.”

Ollie wrote:

**FALSIFIER / BIAS CONTROL: STATE-ENTRY WINDOW MUST BE ESTIMATED FROM CONTROL-STATE DATA BLIND TO RESIDENT / SUPPORT EVENTS.**

Iri lifted the cloth sleeve.

“That is one.”

Ollie said, “Another.”

“I said one.”

“Please.”

Iri looked at the clock.

“Very manipulative.”

“Yes.”

“What if the same shared-loop state happens repeatedly and nothing else follows?”

“That already happens.”

“Then your model needs to say what that means before you test it, not afterward.”

Ollie wrote:

**SHARED-LOOP STATE ENTRY IS NOT SUFFICIENT UNLESS A SEPARATE CONDITION PREDICTS LATER COUPLED RESPONSE.**

He stopped.

That sentence hurt.

The current model did not contain the separate condition.

Iri saw his face.

“There.”

“What?”

“That one upset you.”

“It means the model is incomplete.”

“Yes.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

“Stop.”

Iri stood.

Ollie looked at the negative cases again.

If shared-loop entry happened often without escalation, then finding the entry state did not solve the dangerous behavior.

It only moved the question earlier.

Which was still useful.

Maybe.

He added a third falsifier himself:

**IF LEAD TIMING DISAPPEARS AFTER CONTROL-GENERATION / MODULE EFFECTS ARE HELD OUT, DO NOT RETAIN RESIDENT / SUPPORT LEAD AS GENERAL FEATURE.**

Then:

**IF MOVEMENT-ORDER CASES DO NOT REPRODUCE STATE-ENTRY RELATIONSHIP UNDER CONTROLLED CONDITIONS, DO NOT GENERALIZE SERVICE TRANSFER 4.**

Iri read them over his shoulder.

“You have enough ways to fail now.”

“That is not encouraging.”

“It is a model.”

They picked up the percussion sleeve.

Ollie said, “Good rehearsal.”

Iri paused.

“Thank you.”

The answer was ordinary.

That made Ollie realize how long it had been since either of them had said something that did not contain Ring Six.

Iri left.

Ollie stayed at the table with his food half-finished and the second model open.

He did not rename it FINAL.

He kept the file name:

SECOND MODEL — QUESTIONS ONLY.

Then added:

v2.

* * *

Mara read the proposal at 18:12.

Ollie knew because the submission changed from RECEIVED to OPENED.

He was back on ordinary maintenance by then, checking a mature-ring access panel with Ren.

He did not stop working.

Ren noticed the band vibration.

“You are becoming frightening.”

“What?”

“You ignored two notifications today.”

“I did not ignore them.”

“You delayed looking.”

“That is different.”

“I am reporting this to your friends.”

“They do not have jurisdiction.”

“Emotionally they do.”

Ollie tightened the access latch.

The panel passed.

Only then did he check the status.

MARA: come to service coordination when your block ends

Ollie finished the block.

That delay was seven minutes.

He knew because he checked that too.

Mara was at the same service coordination table where she had refused his raw-data access.

His proposal was open in front of her.

She pointed to the chair.

Ollie sat.

Mara said, “State the question without reading.”

“Can different allowed operational sequences enter the same shared-loop state relationship before fixed environmental display reflects it?”

“And the resident/support part?”

“Separate question. If state entry is estimated independently from control data, do some coarse resident or support events systematically fall after state entry but before fixed display more often than negative controls?”

“Cause?”

“Unknown.”

“Sensor?”

“Not assumed.”

“Participation in feedback?”

“Live alternative.”

“Negative cases?”

“Required.”

“Module effect?”

“Must survive held-out module and control-generation checks.”

Mara turned the display toward him.

His proposed test had three parts.

A hardware-in-loop reproduction of allowed return-correction and service-transition combinations below incident amplitude.

Blind estimation of shared-loop state-entry windows from control states.

Optional later comparison to separately consented coarse human/interface event timing if the review board approved that layer.

Negative control sequences were listed first, not last.

Abort conditions sat at the top.

No residential occupancy.

No Ring Six access required for Ollie.

Mara tapped the third section.

“Remove the volunteers from the first test.”

Ollie looked at her.

“They are already optional.”

“Remove them from phase one entirely.”

“Then we cannot test the lead-timing relationship.”

“You cannot test it until you establish that your shared-loop state estimate is reproducible without using human events to find it.”

Ollie stopped.

That was true.

He changed the proposal.

PHASE 1: CONTROL-STATE REPRODUCTION ONLY.

PHASE 2: SEPARATE REVIEW / CONSENT REQUIRED BEFORE ANY HUMAN OR PERSONAL-INTERFACE TIMING COMPARISON.

Mara nodded.

“Better.”

“Can I run phase one?”

“No.”

The answer arrived so quickly he almost laughed.

“Can I be present?”

“Unknown.”

“Can I help configure it?”

“Unknown.”

“Can I—”

Mara raised a hand.

Ollie stopped.

She changed the proposal status.

SUBMITTED TO INCIDENT REVIEW — CONTROLLED REPRODUCTION DESIGN.

STATUS: ACCEPTED FOR TECHNICAL REVIEW.

NOT AUTHORIZED FOR EXECUTION.

Ollie read the last line.

Mara said, “Your review hold remains.”

“I know.”

“You are not back on Ring Six.”

“I know.”

“This is not reinstatement.”

“I know.”

Mara looked at him.

Ollie said, “You can stop checking.”

“No.”

He almost smiled.

Mara closed the proposal.

“Go eat something.”

“I ate.”

“When?”

Ollie thought about the half-finished meal beside the second model.

Mara waited.

“I began eating.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is adjacent.”

“Go.”

Ollie stood.

His band vibrated as he reached the door.

TEST DESIGN — REVIEW QUEUE.

He looked at the notification.

Then locked the band without opening it again.

The queue would still exist after dinner.
