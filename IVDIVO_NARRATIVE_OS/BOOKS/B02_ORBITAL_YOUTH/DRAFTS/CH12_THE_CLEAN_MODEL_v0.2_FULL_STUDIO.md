# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 12 — THE CLEAN MODEL

Ollie had reduced the problem to three screens.

Mara had made him close the other four.

“This is not an improvement if you reproduce them inside the three,” she said.

“I haven’t.”

“You have tabs.”

“Tabs are not screens.”

“That is exactly the kind of sentence that creates seven screens.”

Ollie closed two tabs.

Mara went back to the commissioning queue.

He reopened one.

Not because she was wrong.

Because the run was incomplete without it.

The diagnostic bench faced an unfinished Ring Six service wall, which meant Ollie could see nothing of the residential spaces people were waiting to inhabit. No trees. No market volume. No school route. Just sealed access panels, test ports, and a section of return ducting whose label had been replaced so recently the old adhesive line still showed around it.

The city was harder to romanticize from here, which suited him.

He arranged the data in time rather than by subsystem.

FIXED BUILDING SENSORS.

MAINTENANCE / PARTITION STATES.

MOVEMENT SEQUENCE.

The first layer was certified and synchronized.

The second came from service logs, door states, air-curtain cycles, route substitutions and maintenance actions.

The third was the messiest: public crossing times, Samir’s route observation, apprentice recollection checked against door records, and Maya’s late-event movement map from the night before.

Maya had sent the map only after removing everyone’s names.

She had also added:

THIS IS NOT YOUR NEW HOBBY

Below it:

AND DO NOT ASK Davi FOR ANYTHING

Ollie had replied:

who is Davi

Maya had not answered.

He aligned the four anomalous runs.

The first looked different from the second.

The second looked different from Samir’s public route event.

The fourth had not technically been a test at all.

But when Ollie stopped asking where the people were and started asking what the habitat had done in what order, the differences narrowed.

Partition opens.

Counterflow enters.

Air curtain increases.

Second partition cycles before the first return correction finishes.

Service-return state updates.

Local environment control changes airflow.

Then—late—fixed return-manifold sensors report the condition the correction was already trying to solve.

Ollie moved the sequence one step to the right.

The delay disappeared.

He moved it back.

The delay returned.

He tried the second run.

Returned.

The third.

Returned, but only if Samir’s estimated crossing time was inside the tighter half of his reported window.

Ollie marked that one amber.

The fourth run failed to line up by 2.4 seconds.

He stared at it.

Then opened the maintenance log he had tried to keep closed.

A service cart had entered before the public route shifted.

Not after.

He corrected the order.

The trace collapsed into the same shape as the others.

Ollie stopped moving.

The bench hummed under his hands.

He replayed all four.

The same shape survived different people, occupancy counts and exact locations. The transition order stayed.

He opened a clean model layer.

Not a component model.

A state-sequence model.

He gave each partition and environment transition a simple state rather than its entire engineering history.

Open.

Closing.

Settling.

Correcting.

Loaded.

Clear.

Then he added movement as events between those states.

The model became smaller, and Ollie distrusted how much he liked that.

He checked the first run again.

It still fit.

Second.

Fit.

Samir’s route.

Fit within the timing uncertainty.

Fourth.

Fit.

Ollie sat back.

Ren appeared over the bench divider carrying a tool case.

“You have the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you think you found something and want everyone to ask.”

“I do not have a face for that.”

“You absolutely do.”

“Iri says you have three,” said the younger apprentice from somewhere behind him.

Ollie turned.

“Why are you discussing my face?”

Ren set the tool case down.

“Slow shift.”

“It is not slow.”

“For you.”

Ollie rotated the model toward them.

Ren immediately stepped back.

“No.”

“You just said—”

“I said you wanted us to ask. I did not say I was asking.”

The younger apprentice leaned around him.

“What is it?”

Ren looked betrayed.

Ollie played the four traces.

The younger apprentice watched once.

“Why are those personal reports on the side?”

Ollie looked at the annotation rail.

Aoife’s note had arrived twenty minutes earlier.

DRY AFTER SECOND DOOR
AIR WENT FLAT
EARS WRONG BEFORE CORRECTION

Below that sat the apprentice reconstruction from the previous shift.

P3 — EAR PRESSURE / SOURCE WINDOW 8.4–10.1 SEC BEFORE FIXED CORRECTION

P7 — PERSONAL INTERFACE EVENT 8.7 SEC BEFORE FIXED CORRECTION

SAMIR — PERSONAL ADAPTATION NOTICE ~9 SEC BEFORE PUBLIC CORRECTION

“They are still in the record,” Ollie said.

“That was not the question.”

“They don’t use the same measurement.”

Ren said, “I have escaped.”

He picked up his tool case and left.

The younger apprentice stayed.

Ollie said, “The primary model uses states I can compare across every run.”

“And those?”

“Annotations until I can distinguish whether they’re early perception of the same environmental shift or part of the causal loop.”

The apprentice considered that.

“Can you?”

“Not yet.”

“Then why is the model finished?”

“It isn’t.”

“You have a title.”

Ollie glanced at the top of the pane.

TRANSITION-SEQUENCE MODEL v0.4

“That is a file name.”

“That is how finished things begin.”

She left before he could answer.

Ollie looked back at the model.

He changed v0.4 to v0.4a.

This did not improve anything.

* * *

Iri arrived from qualification review with a certification tag clipped to their work band and the expression of someone trying very hard not to mention it.

Ollie noticed immediately.

He said nothing.

Iri lasted eleven seconds.

“You are not going to ask?”

“Ask what?”

Iri held up the band.

“Oh.”

“That was convincing.”

“You passed.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“That is all?”

“What do you want?”

“I don’t know. More irritation.”

“You kept the slot.”

“I was there. I noticed.”

Ollie turned back to the model.

Iri moved beside him.

Their personal regulator gave one soft status pulse as they crossed from the cooler service lane into the warmer diagnostic bay. Iri checked the coarse load class at their cuff, waited two seconds, and kept walking.

Ollie saw the movement without opening the data.

He had learned not to ask automatically.

Iri leaned over the bench.

“This is cleaner.”

“Yes.”

“That worries me.”

“It worried me too.”

“No, it didn’t.”

“It did for almost a minute.”

“Historic.”

Ollie replayed the traces.

Iri watched the first two in silence.

On the third they stopped him.

“Your service cut is early.”

“I corrected that.”

“Not there.”

Iri pointed at the movement layer.

“That group did not use the main opening after the cart crossed. They used the side transition because somebody was cleaning the floor seam.”

“That is not in the public route log.”

“It was not a route closure.”

“How do you know?”

“I was there.”

Ollie searched the maintenance notes.

Nothing.

Then the cleaning task appeared in a local service queue, timestamped eleven minutes before the run.

No closure.

No route event.

Just two workers occupying enough of the passage that people had gone around them.

Ollie changed the movement order.

The trace fit better.

He did not say anything.

Iri waited.

Ollie said, “Fine.”

“Beautiful.”

“That does not change the model.”

“It changes the input.”

“Yes.”

“Which changes the model.”

“No. It strengthens it.”

Iri smiled.

“You are going to be unbearable if this works.”

“I am already unbearable.”

“Good self-calibration.”

Ollie opened the prediction layer.

“If the sequence is the driver, then the next defined test should settle normally if we prevent the second partition from cycling before the first correction finishes.”

Iri looked at the annotation rail again.

“And if the people react first?”

“Then we should see the personal events without the later fixed lag.”

“And if their systems change the local environment enough to create the lag?”

“We would see a different relationship.”

“Unless you are not measuring that relationship.”

Ollie tapped the fixed trace.

“We are measuring the environment.”

“Later.”

“We are measuring it continuously.”

“At the return manifold.”

“And local sensors.”

“Not every personal boundary.”

“Correct. Because we are not continuously ingesting private body-support data.”

“I didn’t ask for the feed.”

“Then what?”

Iri tapped P7.

“Make this harder to ignore.”

“It is not one measurement class.”

“I know. P3 isn’t P7. Samir isn’t either. They still all lead the fixed correction.”

“That is why they have timing bands.”

“On the side.”

“They’re unresolved.”

Iri tapped the center trace.

“When you say ‘the model,’ you point here.”

Then the annotation rail.

“When you say ‘unresolved,’ you point here.”

“Yes.”

“That is what I’m complaining about.”

“What variable do you want me to add?”

Iri opened their mouth, then stopped.

“I don’t know.”

“Then I can’t add one.”

“I know.”

Ollie looked at P7’s 8.7-second lead.

“It stays unresolved and visible.”

Iri looked at the side rail, then back at him.

“For now.”

Mara arrived before either of them could improve it.

“What is this?” she asked.

Ollie said, “A state-sequence model that fits all four observed lag events.”

Iri said, “Most of the observed lag events.”

Ollie turned.

“It fits all four.”

“The building lag.”

“Yes.”

“That is what I said.”

Mara looked between them.

“Excellent. You have begun fighting over nouns.”

“We are not—” Ollie started.

“Show me.”

He did, starting with the traces instead of a conclusion: run one, run two, Samir’s public transition, then the corrected fourth sequence.

Mara stopped him twice to ask where an input came from.

Once because he had mixed a recollected participant window with a synchronized door state.

Once because Maya’s anonymized movement map used a public route estimate, not raw transit telemetry.

Ollie corrected both confidence labels.

Then he showed the prediction.

“If the second partition remains in settle until the first correction closes, the lag should not appear.”

Mara said, “Should.”

“Yes.”

“What would count against it?”

“Same sequence control, no overlap, but lag still appears. Or repeated personal lead events followed by fixed lag despite the removed partition overlap.”

Mara glanced at Iri.

“Your objection?”

Iri pointed at the side rail.

“Those are treated as reports about the event. They may be events.”

Mara looked at Ollie.

“Why are they not primary variables?”

“Inconsistent vocabulary. Different devices. Some are remembered afterward. Some are personal support events with privacy limits. We can compare timing bands, but not treat them as one measurement class.”

“That is true.”

Iri shifted.

Mara continued.

“It does not make them irrelevant.”

“I didn’t remove them.”

“Good.”

She opened the model record and locked the annotation rail to the version.

UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE — CANNOT DELETE FROM REVIEW COPY

Ollie frowned.

“I was not going to delete it.”

“Then the lock costs you nothing.”

Iri looked at him.

Ollie did not look back.

Mara said, “This model is good enough to justify a bounded test.”

Ollie felt the sentence before he could stop himself.

Good enough to test. The wording landed harder than praise would have. It was exactly the threshold he had been trying to reach.

Mara added, “It is not good enough to justify opening.”

The rush flattened.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

“Then write the test request.”

She left.

Ren reappeared at the divider.

“I heard ‘good enough.’”

“You were listening.”

“The whole bay was listening.”

“No one was.”

Ren pointed toward the younger apprentice, who immediately looked away with exaggerated innocence.

“Congratulations,” Ren said. “Earth kid gets into the commissioning report.”

“I am not getting into a report.”

“Mara just locked your model to review.”

“The model goes into review.”

“Yes.”

“I do not.”

Ren smiled.

“Absolutely.”

Ollie turned back to the screen.

The top of the record now read:

ORIGINATING ANALYST — OLLIE [YOUTH COHORT / SUPERVISED]

He minimized the header.

Iri saw him do it.

Neither of them commented.

* * *

The test request took thirty-one minutes because every simple statement created a question.

EXPECTED STATE.

ALLOWED OCCUPANCY.

TRANSITION ORDER.

STOP CONDITION.

WHAT THIS TEST DOES NOT ESTABLISH.

Ollie liked the last field least.

He wrote:

DOES NOT ESTABLISH FULL MIXED-OCCUPANCY SAFETY.
DOES NOT VALIDATE UNCONTROLLED ROUTE SUBSTITUTION.
DOES NOT RESOLVE CAUSAL STATUS OF EARLY RESIDENT/PERSONAL-INTERFACE REPORTS.
DOES NOT AUTHORIZE HABITATION.

The system accepted the request.

Mara approved it for scheduling review.

No celebration occurred.

A different queue moved one item forward.

Ollie’s band buzzed.

He ignored it.

The model replay still had one amber timing window.

He narrowed Samir’s public route event by comparing the transfer door close with a sport-group message timestamp Samir had explicitly said Ollie could use for timing only.

The amber band shrank by 1.6 seconds.

His band buzzed again.

He ignored it again.

The city outside the service bay had begun changing for Vela Night.

Even here, the effects arrived indirectly.

A transit line moved to event phasing.

Two technicians left early because they had family commitments.

Someone in the next bay complained that the public food volume had stolen three portable tables from maintenance break space.

A moving-percussion rehearsal passed somewhere beyond the service wall as a pattern of low impacts that changed speed when the performers crossed a gravity transition.

Iri looked up immediately.

“That is too fast.”

Ollie listened.

“You can tell from here?”

“They are rushing the mid-band handoff.”

“Why?”

“Because someone thinks faster is exciting.”

“Isn’t it?”

“No. It sounds like furniture falling down stairs.”

The rhythm stopped.

Three seconds later it restarted slower.

Iri looked pleased.

“You were right.”

“I usually am about things that matter.”

“You were late because you read the wrong closure marker yesterday.”

“That did not matter.”

“It mattered to you.”

“Temporary exception.”

Iri checked the time.

“I have to go.”

“Rehearsal?”

“Change first. Then rehearsal. Then performance route.”

They looked at Ollie’s work band.

“You are still here for the event block.”

“Yes.”

“Enjoy your excellent life decision.”

“You have rehearsal.”

“That is different.”

“How?”

“I like mine.”

Iri left.

Ollie’s band buzzed a third time.

This time the sender preview caught his eye.

ROVER TEAM — MILESTONE

He opened it.

A video thumbnail filled the small display.

The storage area in Virginia.

Same scarred workbench.

Same yellow floor line.

The rescue rover sat on blocks with the new drive assembly installed.

The component Ollie had left behind on departure day was not there.

A different bracket had replaced it.

For half a second he assumed they had misunderstood his design.

Then the video started.

The rover rolled forward.

Stopped.

Changed load.

Rolled again.

No vibration.

No alignment warning.

Someone behind the camera shouted.

Another person hit the side panel twice in celebration even though everybody had been told not to hit the side panel.

The video ended with the team standing around the rover.

Four people.

Ollie’s old place at the left side of the bench was occupied by a toolbox.

A message followed.

WE FIXED THE BRACKET PROBLEM

Then:

YOUR CLEARANCE IDEA HELPED BUT JUNE CHANGED THE LOAD PATH

Then:

IT WORKS

A final photo showed everyone holding terrible vending-machine drinks.

Ollie zoomed in on the bracket.

June’s solution was better than his—not completely, but enough.

He typed:

check fastener wear after third load cycle

He stopped and deleted it.

They knew how to test a bracket.

He typed:

nice

Deleted that too. Even nice looked wrong.

The group thread on his other channel lit up.

MAYA: we are leaving in ten

SAMIR: you said that twelve minutes ago

MAYA: time is different here

ETHAN: it is not different enough to support that argument

AOIFE: do not assist her

MAYA: where is Ollie

ETHAN: work

AOIFE: obviously

Ollie stared at Ethan’s answer.

Ethan had only stated where he was: work. Somehow that landed worse than criticism.

He opened the rover video again.

The team had solved the problem.

The rover had not waited for him.

It should have felt good.

Part of it did.

Another part wanted the bracket to fail just enough that somebody had to ask what he thought.

Ollie locked the video and returned to the model.

One amber timing window remained.

If he ran the sequence once more with the service-cart event shifted to its earliest plausible time, he could see whether the prediction survived the full uncertainty band.

His band buzzed.

ETHAN: We can meet you at second performance route after your block

Ollie typed:

OLLIE: probably

Ethan replied:

ETHAN: not a scheduling term

OLLIE: 20:40 earliest

MAYA: criminal

SAMIR: exhibition starts before that

OLLIE: I know

AOIFE: he came to orbit to miss orbit

Ollie almost answered.

Instead he ran the model.

The prediction survived.

He moved the service cart to the latest plausible time.

Still survived.

He had every reason to stop: the test request was filed, Mara had called the model good enough, the rover team had already had its milestone, and Vela Night was beginning without him.

Ollie opened one more run.

Outside the sealed service wall, the percussion route started again.

This time the timing was right.
