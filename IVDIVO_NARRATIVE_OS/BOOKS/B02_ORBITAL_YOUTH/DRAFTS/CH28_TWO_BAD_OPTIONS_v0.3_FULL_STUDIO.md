# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 28 — TWO BAD OPTIONS

Ethan’s first mistake was adding a column called TOTAL COST.

He knew it was a mistake because Nadia Park stopped talking.

Until then, the Ring Six review had been loud enough to feel normal.

Housing wanted numbers.

Safety wanted conditions.

Service planning wanted to know whether either proposed control path would destroy the maintenance schedule.

Residents wanted to know when they could sleep in the rooms they had already been shown.

Someone from education wanted to know why a school route appeared safe on one map and impossible on another.

Tomas wanted a chair.

He had been given one.

That had not improved his mood.

Ethan sat at the side of the room with a shared consequence sheet open because an adult coordinator had asked him to keep the dependencies readable, not decide the outcome.

He had understood the distinction when they asked.

Then the room had become complicated.

Option A occupied the left side of his screen.

CONSERVATIVE RECOVERY INTERLOCK

Wait for physical return recovery before allowing the next relevant service/environment transition.

Under it:

LOWER MODEL DEPENDENCE.

CLEAR STOP LOGIC.

LOWER THROUGHPUT.

LONGER PARTITION HOLDS.

SERVICE QUEUE PROPAGATION.

REDUCED PEAK OCCUPANCY CAPACITY.

Option B occupied the right.

STATE-AWARE CONTROL

Estimate residual recovery state and inhibit only combinations predicted to enter the reproduced coupled condition.

Under it:

HIGHER AVAILABLE THROUGHPUT.

FEWER GLOBAL HOLDS.

HIGHER MODEL DEPENDENCE.

CONTROL-GENERATION VARIATION.

DANGEROUS ESCALATION CONDITION NOT FULLY EXPLAINED.

Ethan had not written the last line.

Safety had.

He liked that it was there.

The problem was everything beneath it.

Housing consequences did not fit neatly under engineering consequences.

If Option A lengthened service recovery windows, then one connector lost enough peak capacity to make three proposed household allocations impractical.

If Option B preserved capacity, safety wanted tighter operating envelopes during mixed service periods.

Those envelopes could affect environmental profiles differently.

No one had proposed banning a type of person.

No one had to.

A restriction on one environment profile plus one route plus one service period could still mean that a particular household did not get the room they had been promised.

Ethan added:

A — CAPACITY COST

B — UNCERTAINTY COST

Then regretted those too.

Nadia was standing at the main table with a housing display behind her.

“We have forty-one households whose current temporary arrangements materially worsen school, work or care access,” she said. “Not all forty-one require Ring Six this week. Seventeen do not have a stable alternative after the next old-sector service closure.”

A resident representative interrupted.

“What does stable mean?”

Nadia turned.

“Same household can sleep in compatible accommodation without splitting across host or temporary modules.”

“Then say that.”

“I just did.”

“You said stable first.”

Nadia paused.

“Fair.”

Ethan changed the label in his sheet.

Tomas, from the second row, said, “Can you also remove ‘commute burden’?”

Nadia looked at him.

“What would you prefer?”

“Forty-seven minutes and two transfers with one hand.”

A few people laughed.

Tomas did not.

Nadia changed the slide.

Ethan changed his sheet too.

His TOTAL COST column remained blank.

* * *

The conservative interlock looked safer until the review ran actual movement and service schedules through it.

At full physical recovery between relevant transitions, the control logic was easy to explain.

A service combination entered.

Local correction completed.

Return network recovered.

Then the next combination received permission.

Nothing clever.

No estimated hidden recovery state.

No need to trust the second model beyond recognizing which combinations required conservative separation.

The first simulation looked excellent.

Then service planning added the real schedule.

A delivery window moved.

A maintenance cart waited.

One school-route connector lost six minutes.

Then eleven.

Then a worker shift change loaded the same route.

The hold propagated.

Not dangerously.

Operationally.

Nadia pointed at the screen.

“That.”

The safety lead said, “That is expected.”

“I know it is expected.”

“Then what are you asking?”

“I’m asking whether expected means acceptable when the result is twelve households staying temporary for another cycle.”

“Safety does not certify acceptable housing delay.”

“And housing does not certify environmental risk. We have discovered departments.”

The resident representative beside Lea said, “How many households under this version?”

Nadia checked.

“Depends which service windows we protect.”

“How many if you protect all current maintenance windows?”

“Eleven lose first-phase eligibility.”

“Lose?”

“Cannot be allocated under the modeled route capacity.”

Lea was sitting next to an older household member.

She looked at the number.

“Are we one?”

Nadia did not answer from memory.

She opened the household layer.

Ethan respected that.

“Not under this version.”

Lea relaxed.

Then saw somebody else in the room notice.

Her face changed.

The relief disappeared before she could pretend it had not happened.

Ethan looked back at his sheet.

Option A.

Eleven households delayed under current maintenance protection.

He typed the number.

Then stopped.

Eleven was not the cost.

It was a count.

He deleted the cell.

* * *

Option B looked better until safety started asking what it needed to believe.

State-aware control preserved more capacity because it did not wait for full physical recovery after every relevant transition.

Instead, the controller would estimate whether residual return imbalance remained inside the candidate risk state and inhibit only the combinations likely to recreate the distributed coupling.

The test rig supported that idea.

In a test rig.

With pre-registered states.

With adult control.

With nobody moving house.

The safety lead displayed the conditions required for a limited operational trial.

Validated recovery-state estimator.

Control-generation-specific limits.

Conservative fallback if estimator confidence dropped.

No unsupported inference from body or comfort reports.

Enhanced event logging.

Restricted service combinations during unresolved states.

None of those conditions named a person as unsafe. Housing still showed that the combinations would land unevenly: a route/environment restriction that looked neutral in control logic could remove one household's only workable school-and-care path while leaving another household untouched.

A resident in the back said, “That sounds like the same restrictions with more software.”

An engineer answered, “Not the same restrictions.”

“Will my route close?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then from my side it has achieved a family resemblance.”

Another resident said, “If it keeps the ring usable most of the time, I prefer that.”

“You prefer a system they just admitted they don’t fully understand?”

“I prefer my children not changing temporary rooms again.”

“You don’t know if your children are the ones carrying the uncertainty.”

“And neither do you.”

The room did not split into two camps. Every new answer crossed the categories Ethan had drawn on the sheet.

There was no resident position.

There were residents.

One wanted the conservative rule because a household member used a narrow environmental compatibility range and did not want a live estimator deciding when a mixed transition was acceptable.

Another wanted state-aware control because the conservative holds made the work route almost as bad as their current temporary housing.

A third wanted to know why both options treated the ring as one operational object when the reproduced condition depended on particular combinations.

Ethan looked up at that.

So did Ollie.

Ollie was not at the main table.

His REVIEW HOLD had not disappeared.

He sat with the technical support row and only had the released model outputs.

The question seemed to hit him physically.

He leaned toward his screen.

Mara saw.

“Later,” she said.

Ollie sat back.

Ethan wrote the question down anyway.

WHY ONE GLOBAL RULE?

He did not know whether it belonged in his sheet.

* * *

The review broke for thirty minutes.

Samir had Crossband practice in forty-two.

He had attended only because one of the resident-review coordinators had asked whether the transition timing case he contributed had any additional privacy constraints for the decision packet.

He had answered that in six minutes.

Then stayed because Ethan had asked him to look at the consequence map.

Ethan had asked him to look at the consequence map after Samir had already answered the privacy question that brought him to the review.

They were in a food corridor now.

Samir had one shoe half unlaced and was eating too quickly.

Aoife sat opposite him with no recorder.

Ethan put the tablet between them.

“I need to know if this is readable.”

Aoife looked.

“It is readable.”

“Good.”

“That was not praise.”

Samir swallowed.

“You have seventeen rows.”

“There are seventeen dependencies.”

“You color-coded them.”

“Yes.”

“Bad sign.”

“The colors are categories.”

“Worse.”

Ethan ignored him.

The sheet no longer had TOTAL COST.

It now had:

CONSEQUENCE.

WHO EXPERIENCES IT.

REVERSIBLE?

EVIDENCE CONFIDENCE.

OPTION A.

OPTION B.

The columns made sense.

Then, below them, hidden one row lower than the review version, Ethan had built something else.

WEIGHT.

He had not planned to show it.

Aoife found it immediately.

“What’s that?”

“Nothing.”

“You made a hidden score.”

“It isn’t hidden.”

“It is literally hidden.”

“Collapsed.”

Samir looked at Ethan.

“What are you scoring?”

“Not people.”

“That answer came fast.”

“Consequences.”

Aoife opened the row.

Ethan stopped her.

“It’s private.”

“You put it in a shared document.”

“Local private layer.”

“Still sounds healthy.”

Samir finished the food.

“What’s the score?”

Ethan should have closed the sheet.

Instead he explained.

Each consequence had:

severity,

number affected,

duration,

reversibility,

confidence.

He had not used money.

He had not ranked households.

He had tried to make unlike consequences visible without letting the loudest person win.

The method was reasonable.

That was why he kept talking.

“If eleven households are delayed but the delay is reversible, that is different from putting a smaller number of households into an environmental condition with lower-confidence control.”

Samir said, “Sure.”

Ethan felt relieved too early.

Samir pointed at the weight column.

“Who decided how different?”

Ethan looked at it.

“I did for the draft.”

“Why?”

“Somebody has to test whether the map produces nonsense.”

“Does it?”

“No.”

“That’s unfortunate.”

Aoife was reading the weights.

“You gave housing split a seven.”

“It’s provisional.”

“And uncertain environmental exposure is ten.”

“Yes.”

“Long commute?”

“Four to six depending duration.”

“Tomas gets a modifier?”

“For injury and transfer complexity.”

Aoife looked up.

“You built a very polite machine for deciding what other people should hate most.”

“That is not what it does.”

Samir tied his shoe.

“What does the highest score mean?”

Ethan opened his mouth.

Preferred option.

He did not say it.

Samir waited.

“What?” Ethan said.

“You know the answer.”

“The score is comparative.”

“Comparative for what?”

“To identify which option creates lower combined harm.”

Samir stood.

“Acceptable to who?”

Ethan felt the question as irritation before he understood it.

“That’s why the affected-group column exists.”

“No. The affected-group column tells you who.”

“Yes.”

“The weight tells them how much it should count.”

“I’m not telling them—”

“You are if the total chooses.”

“It doesn’t choose.”

Samir pointed at the bottom.

Ethan followed his finger.

OPTION A — 63.4

OPTION B — 57.8

He had added conditional formatting.

Option B was green.

He hated himself slightly.

Aoife said, “Your map is useful.”

Ethan looked at her.

“Thank you.”

“I’m not done.”

“Of course.”

“The part where you show who gets hit, what can be reversed, and what evidence is weak—that’s useful.”

“And the score?”

“That’s you getting lonely and deciding numbers can keep you company.”

Samir laughed.

Ethan did not.

“That is not an argument.”

“No,” Aoife said. “It’s an observation.”

Samir checked the time.

“I have practice.”

Ethan looked at the score again.

“So what, I delete every comparison?”

“No,” Samir said.

“Then what?”

“Keep the part that tells people what happens.”

“And?”

Samir shrugged.

“Let the people it happens to argue.”

“That is not a decision process.”

“Good thing I’m late.”

He left.

Ethan watched him go.

Aoife remained.

She tapped the OPTION B total.

“You were hoping it would tell you.”

“Yes.”

The answer came out before he could improve it.

Aoife nodded and left the silence where it was.

“I’m trying to make the decision less arbitrary.”

“I know.”

“If everyone just states what they want, the people with more status or louder voices—”

“I know.”

“Then why is this wrong?”

Aoife leaned back.

“Maybe it isn’t wrong to calculate.”

Ethan frowned.

“That sounds like a trap.”

“It is.”

She pointed at the sheet.

“You can calculate consequences. You can test sensitivity. You can show that your answer flips if environmental uncertainty goes from ten to eight.”

Ethan looked.

It did.

Option A became lower.

Aoife said, “That seems useful.”

“It means the decision is unstable.”

“It means your values are inside it.”

“They have to be somewhere.”

“Yes.”

Ethan looked at her.

Aoife said, “The problem is when you hide them in a formula and bring the answer back as if the formula discovered them.”

Ethan looked back at the weights and found he could no longer read the green total without seeing the choices he had put inside it.

“Did you rehearse that?”

“No.”

“It sounded rehearsed.”

“Then forget it.”

She stood.

“Where are you going?”

“Schoolwork.”

“You have schoolwork now?”

“I have had schoolwork the entire time.”

“You never mention it.”

“You never ask.”

That landed somewhere Ethan had not prepared.

Aoife picked up her bag.

“Keep the map.”

“The score?”

She looked at him.

“That’s your problem.”

She left.

Ethan stared at the green 57.8.

Then collapsed the row again.

He left it collapsed for now.

* * *

When the review resumed, Ethan’s consequence map went on the wall. The private score did not.

He had removed the private layer from the shared packet entirely.

The public version showed:

what each option required,

what each option prevented,

who carried each consequence,

which consequences were reversible,

where evidence was weak,

what would change the answer.

No preferred option.

The room remained difficult.

The lack of consensus exposed exactly which assumptions still belonged to the decision rather than the engineering.

Option A became worse when service planning showed what conservative recovery did to late maintenance windows.

Option B became worse when safety showed how quickly estimator confidence could fall if a control-generation mismatch appeared.

Residents disagreed about which uncertainty they would accept.

Housing refused to call a delayed household “low impact” because it had stable temporary shelter.

Safety refused to call a technically small probability “acceptable” without specifying the exposure and fallback.

Nobody won.

Ethan found that deeply inefficient.

He kept taking notes.

A resident asked, “Which option can you reverse faster?”

Ethan answered because the answer was on the map.

“A can be relaxed after validation. B can fall back to A automatically if confidence drops, but the operating complexity is higher.”

“Which gives us more rooms?”

“Nadia.”

He redirected the question.

Nadia answered.

Another person asked whether the state-aware option would collect more personal data.

Safety answered: not under the proposed design; the estimator used infrastructure state, not individual body telemetry.

Aoife’s source did not become an argument for monitoring people.

Samir’s timing case did not become permission to track sport.

Ethan had added those boundaries to the dependency map.

That part he was proud of.

Then the same resident who had earlier questioned the global rule said, “Why are we choosing one for the whole ring?”

Pens stopped and several screens went still.

Ollie looked up again.

This time Mara did not say later.

The safety lead answered first.

“Operational simplicity.”

The resident said, “That is not a safety property.”

“It can be. Simpler systems have fewer mode interactions.”

“Does the risk exist equally everywhere?”

“No.”

“Then why does the restriction?”

Nobody answered immediately.

Ethan looked at the map.

Option A.

Option B.

Whole ring.

One policy.

His hidden score had assumed that structure without questioning it.

Ollie raised a hand and waited for Mara to acknowledge him before speaking.

He said, “The reproduced state depends on a combination.”

The safety lead looked at him.

“Continue.”

“Control generation. Recovery state. Service timing. Movement order. The full-ring policy treats all segments as if they present the same combination.”

“That simplifies certification.”

“Yes.”

Ollie looked at Nadia’s housing map.

“What if certification follows the combinations we can defend?”

Nadia said, “Meaning?”

Ollie stopped before turning the question into a plan.

“Meaning I don’t know yet. It needs a segment review.”

Mara’s expression changed by approximately nothing.

Ethan knew her well enough now to recognize interest.

The resident representative said, “That sounds like some places open and some don’t.”

“Probably,” Ollie said.

“Who decides which?”

“Not me.”

Nadia opened the ring map only after the safety lead marked the discussion as a scoping exercise, not an operating decision.

Segments lit by service geometry.

Environmental profile.

Route load.

School access.

Delivery windows.

Ethan watched the options on his own screen become inadequate.

A and B were still real.

They just might not have to apply globally in the same way.

He created a new section.

QUESTIONS FOR SEGMENT REVIEW

He kept it to questions rather than building a third-option score.

Which segment/state combinations were already defensible?

Which service windows produced the risky recovery condition?

Which occupancy patterns depended on those windows?

Which households could actually live in a segment without crossing a closed one for school, work or care?

Who would still wait?

That last line stayed at the bottom without a weight or number attached.

The review ended without choosing Option A or B.

Nobody seemed satisfied.

The review closed with both operating paths still live and a new segment-specific question assigned for technical/housing review. Ethan saved the packet without adding a recommendation.

* * *

Outside, Aoife was sitting on the floor against the corridor wall finishing the schoolwork she had claimed to have all along.

Ethan stopped.

“You were serious.”

She looked up.

“About?”

“Schoolwork.”

“No. This is decorative.”

He sat beside her.

She kept reading.

Ethan opened his tablet.

The private weighted score was still there.

63.4.

57.8.

He selected the layer.

Aoife noticed.

“You deleting it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Very dramatic.”

“It was useful.”

“Then keep it.”

He looked at her.

“You said—”

“I said don’t pretend it discovered the answer.”

“So I can use it?”

“You don’t need my permission.”

“That is not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Ethan left the private sheet in his own archive.

Then removed the result row.

The weights remained.

The sensitivity tests remained.

The green preferred option disappeared.

Aoife went back to her assignment.

After a minute she said, “Better.”

Ethan looked at her.

“You did that on purpose.”

“Yes.”

He almost argued.

Then his band chimed.

Samir had sent a Crossband score.

LOSS BY TWO.

Under it:

SAMIR: your fault

ETHAN: I was not there

SAMIR: exactly

Ethan smiled.

The Ring Six review packet updated.

GLOBAL OPTIONS REMAIN ACTIVE:
A — CONSERVATIVE RECOVERY INTERLOCK
B — STATE-AWARE CONTROL

NEXT WORKING QUESTION:
SEGMENT-SPECIFIC OPERATING ENVELOPES / OCCUPANCY FEASIBILITY.

No recommendation had been issued.

Ethan closed the packet.

He closed the tablet with the next problem organized and no answer attached to his name.
