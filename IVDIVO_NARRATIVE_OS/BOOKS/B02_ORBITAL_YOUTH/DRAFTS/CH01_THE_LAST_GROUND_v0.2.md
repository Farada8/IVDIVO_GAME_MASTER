BOOK TWO — ORBITAL YOUTH

CHAPTER ONE

THE LAST GROUND


Ollie had seventeen kilograms left and a problem that weighed two point three.

The two point three kilos were sitting on his workbench in a gray foam case with RESCUE ROVER TEAM written across the lid in somebody else’s block letters. Inside was a calibration jig for the rescue rover’s wheel assemblies: aluminum frame, printed ceramic spacers, three reference blocks, and the stupid little spring clip that had taken them four versions to stop snapping.

It was not his.

This had not stopped him from putting it on the luggage scale twice.

The scale had not become more sympathetic.

17.2 KG REMAINING

Ollie took the case off and put his duffel back on.

14.9 KG REMAINING

He stared at the difference.

His orbital residency portal had been very clear about personal baggage. Clothing could be fabricated or replaced locally within ordinary limits. Basic hygiene supplies existed. Standard tools would be issued by placement. Unauthorized personal tools would be held at transfer security, which seemed like a complicated way of saying please do not smuggle your favorite wrench into orbit.

The portal had not said anything useful about the fact that a hoodie could be fabricated and still not be the hoodie his maker team had signed in silver marker after the rover’s first successful field run.

He had packed that.

He had also packed a paperback repair manual printed before he was born, because its margins contained six years of bad notes from three different people and one drawing of a wheel bearing wearing a crown.

That could not be fabricated either.

The jig absolutely could.

Not before Friday, though.

His tablet chimed on the bench.

RESCUE ROVER TEAM

Priya: left front still drifting under load

Lidia: encoder?

Priya: changed it

Lidia: wiring?

Priya: continuity good

A photo appeared underneath: the rover up on blocks in the co-op, one wheel hanging free, the orange chassis half-disassembled around it.

Ollie zoomed in.

The left-front suspension cable was routed differently from the right.

He typed:

check strain at full compression. cable’s pulling—

Three dots appeared from Lidia.

He stopped.

Lidia: found it

A second photo arrived. She had circled the cable tie in red.

Priya: re-routing now

Ollie deleted what he had started.

For almost a year, the rover had occupied the back third of the maker co-op and an unreasonable amount of Ollie’s brain. It had failed in mud, rain, loose gravel, a drainage ditch, and once on perfectly flat concrete because Maya had convinced them to let her test the “emergency obstacle mode” indoors.

The team had fixed it every time.

He had fixed a lot of it.

That was different.

His tablet chimed again.

Priya: leave the jig. Lidia booked the replacement print if we need it.

Ollie looked at the gray case.

He typed:

Was going to.

Priya reacted with a thumbs-up.

No one asked when he would be online.

No one asked whether he could walk them through the new wheel-load test from Dublin.

They had a plan.

He should have been pleased.

He was pleased.

He put the jig on the shelf.

The empty space in the duffel did not feel like seventeen kilograms. It felt like permission to take something else, which was worse.

He opened the packing list again.

CLOTHING: compliant.
PERSONAL MEDICATION: none.
WORK PLACEMENT PPE: issued on arrival.
PERSONAL TOOLING: not authorized.
ELECTRONICS: compliant.
FOOD / ORGANIC MATERIAL: restricted.
UNREPLICABLE PERSONAL ITEMS: traveler discretion within mass allowance.

“Unreplicable” was doing a lot of work.

Ollie took the hoodie off the back of his chair, folded it once, and pushed it into the bag.

The luggage scale dropped to 14.2.

Fine.

He added the paperback manual.

13.7.

Still fine.

On the corner of the bench sat a little resin wheel from the rover’s first prototype, warped from a printer bed that had never heated evenly. It served no function. The wheel was too small for anything they built now, one side was flat, and there was a permanent black fingerprint baked into the resin where Ollie had touched it before it cooled.

He weighed it in his hand.

Probably thirty grams.

Maybe forty.

He put it in the duffel.

His tablet chimed.

Priya: fixed

Jamal: load test clean

Then, after a few seconds:

Priya: don’t miss the shuttle thing because you’re staring at this chat

Ollie checked the time.

He had forty-three minutes.

He typed:

It’s not a shuttle.

Jamal: space bus

Ollie: International flight first.

Lidia: earth bus

Ollie: You are both banned from terminology.

Jamal: have fun in the sky factory

Ollie smiled despite himself.

He sent a thumbs-up.

Then he closed the group chat before they could solve anything else without him.


* * *


The residency staging area had been built into a terminal that still looked mostly like an airport.

That helped.

There were bag scales. There were families standing in inconvenient places. There were people asking whether water bottles counted as liquids during one part of the route and not another. A toddler was crying because a stuffed animal had to go through a scanner. An older man in a Confederation-adapted mobility frame was arguing with a human security officer about whether one detachable support rail counted as personal equipment or medical hardware.

The future had apparently decided to keep queues.

Ollie liked that more than he expected.

The five of them had checked in through different counters even though they were on the same residency.

Maya had been sent to a body-adaptation station because she had selected three optional high-mobility activity blocks before anyone could tell her not to.

Samir had a case for sport equipment that kept triggering an “unverified geometry” warning.

Aoife had already recorded forty seconds of airport sound and been told to stop once.

Ethan was standing under the departure board, reading something nobody had asked him to read.

Ollie found the work-placement desk before he found the others.

A woman wearing a blue transit vest scanned his residency band and pulled up his placement options.

“You have not finalized,” she said.

“I know.”

“You selected maintenance and commissioning as preferred.”

“Yes.”

“And public education support as secondary.”

“That was the default secondary.”

“It is still available.”

“I don’t want it.”

She looked at him for a moment, then rotated the display.

Two placement cards sat side by side.

ORBITAL HABITAT SYSTEMS — SUPERVISED MAINTENANCE / COMMISSIONING

PUBLIC CONTACT EDUCATION — YOUTH RESIDENCY SUPPORT

The second one had better hours.

Ollie had known that already.

The portal showed it again anyway.

Maintenance included early shifts, irregular shift extensions, restricted tool custody, mandatory safety briefings, and work periods that could move when commissioning windows changed. Public education support had fixed blocks, media/public interaction, guided visitor work, and more unscheduled residency time.

The five of them might actually see one another.

He read the maintenance card again.

“You understand that this is supervised work,” the coordinator said. “Not observational access.”

“Yes.”

“You may be assigned cleaning verification, inventory, inspection support, tagging, basic calibration assistance, or other tasks that are not interesting.”

“Yes.”

“You say that very quickly.”

“They’re work.”

“That was not my concern.”

Ollie looked up.

She pointed at the schedule.

“Shift changes can override elective residency activities. Tool access is logged. You cannot independently authorize a repair. You cannot move into a restricted system because you think you know what is wrong. If your supervisor says stop, you stop.”

“I know.”

“Your cohort file says you know many things.”

He waited.

The coordinator’s mouth moved slightly.

“Would you like to read it before deciding?”

He did.

He had already read it twice.

He tapped maintenance.

The card expanded.

CONFIRM PLACEMENT?

His finger stayed over the display.

There was no reason to hesitate.

The rescue-rover team had already survived a morning without him. The point of going to orbit was not to spend six weeks explaining exhibits to visitors about systems other people actually maintained.

He pressed CONFIRM.

The display changed.

PLACEMENT LOCKED
ORBITAL HABITAT SYSTEMS
INITIAL REPORTING WINDOW: ASSIGNED ON ARRIVAL

“Done,” the coordinator said. “Your work kit allocation will update before Dublin.”

“Okay.”

“Check the first-week schedule when it arrives. People often assume ‘youth placement’ means the city will schedule around them.”

“I don’t.”

“Good. The city does not know who you are.”

Ollie liked her immediately.

“Thanks.”

He stepped away from the desk and nearly walked into Ethan.

“Maintenance?” Ethan asked.

Ollie adjusted the duffel on his shoulder. “Yes.”

“That’s the right choice.”

The annoyance arrived before Ollie could stop it.

Not because Ethan was wrong.

That was the irritating part.

Ethan looked genuinely pleased for him. No competition. No hidden argument. Just Ethan seeing a decision, placing it in the larger structure in his head, and approving the fit.

As if the universe had handed him a clipboard.

Ollie said, “Glad we got authorization.”

Ethan blinked.

Then he smiled. “You’re welcome.”

That made it worse and better at the same time.

Aoife came up behind him with her recorder lowered at her side.

“What did he authorize?”

“My career.”

“I said it was the right choice.”

“That’s how it starts,” Aoife said.

Ethan pointed at her. “You are not helping.”

“Correct.”

Maya arrived with a paper cup in each hand and gave one to Samir, who was carrying the long equipment case like it had personally offended him.

“They think my training fins are tools,” Samir said.

“They are tools,” Ethan said.

“They are shoes.”

“They have tension inserts.”

“They are shoes with ambition.”

Ollie laughed.

Maya handed him the other cup.

“What’s this?”

“Coffee.”

“You don’t know if I wanted coffee.”

“I did not buy it for you. I bought two and regretted the second.”

Ollie tasted it.

It was terrible.

“Thanks.”

“Exactly.”

For thirty seconds, the five of them stood in one place.

Then all five residency bands chimed.

Ollie looked down.

PRE-DEPARTURE REQUIREMENTS UPDATED

His screen listed work-equipment verification and a Dublin occupational briefing.

Maya swore softly.

“What?”

“Mine says mobility adaptation check.”

“You selected the high-mobility blocks,” Ethan said.

“I selected things to do.”

“Those are the same thing if the things are stupid enough.”

“Your birthday is in twelve days.”

“That is not relevant.”

“It felt relevant.”

Aoife was reading hers. “Media storage declaration.”

Samir looked at his. “Sport equipment inspection. Again.”

Ethan’s was longer.

Of course it was longer.

“Mine has route coordination,” he said.

Aoife looked at him.

“Don’t.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were about to.”

“I was going to say we should compare—”

“No.”

“We’re literally traveling together.”

“Apparently not.”

Ollie checked his screen again.

INITIAL WORK ORIENTATION may conflict with group cultural block.

He almost opened the detailed schedule.

Then he remembered the rescue-rover chat.

He had told himself he would join their remote milestone from orbit if the time worked. The test weekend was supposed to happen during the second week of residency.

He had not checked the maintenance calendar against it before confirming.

That was fixable.

Probably.

He locked the screen.

Across the staging hall, a boarding notice changed from blue to amber.

Their flight to Dublin had opened.

The five of them started moving at once.

Not in the same direction.


* * *


The food court was on the wrong side of security and had six options, three of which appeared to be the same sandwich under different lighting.

Ollie chose fries because fries did not pretend to be a meal.

They found a table meant for four people and made it hold five by putting Samir’s equipment case upright against the wall.

Aoife set her recorder on the table.

Maya pointed at it.

“Are we content?”

“Not currently.”

“Good.”

Ethan had his tablet open between the ketchup and a cup of ice.

Ollie looked at him.

Ethan looked back.

“What?”

“You brought a spreadsheet to fries.”

“It’s not a spreadsheet.”

Aoife leaned over.

“It has columns.”

“It’s a route schedule.”

“With columns.”

“That doesn’t make it a spreadsheet.”

Samir ate one of Ethan’s fries.

Ethan moved the tray away.

Samir took another before it got far enough.

Maya was messaging someone Ollie did not know. Her screen flashed a short video of a room with curved walls and three people bouncing something between two different floor levels.

“Vela?” Aoife asked.

Maya nodded. “A girl from the youth mobility group. She says the transfer queues are worse this month because of ring work.”

Ethan looked up immediately.

“Which ring?”

Maya put her phone face down.

“No.”

“I asked one question.”

“You asked it in the voice.”

“What voice?”

Four people did not answer.

Ollie ate another fry.

The five of them had become very good at this particular silence.

A boarding announcement sounded overhead, followed by a second announcement telling a completely different group not to proceed to the gate yet.

Ollie’s residency band pulsed once against his wrist.

DUBLIN ARRIVAL: OCCUPATIONAL TRANSFER BRIEFING
ASSIGNMENT DETAILS PENDING

Below it was a note:

PERSONAL FABRICATION ACCESS SUBJECT TO LOCAL ALLOCATION

He thought of the warped prototype wheel in his bag.

Forty grams of useless plastic crossing the Atlantic because he had decided it could not be replaced.

“Did anyone else get fabrication access?” he asked.

Maya checked. “No.”

Samir shook his head.

Aoife said, “I have media storage.”

Ethan had already opened his. “I have systems orientation and shared residency block.”

“Shared residency?” Maya asked.

“We all have that.”

“I don’t.”

“You probably do.”

“I’m looking at my screen.”

Ethan reached for his own tablet as if more data would resolve Maya’s.

Aoife put one finger on the edge of it.

“Eat.”

“I am eating.”

“You are holding one fry.”

“I was about to eat it.”

“Live your truth.”

Ollie looked down at his food before he smiled.

His tablet lit up on the table.

RESCUE ROVER TEAM

For one second he expected another question.

Instead there was a video.

Priya had the rover on the floor. Lidia was kneeling beside the left wheel, one hand on the suspension housing. Someone off camera counted down.

The rover rolled forward.

Stopped.

Turned.

Climbed the low obstacle ramp they had been fighting with for three weeks.

No drift.

The turn was cleaner than Ollie’s last version.

A second message appeared.

Priya: new cable route fixed it. also moved the strain relief 12mm. way better.

Then:

Jamal: you were making it too complicated

Ollie watched the video again.

The strain relief had been moved twelve millimeters in exactly the wrong direction according to the layout he had drawn in December.

It also worked.

He could see why.

Moving the anchor changed the bend radius under full compression. It reduced the load on the cable sheath and gave the wheel assembly a cleaner path when the suspension twisted.

His first thought was that they needed to update the reference drawing.

His second was that the new route might rub against the service cover after repeated impacts.

He zoomed in.

Probably not.

Maybe.

He typed:

Watch clearance at—

He stopped.

Priya had already posted another photo.

The service cover was off.

A strip of temporary abrasion tape marked the possible contact point.

Priya: already checking 50-cycle

Ollie sat back.

Samir glanced at the screen.

“They broke it?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“They fixed it.”

“Also good.”

Ollie watched the little rover climb the ramp again.

“They changed my cable route.”

Maya looked over. “Did yours not work?”

“It worked.”

“Then they made it work more?”

“That is not a technical category.”

“It is now.”

Ethan looked at the video. “That’s good, though.”

There it was again.

Good.

As if Ollie needed the result certified.

He knew Ethan did not mean it that way.

Which was a useless fact, because it still landed that way.

Ollie locked the screen.

“They should keep it.”

Ethan frowned. “Keep what?”

“The route.”

“I assume they will.”

“Right.”

Aoife watched him for half a second too long.

Ollie took a fry.

She looked away.

That was worse than asking.

The boarding notice changed again.

Their five bands chimed almost together, but not with the same message.

Ollie got:

WORK-PLACEMENT TRAVEL GROUP B
GATE 42
REPORT IN 18 MINUTES

Aoife got a media-storage verification desk first.

Samir’s sport case had to go to oversized-equipment screening.

Maya had been reassigned to a mobility-prep group boarding from the same gate ten minutes later.

Ethan looked between their screens.

“Okay. We can meet after—”

Aoife laughed.

“What?”

“We haven’t left Earth and you’re already trying to schedule the reunion.”

“We are going to the same city.”

“Eventually.”

Maya stood and pushed her chair in with her foot.

“I have to go get assessed for my dangerous shoes.”

“They’re not shoes,” Samir said.

Maya looked at him.

Samir picked up the equipment case.

“They’re equipment with foot access.”

“Traitor.”

He followed her toward screening.

Aoife picked up her recorder and shouldered her bag.

“I’ll see you in Dublin.”

Ethan said, “We’re on the same flight.”

“Then this is going to be embarrassing.”

She left.

Ollie looked at his band.

Sixteen minutes.

Ethan closed the route schedule.

For once, he did not offer a plan.

“Maintenance,” he said instead.

Ollie looked at him.

Ethan raised both hands slightly. “Not authorization. Just—good.”

The annoyance was still there.

So was something else.

Ollie nodded.

“Yeah.”

Ethan headed toward his own check-in lane.

Ollie was left with the fries, the upright equipment case’s empty spot, and the small mess five people made when they stopped being in one place.

On his tablet, the maker chat showed another photo.

Fifty-cycle test complete.

No abrasion.

No question for him.

He tapped the thumbs-up reaction.

Then he put the tablet away and carried his bag toward Gate 42.

The gray calibration jig was still on a shelf in Virginia.

The rover did not need him to be there.

He was not sure yet whether that was freedom or a problem.

At the gate, the windows showed wet January pavement, service trucks, baggage carts, and an aircraft waiting under a low gray sky.

Last ground, Ollie thought.

Then somebody behind him said, “Move, please,” because he had stopped in the middle of the boarding lane.

He moved.
