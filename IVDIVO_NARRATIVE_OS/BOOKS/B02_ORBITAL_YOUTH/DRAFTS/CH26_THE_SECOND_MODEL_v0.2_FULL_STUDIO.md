ORBITAL YOUTH
Chapter 26 — THE SECOND MODEL

Tomas was on light-duty handling with his left hand and apparently considered the phrase an insult.

“Give me the filter case.”

“No.”

“Ollie.”

“You have a restriction.”

“I have a forearm restriction.”

“You have a manual-service restriction.”

Tomas looked down at the case between them.

It contained eight replacement filters, two seal cartridges and a diagnostic coupler that weighed less than the argument.

“You read the whole reassignment note?”

“Yes.”

“Of course you did.”

Ollie lifted the case.

They were halfway through a mature residential module three rings away from Ring Six, replacing service filters because the morning crew had lost forty minutes to a route closure and somebody had decided the light-duty worker and the Youth Cohort analyst under review hold made an efficient afternoon pair.

Tomas had called that decision creative.

Ollie had not asked whether he meant it positively.

The corridor smelled faintly of heated polymer from an access-panel repair. A household door stood open across from them. Two children were building something from food containers and had already been told twice that the corridor rail was not structural material.

One of them waited until the adults looked away and touched it again.

Tomas noticed.

He did nothing.

Ollie noticed that too.

“You are supposed to be checking the return grille.”

“I am.”

“You are watching children commit infrastructure crime.”

“They are not damaging it.”

“Growth.”

Ollie set the filter case beside the wall and opened the service panel.

His band vibrated.

REVIEW QUERY RESPONSE AVAILABLE.

He left it closed.

Tomas saw the header.

“Open it.”

“I am working.”

“You have spent three days trying to get that answer.”

“I am still working.”

Tomas used his good hand to point at the grille.

“Then work faster.”

Ollie removed the first filter.

The first filter came out packed with ordinary dust and household debris, enough to make Ollie wonder what people believed ventilation systems did with reality.

He replaced it, checked the seal, logged the pressure difference and moved to the second.

Tomas waited without pretending not to.

When the panel closed, Ollie opened the review response.

It contained no incident file.

Good.

He had asked for no incident file.

It contained a bounded comparison table routed through Mara’s review request.

Six mature modules.

Two control generations.

Thirty days.

De-identified event order only.

The first line of the response was almost aggressive in its caution.

NO GENERALIZED EFFECT ESTABLISHED.

Ollie read the table twice.

Most of the cases were useless to the pattern he wanted.

Several modules had resident complaints with no later fixed change.

Several had fixed corrections with no prior complaint.

Two had both in the same service period but in the wrong order.

One control generation produced so much ordinary timing variation that the apparent lag disappeared inside it.

Then there was the smaller subset.

Return correction active.

Service transition changed.

A complaint or interface event entered before the fixed display settled.

Not every time.

Not every module.

Not enough.

He filtered one module out.

The cluster almost disappeared.

Ollie stared at the result.

Tomas leaned against the wall.

“Bad?”

“Useful.”

“That is your bad face.”

“It means the module may be carrying most of the apparent effect.”

“Which means?”

“It means I cannot call it a general timing relationship.”

Tomas nodded as if Ollie had told him the food counter was closed.

“You sound disappointed.”

“I am recalibrating.”

Tomas laughed hard enough to regret it and held his injured arm against his body.

Ollie looked at him.

“Are you okay?”

“Yes.”

“That answer has poor evidence.”

“I am surrounded by apprentices.”

A service alert appeared above the corridor junction.

ROUTE C — TEMPORARY HOLD. SERVICE CART REVERSAL REQUIRED.

Tomas looked toward it.

“Your next filter is on the other side.”

Ollie picked up the case.

A delivery cart appeared at the junction, stopped, backed into a side pocket and waited while two residents crossed from the opposite connector. The worker did not follow the route diagram displayed on the wall. They looked at the actual corridor, saw a child standing too close to the corner and reversed farther than the marked holding point.

Then another service worker came through, signaled with two fingers and sent the cart forward by a different line.

The wall still showed the original route.

Ollie stopped walking.

Tomas kept going three steps before noticing.

“What?”

“The cart.”

“It moved.”

“It reversed before the displayed change.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because there were people in the way.”

“That is not in the route state.”

Tomas looked at the wall.

Then at Ollie.

“No. It is in the corridor.”

The cart passed them.

Ollie opened a blank model layer.

Tomas said, “Do not build a theory in front of the filters.”

“I am writing one line.”

“You are incapable of one line.”

Ollie typed:

MOVEMENT ORDER MAY PRECEDE DISPLAYED ROUTE STATE.

Then locked the band.

Tomas pointed at the case.

“See? You can learn.”

* * *

The second model did not begin as a model.

It began as seven columns Ollie disliked.

He worked on them after shift in a public study bay because his review hold still blocked the commissioning analysis rooms and because Mara had specifically said that being under review did not create a right to find a more convenient door.

Fixed trace.

Return correction.

Service transition.

Movement order.

Privacy-safe early timing.

Module and control generation.

Confidence.

The first model had been cleaner.

That was one of its attractions.

Transition overlap in.

Fixed lag out.

The new sheet looked like somebody had emptied a maintenance drawer into a table.

Ollie added the six-module comparison.

Negative cases first.

He made himself do that before the weak cluster.

The result became less impressive immediately.

Good.

He hated that good was becoming the word for things that made his work harder.

The source-bound timing correction arrived only as an authorized review note.

EARLY WARNING EVENT — DE-IDENTIFIED TIMING CORRECTION ACCEPTED.

No recording.

No source context.

No name.

The corrected event started earlier than the original reconstruction.

Ollie placed it in the timing layer and did not try to infer what the missing context might have been.

His hand paused over the empty field anyway.

He closed it.

Then came the review-routed movement-order result.

Not Samir’s practice data.

Not a band export.

Not team movement.

One Service Transfer 4 note/order Samir had chosen to provide, matched by review staff to controller timing already held by the system.

The review summary was careful.

HELD / REVERSED TRANSITION ORDER PRECEDES DISPLAYED FIXED CORRECTION IN ONE INDEPENDENT CASE.

NO GENERALIZATION.

Ollie put it beside the mature-module subset.

The times did not match perfectly.

The ordering did.

The ordering held his attention.

He moved the rows.

Then moved them back.

If he aligned them by the fixed display, the early events scattered.

If he aligned them by service-transition start, the subset tightened slightly and then broke across control generations.

If he aligned them by movement reversal—

Ollie stopped.

He tried again without the strongest mature module.

The pattern weakened.

Still there in pieces.

Not enough.

He added a large field at the top.

SECOND MODEL — QUESTIONS ONLY.

A chair scraped beside him.

Iri sat down with a service pouch between their feet.

“You are using my phrase.”

“It is not your phrase.”

“You looked offended when I said it.”

“That does not establish ownership.”

Iri leaned toward the display.

Ollie reduced the resident-timing layer before they could see anything they were not cleared to see.

Iri noticed that.

Did not comment.

“Seven columns,” they said.

“Yes.”

“Your first model had three.”

“Yes.”

“Recovery.”

Ollie ignored that.

He pointed to the movement-order column.

“Today I watched a service cart reverse before the displayed route state changed.”

“That happens constantly.”

“Why is it not in the planned sequence?”

“Because planned sequence is what the system permits. Movement is what people actually do inside permission.”

“That distinction is too broad.”

“Then make it less broad.”

Ollie rotated the sheet toward them.

“What variable am I missing?”

Iri stared at him.

He waited.

“What?” he said.

“You asked.”

“Yes.”

“Before finishing.”

“Yes.”

“Are you ill?”

Ollie closed his eyes for one second.

“Iri.”

They looked at the table again.

“Route substitution is not one thing.”

“I know.”

“No, you know the words. Look at what people do.”

They tapped the movement-order column without touching the screen.

“Hold because someone is crossing. Reverse because a cart is wrong. Resume because the path clears. Cross before the display updates. Wait after the display says go because the worker does not trust the angle. Same route state. Different order.”

Ollie wrote:

HOLD / REVERSE / RESUME / CROSS-BEFORE-DISPLAY.

Iri said, “Those are examples.”

“I know.”

“Do not turn four verbs into physics.”

He deleted the heading.

Iri smiled.

“Better.”

“What would you call it?”

“I would not.”

“That is unhelpful.”

“You are welcome.”

They stood.

“Rehearsal?” Ollie asked.

“Work first. Rehearsal later.”

“Which work?”

Iri pointed at the service pouch.

“The kind that does not care about your model.”

They left.

Ollie looked at the seven columns.

Then added an eighth.

ACTUAL MOVEMENT ORDER RELATIVE TO CONTROL STATE.

He did not like the length.

He left it.

* * *

Maya complicated it again the next afternoon.

They were not meeting about the model.

Maya was standing in a transfer queue eating something from a paper sleeve while trying to persuade her host household that a late practice visit did not require changing the return condition they had agreed after the last argument.

Her band was open.

HOST: zone stays same if route remains available

MAYA: yes

HOST: and if it closes?

MAYA: named contact + message before reroute

HOST: correct

Maya typed:

MAYA: i hate how pleased you sound

Then noticed Ollie.

“You saw nothing.”

“I saw a functional agreement.”

“Worse.”

The queue moved.

Ollie followed because they were going to the same connector.

“Question.”

“No.”

“You do not know the question.”

“I have learned from experience.”

“It is about route changes.”

Maya stopped eating.

“That is suspiciously close to my interests.”

“When a route closes at night, do you follow the substitute route exactly?”

She looked at him as if he had asked whether people used doors.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because sometimes the substitute is stupid.”

“Define stupid.”

“Full. Slow. Wrong direction for where your friend actually is. Makes you miss the connection after it. Puts you through a warm transition when everyone is already moving that way.”

“That last one.”

“What?”

“You choose around environmental load?”

“Sometimes.”

“Consciously?”

Maya thought.

“Not like an engineer.”

“I did not ask if you were an engineer.”

“You looked like you wanted to.”

The connector arrived.

They stepped inside.

Maya held the rail correctly now and pretended not to notice that Ollie noticed.

He said, “What changes first? The route instruction or what people start doing?”

“Depends.”

“On what?”

“Who notices.”

“That is not a system variable.”

“It is if the system contains people.”

Ollie looked at her.

Maya chewed.

“Do not make that a quote.”

“I am not writing a philosophy book.”

“Good. Nobody would finish it.”

The connector changed acceleration.

Three passengers shifted before the floor cue because an older rider near the door had already braced.

Maya pointed with the food sleeve.

“That.”

Ollie watched the sequence.

One body moved.

Others copied.

The cue followed.

Not a failure.

Not even unusual.

But not the order the interface represented.

He added nothing to the model until he was off the connector.

Then he wrote:

CONTROL STATE ≠ MOVEMENT ORDER.

Below it:

SOCIAL / OPERATIONAL PROPAGATION MAY CHANGE ORDER BEFORE DISPLAY.

He stared at the second line.

Deleted social.

Kept operational.

* * *

Aoife refused to help him infer anything.

That made her useful to ask.

She was editing a school assignment in a food volume with the recorder case under the table. The empty ninety-two-second gap in her documentary had become shorter but had not disappeared.

Ollie sat opposite her.

“I need the scope of the timing correction.”

“No.”

“I do not need the source context.”

“Good, because you are not getting it.”

“I need to know whether the accepted review note can be treated as an event start or only an upper bound.”

Aoife stopped editing.

“That is a real question.”

“Yes.”

“Annoying.”

She opened the authorization copy, not the original material.

The visible line contained exactly what review had accepted.

DE-IDENTIFIED WARNING TIMING MAY BE USED FOR INCIDENT-SEQUENCE COMPARISON.

No more.

Aoife said, “It is the earliest point the source is willing to stand behind as a warning event. Not necessarily the first thing they felt. Not permission to infer anything before it.”

Ollie typed:

AUTHORIZED EARLIEST CONFIRMED WARNING — NOT ONSET.

Aoife read it upside down.

“Fine.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me like I gave you access.”

“You gave me a boundary.”

“You make boundaries sound like equipment.”

He closed the note.

Aoife looked at his model header.

“Second model?”

“Questions only.”

“That is either growth or branding.”

“Iri said something similar.”

“Then probably branding.”

She went back to the assignment.

Ollie stood.

Aoife said, without looking up, “Are you going to ask Samir?”

“No.”

Aoife looked up.

“The review already has the movement-order result he agreed to provide. I do not need anything else from him.”

Aoife watched him for a second.

Then nodded once.

No praise.

Good.

He left before he could become pleased with himself for meeting a basic standard.

* * *

The model became less elegant over the next two days.

It also became harder to kill.

Ollie pre-registered the windows before checking the strongest cases again.

He marked which comparisons would count against his hypothesis.

Matched movement-order change without later delayed fixed response.

Delayed response with no relevant ordering change.

Effect disappearing when module and control generation were matched.

Early warning timing failing to lead fixed movement more often than the control window.

The negative cases stayed on the same page as the positives.

He was tempted to move them to an appendix.

He did not.

The resulting hypothesis fit in two sentences.

A delayed fixed response may require an ordered interaction between return correction, service transition and actual movement-order change before the displayed control state catches up.

Resident or personal-interface reports may precede fixed sensing in some instances, but are neither necessary nor sufficient evidence of that coupled state.

Ollie read it aloud.

It sounded much less impressive than the first model.

He trusted it more.

The reproduction protocol was harder.

He could not ask to recreate the occupied failure.

Nobody would.

So he removed consequences one by one.

No household occupancy.

No uncontrolled public route.

Low load.

Adult safety lead.

Predefined stop.

Matched control state.

One deliberately held service transition.

One reversed/resumed movement order.

No need for resident medical data.

Willing participants only where a human movement input was necessary.

Fixed monitoring plus the same privacy-safe report channel used in review.

If the ordering changed and nothing followed, the model lost support.

If the fixed response appeared without the ordering, the model lost support differently.

If a body/interface report appeared early, it was recorded.

If none appeared, that was also recorded.

He did not write success anywhere in the protocol.

* * *

Mara read the second model standing up.

That was unfair because Ollie had spent two days on it and she turned the pages in under four minutes.

They were in a small review office beside a maintenance intake desk. People kept entering to collect assignments. Somebody behind the partition was arguing about a missing tool return.

Ollie waited.

Mara reached the negative cases.

Stopped.

“Why are these first?”

“So I do not forget what breaks the pattern.”

“Better answer than I expected.”

She continued.

At the review-routed movement-order line, she checked the source label.

“Review-routed output only?”

“Yes.”

“No team data?”

“No.”

“No personal-band window?”

“No.”

“No inference beyond the agreed movement-order comparison?”

“No.”

She moved on.

At Aoife’s source timing she asked the same questions in different form.

Ollie answered them.

At the hypothesis she read it twice.

Then looked at him.

“What is the mechanism?”

“I do not know.”

“What are the coupled quantities?”

“I do not know yet.”

“What does your model predict?”

“An increased probability of delayed fixed response when return correction, service-transition change and actual movement-order reversal or hold-resume sequence occur in the tested order before displayed state closure.”

“Probability?”

“Yes.”

“You have enough data for a probability?”

“No.”

Ollie deleted the word.

Mara waited.

He rewrote:

The model predicts that the delayed response should be more reproducible under the ordered condition than under matched controls.

“Better,” she said.

He hated how much relief one word could create.

She reached the falsifiers.

“Good.”

Then the protocol.

“Who designed this?”

“I did.”

“Who approved it?”

“No one.”

“Correct.”

She marked three sections.

“Safety will redesign the load boundaries. Operations will decide whether the held/reversed movement sequence is physically representative. Participant protections need review. You are not running it.”

“I know.”

“You said that quickly.”

“I knew before I came in.”

Mara studied him.

Ollie kept his mouth closed.

She tapped his status line.

REVIEW HOLD.

It did not change.

He had known it would not.

Knowing did not improve the feeling.

“I can monitor one timing channel,” he said.

“That is not your decision.”

“No.”

“Are you asking?”

Ollie stopped.

The old version of the conversation was available immediately.

The model is mine.

I found the pattern.

I need to see the test.

He closed all three before speaking.

“I am asking whether a bounded analyst role would improve test continuity enough to justify including me.”

Mara’s expression did not change.

“That is a legitimate question.”

“And?”

“And the adult test team will answer it.”

He waited.

She waited back.

Ollie nodded.

“Okay.”

Mara closed the model.

“Proposal goes to safety review.”

His status line still read REVIEW HOLD. The model had earned review, not reinstatement.

He picked up his band.

Mara said, “Ollie.”

He turned.

“The second model is better.”

He felt the answer rising.

How much better?

Better enough for access?

Better enough to matter?

He did not ask.

“Good,” he said.

Mara’s mouth moved slightly.

“Go finish your shift.”

Tomas was waiting beside a service cart with three filter boxes and a look of personal betrayal.

“You are late.”

“Review.”

“I know. I still had filters.”

Ollie took the first box.

Tomas pushed the cart with his good hand.

“Did they give you your access back?”

“No.”

“Did you argue?”

“No.”

Tomas stopped the cart.

Ollie kept walking two steps before noticing.

“What?”

“I want the medical liaison to document this.”

“Move the cart.”

“Historic event.”

“Tomas.”

He pushed again.

They reached the next panel.

Ollie set the model aside and opened the filter case.

Behind him, his band vibrated once.

TEST PROPOSAL — RECEIVED FOR SAFETY DESIGN REVIEW.

He did not open it.

The first filter was overdue.
