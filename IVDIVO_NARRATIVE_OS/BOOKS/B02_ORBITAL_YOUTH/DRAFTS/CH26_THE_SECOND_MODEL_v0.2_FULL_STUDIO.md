# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 26 — THE SECOND MODEL

Ollie was allowed to repair a waste-line sensor and not allowed to see the Ring Six incident record.

The distinction had become very clear.

The sensor lived behind a panel in an occupied module two rings away from anything important enough to appear in a briefing. It had been reporting a fill estimate six percent higher than the physical inspection for three days.

Nobody had evacuated.

Nobody had called a review board.

A resident had complained that the service warning appeared before breakfast every morning and made the wall panel orange while they were trying to eat.

Ollie replaced the sensor mount.

The reading dropped five percent.

Iri looked at the service tablet.

“Congratulations.”

“It is still one percent high.”

“The tank is not a theorem.”

“It is a measurement.”

“It is holding waste.”

“Those are compatible statements.”

The resident, standing in the kitchen entrance, said, “Can the wall stop being orange now?”

Ollie checked the state.

“Yes.”

“Best thing you’ve done all week.”

“Thank you.”

The resident left.

Iri clipped the panel shut.

“There. Recognition.”

Ollie put the old mount into the return pouch.

“That does not count.”

“You were thanked by a person whose breakfast improved.”

“I meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

Ollie did not finish.

His work band vibrated.

Not Ring Six access.

Still not.

INCIDENT REVIEW — MODELING RESPONSE AVAILABLE.

He looked at it long enough for Iri to notice.

“You can open messages and walk.”

“I can also hit a wall.”

“You have practiced that less than Samir.”

Ollie opened the message.

The review had not sent him incident data.

It had sent six bounded outputs.

Each one had a source label, purpose restriction and confidence note.

No names.

No personal route histories.

No raw body-support feeds.

No full Ring Six replay.

The first was the mature-module comparison Mara had routed through review after Ollie’s maintenance query.

Six modules.

Two control generations.

Thirty days.

Most cases did nothing useful.

That line remained at the top.

NEGATIVE CASES DOMINATE.

Ollie read it again.

The weak subset remained exactly as weak as before: several reports clustered near combined return-correction and service-transition states, but the effect changed sharply by module and control generation. Remove one module and the apparent timing relationship nearly disappeared.

The second output was the source-bound correction Aoife had passed through incident review.

Only timing.

No recording.

No route.

No accommodation detail.

EARLY REPORT WINDOW PRECEDES OFFICIAL RECONSTRUCTION MARKER.

The third was Samir’s Service Transfer Four comparison.

HELD / REVERSED MOVEMENT STATE ACTIVE BEFORE FIXED CORRECTION DISPLAY.

No exact subjective onset.

No team data.

The remaining three outputs were mostly reasons not to get excited.

Two negative comparisons.

One local-support event class that could not be aligned tightly enough to anything else.

Ollie scrolled to the bottom.

REVIEW QUESTION:

CAN THESE EVIDENCE CLASSES SUPPORT A FALSIFIABLE MODELING PROPOSAL WITHOUT EXPANDING DATA ACCESS?

He stopped walking.

Iri nearly walked into him.

“You chose the wall option.”

Ollie turned the display.

Iri did not take it.

“What are you allowed to see?”

“Only these outputs.”

“Good.”

“I know.”

“That sounded disappointed.”

“It is enough.”

“That sounded worse.”

Ollie read the review question again.

Not:

What happened?

Not:

Fix Ring Six.

A modeling proposal.

No expanded access.

He felt the old irritation first.

Then something else.

Relief, maybe.

If the model failed, he would not be able to solve the failure by opening another dataset.

He read the question again.

* * *

The first model had one center.

That had been the problem.

Ollie had drawn the building states in the middle and everything else around them.

Fixed sensors.

Partition states.

Movement.

Then an annotation rail where resident reports and personal-interface events waited until somebody could translate them into the same language.

He did not do that this time.

He had one hour between maintenance shifts and a workroom table that was technically available for training review.

Mara had approved the room.

Not the model.

Ollie wrote that at the top.

AUTHORIZED TASK:
MODELING PROPOSAL ONLY.

NO INCIDENT-REVIEW ACCESS.

NO LIVE RING SIX ACCESS.

He considered adding a fourth line.

NO PRETENDING THIS MEANS REINSTATEMENT.

He did not.

The screen divided into layers.

FIXED ENVIRONMENTAL STATE.

PARTITION / SERVICE STATE.

MOVEMENT ORDER.

PRIVACY-SAFE TIMING ANCHORS.

EARLY RESIDENT / BODY / SUPPORT RESPONSE CLASS.

SOURCE CONSTRAINT.

NEGATIVE CASES.

CONFIDENCE.

He stared at them.

The problem was obvious.

They were not the same kind of thing.

A fixed return sensor gave a synchronized number.

A service log gave a commanded or observed machine state.

Samir gave movement order and an approximate timestamp from a note he had made at the time.

The source correction gave one bounded earlier warning window.

A resident might say air felt dry, pressure felt wrong, breathing support adapted, or nothing at all.

Some people noticed early.

Some did not.

Some systems responded locally before a person noticed.

Some personal systems were not present.

Some negative cases had the same building states and no complaint.

If Ollie combined them into one confidence score, the score would lie politely.

He deleted the combined score.

Ren appeared at the workroom door.

“Was that important?”

“What?”

“You looked angry when you deleted it.”

“It was a bad idea.”

“Historic.”

“I have had bad ideas before.”

“Not voluntarily.”

Ren stepped inside carrying two sealed food packs.

“Iri said you were building Model Two.”

“I did not call it that.”

“You named the file?”

Ollie looked at the top.

MODEL_SECOND_WORKING.

Ren smiled.

“That is worse.”

“It is temporary.”

“Everything becomes permanent after somebody writes temporary.”

“Why are you here?”

“Food.”

“For me?”

“No.”

Ren put one pack on the opposite side of the table and opened the other.

“Then why—”

“Kalen is late.”

“So you are eating his food?”

“I am preventing temperature degradation.”

“That is theft.”

“Maintenance.”

Ollie turned back to the screen.

Ren watched for ten seconds.

“What does that column mean?”

“Negative cases.”

“Why is it the biggest one?”

“Because there are more of them.”

“Then why are you looking at the small ones?”

Ollie stopped.

“Because the small ones might tell us what changes.”

“Or they are small because nothing changes.”

“Yes.”

Ren ate.

Ren kept eating.

The negative cases stayed where they were.

Ollie left them there too.

He replayed the mature-module output.

The weak cluster did not give him a universal lead time.

Samir’s case did not give him a body warning.

Aoife’s source correction did not give him route structure.

But the three classes had one thing in common that was not a number.

Order.

Something local happened.

Then something else.

Then the fixed display represented the state later.

Not always.

Not in most cases.

Only under some combinations.

Ollie added a field.

RELATIVE EVENT ORDER.

Then another.

STATE EQUIVALENCE UNKNOWN.

He moved the early-response layer into the same horizontal sequence as the building states instead of leaving it on an annotation rail.

Ren looked at the screen.

“That looks uglier.”

“Yes.”

“Good?”

“I think so.”

“That is disappointing.”

Kalen arrived at the door.

“You ate my food.”

Ren held up the empty pack.

“It was degrading.”

Kalen looked at Ollie.

“You allowed this?”

“I was modeling.”

“Of course.”

The two of them left arguing.

Ollie kept the ugly screen.

* * *

Tomas was not supposed to be in the service passage.

He was supposed to be at light-duty coordination.

This meant he was standing in the service passage holding a tablet and telling two workers where not to put a cart.

Ollie found him there because Mara had approved one question.

One.

Not an interview.

Not an informal incident reconstruction.

A question about service substitutions in mature occupied modules.

Tomas saw him coming.

“No.”

“I haven’t asked.”

“That has never protected anyone.”

“I have one approved question.”

“Worse.”

Ollie showed the request header.

Tomas read it.

“Fine.”

“How often does a service route change without becoming a route event?”

Tomas looked down the passage.

A cleaning cart blocked half the wall seam.

“Now.”

“That’s not a route change.”

“Correct.”

“Then—”

“People go around it.”

“Yes.”

“So their route changed.”

“But the system doesn’t log—”

“A closure? No.”

Ollie waited.

Tomas shifted the tablet into his left hand. His forearm was still braced.

The movement was small.

Ollie noticed anyway.

“How’s the arm?”

“That is a second question.”

“Sorry.”

“It is attached.”

“Good.”

“Debatable.”

A worker moved the cart.

Tomas pointed with the tablet.

“Service work changes choreography all the time without changing official routing. Cleaning. Deliveries. Inspection stands. Slow mobility crossing. Someone opens a panel and occupies half a corridor. Nobody files a transit event because transit is still open.”

“Can it change partition order?”

“Sometimes.”

“Do you know how often?”

“No.”

“Could we estimate—”

Tomas lifted one eyebrow.

Ollie stopped.

“One question.”

“You have already used four.”

“Right.”

Tomas looked at the approved request again.

“What are you trying to distinguish?”

Ollie hesitated.

The old answer would have been the model.

He said, “Whether two routes that look different in logs can put the building into the same local sequence.”

Tomas considered that.

Then pointed farther down the passage.

“You should ask someone who actually walks service routes all day.”

“You?”

“I coordinate them now.”

The brace moved slightly as he adjusted the tablet.

“Temporarily.”

Ollie nodded.

He did not say sorry again.

Tomas said, “If you put ‘service substitution’ in the model, do not make it a yes/no field.”

“Why?”

“Because moving around a cart is not the same as rerouting a delivery across a climate boundary.”

“That was another answer.”

“You are welcome.”

* * *

Maya refused to help because Ollie called it route behavior.

“I am not route behavior.”

“You are not.”

“You said my night-route correction matters.”

“It might.”

“That is how it starts.”

They were walking toward a food counter between her training block and his maintenance shift. Maya had seven minutes.

Ollie had nine.

This was apparently enough for an argument.

“I need one thing,” he said.

“No.”

“You don’t know what.”

“I know you.”

“During a closure, what makes people take a route the official planner does not predict?”

Maya kept walking.

“Friends.”

Ollie waited.

She looked at him.

“That’s it?”

“No. Food. Somebody you’re trying to avoid. Somebody you’re trying to find. A queue that looks bad. A route that was faster yesterday. A host cutoff. If you’re carrying something. If one corridor feels awful because everyone is coming the other way.”

Ollie opened a note.

Maya slapped the edge of his tablet down.

“No.”

“I’m writing categories.”

“You are turning me into transit.”

“I’m turning the answer into—”

“Exactly.”

He closed the note.

Maya kept walking.

“Also people follow other people.”

“That came up in the Ring Six test.”

“Because humans are lazy.”

“Locals too.”

“Especially locals. They think they know where they’re going.”

Ollie almost wrote that down.

Did not.

Maya looked suspiciously satisfied.

“What do you actually need?”

“A condition that produces the same transition order without the explicit route sequence from my first model.”

“That sounds like a sentence designed to make me leave.”

“It is not.”

“Then fail better.”

Ollie tried again.

“The first model assumed people changed the system because the main partitions changed in a certain order.”

“Okay.”

“But in the failure, the same kind of state may have happened through service movement and people taking side routes.”

“Okay.”

“I need to know what makes side-route behavior ordinary instead of exceptional.”

Maya stopped.

That cost one of her seven minutes.

“You keep saying side route.”

“Yes.”

“What if it isn’t side to the person?”

Ollie looked at her.

“If I’m going from Niko’s host zone to training after a closure, the route the planner calls alternate might be the route everyone I know uses. Your system thinks we deviated. We think Tuesday happened.”

Ollie said nothing.

Maya pointed at him.

“That face is why I hate helping.”

“What face?”

“You got a thing.”

“I got a question.”

“Worse.”

She started walking again.

Ollie followed.

“What question?”

“Whose route defines substitution?”

Maya considered.

“Fine.”

He opened the note.

She did not slap it down this time.

* * *

Aoife would not tell him what the source had said.

Ollie had not asked.

This made the conversation shorter.

They met in a transit waiting volume because Aoife refused to come to a maintenance workroom if she was not being recorded as a consultant.

“You are not a consultant.”

“Good. Then buy me tea.”

“I have no reason to buy you tea.”

“You asked me here.”

“You were already here.”

“Details.”

Ollie bought the cheaper tea.

Aoife looked at it.

“This is hostile.”

“It is tea.”

“That is not a defense.”

He sat.

“I only need the authorized correction.”

“You already have the review output.”

“I know.”

“Then why am I here?”

“Because the output says the first warning window is earlier than the official reconstruction marker.”

“Yes.”

“I want to know whether that correction is about timing confidence or event order.”

Aoife’s expression changed.

Not dramatic.

Closed.

“I can answer only what was authorized.”

“That’s why I asked you.”

She looked at her band.

Then at him.

“The source allowed de-identified timing to incident review. Not the original words. Not the recording. Not the accommodation context.”

“I know.”

“The correction is this: the warning happened before the point the first reconstruction used as the start of the event.”

“How much before?”

“No.”

“I’m not asking for raw—”

“No because the authorized correction does not support a tighter number than review already has.”

Ollie stopped.

“Okay.”

Aoife waited.

He said, “Does it establish what caused the warning?”

“No.”

“Does it establish the warning was about the same environmental variable?”

“No.”

“Does it establish the person was physically in the state my first model marked as causal?”

“No.”

Ollie looked at the table.

Aoife said, “You look happier.”

“It removes things.”

“That is your version of happiness.”

“It means I shouldn’t use the correction to fit a seconds value.”

“Was that going to happen?”

“No.”

Aoife looked at him.

“Was it going to happen after one more screen?”

Ollie ignored that.

“What it gives me is order.”

“Maybe.”

“Early report before official start marker.”

“That is what review says.”

“Not cause.”

“Correct.”

“Not variable identity.”

“Correct.”

“Not exact universal lag.”

“Correct.”

Aoife took a drink.

Ollie looked at her.

“What?”

“You’re being very careful.”

“I broke something by being less careful.”

That ended the joke.

He nodded.

No repair speech.

No reassurance.

Aoife said, “Do not make the source into the person who solved your model.”

“I won’t.”

“You say that like I gave you an instruction.”

“You did.”

“I gave you a warning.”

“Those can overlap.”

She almost smiled.

Did not.

* * *

Iri disliked the second model immediately.

This was useful.

They were in the older service corner with bad seating and better food.

Iri had twenty-three minutes before a rehearsal equipment check.

Ollie had forty before his next maintenance rotation.

The model stayed on his tablet.

Not connected to Ring Six.

Not live.

A proposal.

Iri scrolled through the layers.

Fixed state.

Service state.

Movement order.

Privacy-safe timing anchor.

Early response class.

Confidence.

Negative cases.

Route/service substitution.

Then they went back to the top.

“You made people a column.”

“They were an annotation before.”

“That does not make this good.”

“What is wrong with it?”

“Which people?”

Ollie frowned.

“Resident/body early-response classes.”

“That is not people.”

“It is the observable class.”

Iri pushed the tablet back.

Ollie did not take it.

“What would you call it?”

“I wouldn’t.”

“That is not helpful.”

“Good.”

Ollie waited.

Iri ate.

He waited longer.

Eventually Iri said, “You still want one event that means the same thing every time.”

“I explicitly added confidence and negative cases.”

“Yes.”

“Then no.”

“You called dry, pressure, regulator adaptation and airflow complaint one early-response layer.”

“Because they may all indicate a local boundary state before the fixed return changes.”

“May.”

“Yes.”

“Or three are irrelevant and one matters.”

“Yes.”

“Or all matter for different reasons.”

“Yes.”

“Or none cause anything.”

“Yes.”

Iri looked annoyed that he kept agreeing.

Ollie said, “That’s why the model is a hypothesis.”

“No. That is why it is eight hypotheses wearing one coat.”

Ollie looked at the screen.

He had no immediate answer.

Iri noticed the delay.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You normally defend faster.”

“I’m checking whether you’re right.”

“That sounds unhealthy.”

Ollie split the early-response layer.

Not by species.

Not by diagnosis.

Not by device.

By source relation.

PERSON-REPORTED ENVIRONMENTAL CHANGE.

PERSONAL SUPPORT / LOCAL INTERFACE RESPONSE.

LOCAL SERVICE-BOUNDARY OBSERVATION.

NO EARLY RESPONSE OBSERVED.

Iri leaned closer.

“Negative case inside the same layer.”

“Yes.”

“Better.”

Ollie looked at them.

Iri sighed.

“Do not enjoy that.”

“What else is missing?”

Iri sat back.

“That is a dangerous question.”

“I mean it.”

“You said that last time.”

“Last time I meant ‘what variable proves my model.’”

“And now?”

“What would make this fail before we build a test around it?”

Iri stopped eating.

The room around them kept moving.

Two maintenance workers argued quietly about a tool return.

Someone at the far table was asleep with one hand around a drink.

A child passed the entrance twice in opposite directions, followed by an adult who looked increasingly tired.

Iri looked at the service map on the wall.

“Your routes.”

“Maya said that.”

“Maya means social routes.”

“What do you mean?”

“Service state.”

“That’s already there.”

“No. Scheduled service state is there.”

Ollie waited.

Iri pointed at the child and adult crossing the doorway again.

“People go around whatever is actually in front of them. Service does too.”

“Tomas said not to make substitution binary.”

“Good.”

“So what am I missing?”

Iri thought.

“Equivalent states.”

Ollie did not write.

Iri continued.

“A door does not care why six people arrive while local control is correcting. It does not care whether they came through the planned connector, followed a delivery cart, avoided cleaning, or doubled back because someone forgot food.”

“That’s movement order.”

“No. You still name the route first.”

Ollie looked at his own model.

Iri pointed at the first-model sequence he had kept for comparison.

MAIN PARTITION A.

COUNTERFLOW.

RETURN CORRECTION.

SERVICE TRANSITION B.

LOCAL ADAPT.

FIXED DISPLAY.

“You named the doors,” Iri said.

“Yes.”

“What if different doors make the same load relationship?”

Ollie’s hand stopped above the screen.

Iri saw.

“Do not make that face.”

“What face?”

“The one.”

“I hate this city.”

“No, you don’t.”

Ollie changed the labels.

Not doors.

STATE RELATIONSHIPS.

LOCAL DEMAND CHANGING.

RETURN CORRECTION ACTIVE.

MOVEMENT LOAD REDIRECTED.

SECOND LOCAL ADAPTATION ENTERS BEFORE SETTLE.

FIXED / AGGREGATE REPRESENTATION LAGS LOCAL STATE.

He did not like the last line.

Too broad.

He marked it:

CANDIDATE / MUST FALSIFY.

Iri read.

“That is less wrong.”

“Thank you.”

“That was not praise.”

“Still counts.”

“No.”

* * *

Mara read the proposal in silence.

This took eleven minutes.

Ollie knew because the clock was in his peripheral vision and because every second after eight felt like a separate administrative process.

They were not in Ring Six.

They were in a review room beside maintenance coordination.

No live systems.

No privileged screen.

Mara’s tablet.

Ollie’s proposal.

Nothing else.

At minute seven, Mara made one annotation.

At minute nine, another.

At minute eleven, she looked up.

“What do you want?”

Ollie had prepared for the technical questions.

He had not prepared for that.

“A controlled reproduction.”

“That is in the document.”

“Yes.”

“What do you want?”

He knew the answer.

Access.

Ring Six.

His old station.

The right to see the system move again.

He said, “For review to test the hypothesis.”

Mara watched him.

“That was a better answer than the first one in your head.”

Ollie did not ask how she knew.

She turned the proposal toward him.

“You have improved the model.”

“I think so.”

“You have improved the question more.”

Ollie waited.

“Do not look pleased yet.”

He stopped looking pleased.

Mara tapped the negative-case section.

“You kept these.”

“They can falsify it.”

“You kept the module-generation effect.”

“Yes.”

“You did not force Samir’s case into a lead-time number.”

“No.”

“You preserved source restrictions.”

“Yes.”

“You split person-reported events from interface/support events.”

“Yes.”

“You added service-route equivalence.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

The word landed exactly where Ollie did not want it to.

He said, “So—”

“No.”

He stopped.

Mara almost smiled.

“No reinstatement.”

“I didn’t ask.”

“You arrived asking.”

“That is not—”

He stopped again.

Mara waited.

Ollie looked at the proposal.

“Okay.”

“Your review hold remains.”

“Okay.”

“If safety accepts the reproduction concept, the test will be adult-designed.”

“Okay.”

“You may be offered a bounded observation or timing role because you wrote the hypothesis.”

“Okay.”

“You will not have unrestricted incident or Ring Six access.”

“Okay.”

“You are saying okay too fast.”

Ollie looked at her.

“I’m disappointed.”

“Good.”

“That is a strange response.”

“It means you understood the sentence.”

She closed the proposal.

Ollie did not argue.

The absence of argument felt physically incomplete, like leaving a tool on a bench and walking away.

Mara opened a routing form.

PROPOSAL → INCIDENT REVIEW / SAFETY TEST DESIGN.

She attached the document.

“Anything else?”

Ollie looked at the status field.

PENDING TECHNICAL REVIEW.

Not approved.

Not rejected.

Not reinstated.

“No.”

Mara sent it.

His model left his screen without taking him with it.

Ollie watched the status field until Mara closed it.

He stood.

At the door, Mara said, “Ollie.”

He turned.

“Safety will change this.”

“How much?”

“I don’t know. That is their job.”

Ollie nodded.

Outside, Iri was waiting with a percussion-frame clamp in one hand.

“You got access?”

“No.”

Iri looked at his face.

“Good.”

Ollie stared.

“I mean—”

“I know what you mean.”

“You do not.”

“You mean the process is working.”

“Yes.”

“And you are still annoyed.”

“Yes.”

“Excellent.”

“That word is becoming hostile.”

Iri handed him the broken clamp.

“Hold this.”

“Why?”

“I need both hands.”

“For what?”

“To fix something that is actually broken.”

Ollie held the clamp.

Iri opened a small tool roll on the floor.

Ollie’s band vibrated.

He checked it.

INCIDENT REVIEW:

SECOND-MODEL PROPOSAL RECEIVED.

NO ACCESS CHANGE.

SAFETY DESIGN REVIEW PENDING.

Ollie locked the message.

Iri said, “Important?”

“Not yet.”

“Good.”

They worked on the clamp.
