# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 7 — STATIC SAFE

The system passed every test Ollie wanted it to fail.

Not catastrophically.

Not even interestingly.

It passed.

Ventilation response stayed inside band.

Partition timing matched specification.

Thermal recovery was boring.

Pressure differential behaved.

Local environmental controls talked to the building.

The building talked back.

The crawler inspection found nothing outside.

The service seals were clean.

At 09:20, Ollie had asked Mara whether he could stay for the afternoon diagnostic block.

Mara had opened his residency training record instead of the test schedule.

“You’re two credits behind.”

“They’re remote.”

“They’re due.”

“I can do them tonight.”

“Then you can have the next extra shift after they’re done.”

That had ended the negotiation.

Three different technicians checked the same transition and found three versions of normal.

By 10:40, Ollie had a list of things that were not wrong.

He disliked the list.

Mara noticed.

“You’re making a face.”

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

“What face?”

“The one people make when they wanted a fault and got a functioning system.”

“I don’t want a fault.”

“You want a cause.”

“That is different.”

“It is until you start preferring the cause.”

Ollie looked at the static test display.

The environmental transition sat between two occupied-use zones that were currently empty.

One side ran slightly cooler.

One side ran slightly warmer.

The air curtain held.

The partition cycled.

A pair of load simulators stood where residents had walked the day before.

Nothing happened.

“Again,” Ollie said.

Mara did not look at him.

“No.”

“We only ran the full sequence twice.”

“We ran component verification before you arrived, static transition four times this morning, and two controlled load cycles.”

“The eleven-second lag didn’t appear.”

“That is not evidence that the test failed.”

“It means we didn’t reproduce the condition.”

Mara looked at him.

“That sentence is better.”

Iri was sitting on the floor near an open service panel, one knee raised, checking a bundle of labeled couplings against a work order.

Iri had arrived twelve minutes late, said only, “My cousin’s activity handoff moved,” and started working before Ollie could ask anything else.

“You can run it fifty times,” Iri said. “Nobody lives like that.”

Ollie turned.

“Like what?”

Iri looked at the two load simulators.

“Standing where you told them.”

“They’re not supposed to simulate behavior. They’re load.”

“Exactly.”

Ollie looked back at the transition.

“Yesterday the occupancy group had bags, children, carts, route changes—”

“Exactly,” Iri said.

Ollie looked back at the transition.

“What would you change?”

She was asking Iri.

Iri put the work order down.

“Movement.”

“What kind?”

“Bad movement.”

Ollie said, “That is not a test condition.”

Iri looked at him.

“It was yesterday.”

Mara crossed her arms.

“Define bad.”

Iri stood.

“People stop where they shouldn’t. They double back. They hold a door because someone is behind them. Someone sees a friend and changes direction. A child decides the floor is interesting.”

“That is not reproducible,” Ollie said.

“No.”

“So we can’t compare.”

Iri shrugged.

“You wanted the cause.”

Ollie looked at Mara.

Mara was already opening a scheduling panel.

“We can do controlled movement.”

“That’s not bad movement,” Iri said.

“No. It is the beginning of finding out which part matters.”

She looked at Ollie.

“You wanted another run.”

“I did.”

“You are not allowed to complain when the run becomes more complicated.”

“I won’t.”

Iri made the respiratory sound again.

This one was definitely a laugh.

* * *

The controlled movement run used twelve people.

Four residents from the earlier test volunteered to come back.

Three local apprentices.

Two housing staff.

One maintenance technician.

Ollie.

Iri.

Mara did not count herself because she remained outside the path.

Nadia Park appeared anyway.

“You are not in this test,” Mara told her.

“I know.”

“Then why are you here?”

“Because four of my future residents are.”

“They are volunteers.”

“That does not erase my calendar.”

Mara looked at the occupancy list.

“Your calendar is not a sensor.”

“No, but it is the thing your sensor keeps moving.”

Ollie pretended not to hear.

The first run was simple.

Enter Sector A.

Cross the environmental transition.

Pause.

Use one service point.

Return by the same route.

The system remained green.

Lea was not there.

Tomas was.

He had volunteered because the test time overlapped badly enough with his shift that Nadia could count it as relocation preparation instead of making him take another trip.

He carried an empty bag.

“Why empty?” Ollie asked.

“Because Nadia said realistic.”

“That’s not realistic.”

“It is after I move.”

“You’ll have things.”

“Not if I lose enough boxes first.”

Nadia said, “He will not.”

Tomas gave Ollie the bag.

“You carry it.”

“Why?”

“Realism.”

Ollie carried it.

Nothing happened.

Second run.

Different order.

Two people paused before the transition.

One returned to the service point.

Iri crossed first.

Tomas followed.

The air curtain corrected.

Fixed display remained inside band.

No complaint.

Third run.

They changed which door opened first.

The local maintenance technician held one partition three seconds longer.

A housing staff member crossed back to retrieve a marked object.

Iri’s regulator pulsed amber.

Ollie saw it.

“What?”

Iri said, “Dry edge.”

The fixed display remained green.

Ollie checked time.

He did not say anything.

Five seconds.

Seven.

Nine.

At ten, the humidity return moved.

Small.

Still within band.

The correction came.

Ollie looked at Mara.

Mara logged it.

“Again,” Ollie said.

This time she nodded.

The fourth run reproduced almost everything.

Same number of people.

Same doors.

Same timing windows.

Different person order.

No lag.

Ollie stared at the trace.

“That should have done it.”

Iri said, “Clearly.”

“It was the same sequence.”

“No.”

“It was.”

“I crossed after Tomas.”

“That doesn’t matter.”

“You don’t know that.”

“Body order?”

“Person order.”

“That is not a variable.”

Iri looked at the display.

“It is if it changes the result.”

Mara said, “We are not naming causes yet.”

Ollie pulled up the movement log.

The test record stored:
- door cycle;
- zone occupancy count;
- service-point load;
- aggregate environmental demand.

It did not store who crossed in what order unless someone had been manually tagged.

That made sense.

The system did not need identity to operate the corridor.

Ollie looked at the video.

Tomas had crossed first in run three.

Then Iri.

Then the housing staff member.

In run four, Iri crossed before Tomas.

A third participant stopped halfway through because someone behind them had said something.

That pause was two seconds.

Maybe three.

“You can’t compare this,” Ollie said.

Mara said, “Correct.”

He hated that answer.

Not because she was wrong.

Because she was exactly right.

“What do we need?”

“A more useful movement record.”

Nadia said, “Without turning residents into tracking tags.”

Ollie looked at her.

“I didn’t say identity.”

“You said person order.”

“That can be anonymous.”

“Good.”

“Can the local movement system export—”

Mara interrupted.

“Ask the question before choosing the dataset.”

Ollie stopped.

The test group was dispersing.

Tomas took his empty bag back.

“Did I help?”

“Yes.”

“Do I get a room?”

“No,” Nadia said.

Tomas nodded.

“Engineering remains disappointing.”

He left.

Iri was checking the regulator log.

Ollie looked over.

“Can I see the timing?”

Iri looked up.

“Which timing?”

“The local response.”

“Why?”

“To compare it to fixed.”

“Compare what?”

“Onset.”

“What are you trying to prove?”

Ollie opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

That was irritating.

Iri waited.

Ollie tried again.

“I want to know whether your regulator is reacting to the same state before the building sees it.”

“That is better.”

“Will you share the timing?”

“Not raw.”

“I don’t need raw.”

“What do you need?”

“Start time. Correction class. No biometric data.”

Iri looked at Mara.

Mara said, “Your choice.”

Iri considered.

Then sent a tiny record to Ollie’s work tablet.

09:52:14.2
LOCAL ENVIRONMENT RESPONSE
CLASS: DRY/WARM TRANSITION
NO BIOMETRICS

Ollie put it beside the fixed return shift.

Ten point eight seconds.

Close to yesterday.

Not proof.

Better than nothing.

“Thank you.”

Iri nodded once.

Then:

“Your bag is still empty.”

Ollie looked down.

He was still holding Tomas’s bag.

“Why did nobody tell me?”

“I did.”

“When?”

“Now.”

* * *

By the time their shift ended, Ollie had fourteen timing notes, three useless theories, and no idea whether any of them would survive another day.

He and Iri ate at a narrow service counter between the maintenance bay and a ringline junction.

The food was cheap by local standards and expensive by whatever Ollie’s instincts still thought food should cost.

He had a bowl of something with noodles, greens, and a sauce that kept migrating to one side because the table was not perfectly level relative to the ring’s local gravity.

He moved the bowl.

The sauce moved again.

Iri watched.

“You are losing.”

“To lunch?”

“Yes.”

Ollie rotated the bowl ninety degrees.

“That will not help.”

“It changes the slope.”

“It changes your relationship to the slope.”

“That is helping.”

Iri ate.

Ollie looked at the movement trace again.

“Do people actually care about view rooms that much?”

Iri did not look up.

“What?”

“Nadia said one housing conflict yesterday was because of view access.”

“Of course.”

“You live in orbit.”

“Yes.”

“You can see space all the time.”

“No.”

Ollie waited.

Iri sighed.

“Most inhabited places are inside the structure. Views cost surface, shielding design, access, maintenance, booking, and people standing in front of them for too long.”

“You don’t like them?”

“I like one.”

“Which one?”

“No.”

“Why do you keep doing that?”

“Because it works.”

A group of apprentices came through the service loop.

One of them called to Iri.

“Saturday block?”

“Lost.”

“Visitor group?”

“Yes.”

The apprentice made a face.

“Again?”

Iri pointed at Ollie without looking at him.

“Earth has arrived.”

Ollie said, “I gave you fourteen minutes.”

The apprentice stopped.

“You did?”

Iri said, “Maybe.”

“Fourteen?”

“Yes.”

“That’s enough to set the jig.”

Ollie looked between them.

“What are you making?”

Both apprentices said, “No.”

Ollie put his tablet down.

“Fine.”

The apprentice grinned and left.

Iri kept eating.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You look pleased.”

“I am learning local culture.”

“No, you are learning people will hide projects from you if you ask badly.”

“That is culture.”

Iri considered.

“Unfortunately.”

Ollie’s tablet vibrated.

A message from Ethan.

ETHAN: Dinner attempt Friday. No spreadsheet.

Below it:

AOIFE: this is false advertising

MAYA: if there is no spreadsheet how will we know where to sit

SAMIR: chairs

OLLIE: working until 19:10

ETHAN: I checked.

Ollie stared at that.

OLLIE: You checked what?

ETHAN: Your public shift block. Not your work details.

AOIFE: he lasted two days

MAYA: surveillance state

ETHAN: it is literally shared residency scheduling

SAMIR: still funny

Ollie smiled.

Then he looked at the timing trace again.

Iri tapped the edge of his tablet with one finger.

“Work or friends.”

“What?”

“You are doing both badly.”

“I’m eating.”

“You are not.”

Ollie looked at the bowl.

He had stopped halfway.

He picked up the fork.

Iri’s regulator gave a soft green pulse.

Across the junction, a group of residents crossed from a warmer corridor into the cooler service loop.

One person rubbed both arms.

Another immediately opened their jacket.

The building adjusted.

Nobody cared.

Ollie watched them.

Not the display.

One of the apprentices from before cut across the flow, apologized to nobody, and caught a closing ringline door with one hand.

Two people behind changed direction to follow.

The route reshaped itself in three seconds.

Ollie looked down at the test log.

Doors.

Counts.

Loads.

Not people.

Not sequence in any useful human sense.

He opened a blank note.

RUN 3 SOCIAL RECONSTRUCTION

He wrote:

Tomas first.
Iri second.
Housing staff stops.
Someone calls from behind.
Return movement begins before partition fully settles.

Iri glanced over.

“What is that?”

“Bad data.”

“Good.”

“That was not agreement.”

“It was.”

Ollie looked at the real corridor again.

He had spent the morning trying to make the residents move like a test.

Maybe the test needed to get worse at moving like a machine.

He did not write that down.

He wrote:

Need better timing source.
