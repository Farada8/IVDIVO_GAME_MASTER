# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 11 — WHOSE STORY

The snack dispenser had rejected the same payment four times and accepted three empty cups.

Aoife recorded it for six minutes.

The machine occupied a shallow wall niche between a laundry return and a public charging bench. Nothing about it deserved six minutes except that everyone using the corridor had an opinion.

A boy in a school jacket slapped the payment panel with the flat of his hand.

“That doesn’t help,” his friend said.

“It knows I’m serious.”

The dispenser released another cup.

The boy stared at it.

Aoife shifted the recorder slightly to catch the soft plastic knock as the cup dropped into the tray.

His friend saw the recorder.

“Oh, no.”

Aoife lowered it.

“You’re one of them,” he said.

“One of who?”

“The Earth people asking what it feels like to live in space.”

The boy stopped glaring at the machine long enough to look at her.

“Tell them it feels like being robbed by a wall.”

“That I can use.”

“Don’t.”

Aoife switched the recorder off.

The boy checked the dark indicator before he turned back to the machine.

The corridor was busy in the way Vela had taught her to recognize as local rather than dramatic. A laundry queue had formed because one return bank was out of service. Two young maintenance workers ate from folded containers while standing because every seat was taken. A woman with a toddler was arguing with a route display that kept offering a connection with a six-minute walking interval through a gravity transition the toddler clearly did not intend to respect.

Beyond the opposite wall, a view cut between structures and showed Earth.

Nobody was looking at it.

Aoife had recorded Earth twice that morning and deleted both clips.

The first had looked like an advertisement.

The second had looked like somebody trying very hard not to make an advertisement.

The broken snack dispenser was better.

A girl in a dark service apron came out from behind the food counter across the corridor, crossed to the dispenser and crouched beside it.

“You’re wasting your time,” she told the boys.

“It took my money.”

“It reserved your payment. You’ll get it back when I lock the unit.”

“That sounds suspiciously like work.”

“It is. Move.”

The girl opened a maintenance flap with a borrowed tool that looked slightly too large for the job.

Aoife turned the recorder on again, then stopped herself.

“Can I record this?”

The girl glanced up.

“Why?”

“It’s been giving people cups instead of food for at least six minutes.”

“You’ve been recording that?”

“Mostly the people losing arguments with it.”

The girl looked through the opening toward Earth, then back at the machine.

She laughed once.

“Audio only.”

Aoife showed her the camera still inside the bag.

“Audio only.”

The girl reached into the open flap and pressed something.

The dispenser hummed. A row of lights reset. The payment panel changed to:

OUT OF ORDER — REFUND QUEUED

The boy checked his account.

“Oh.”

“Give it a minute,” the girl said.

He kept the empty cup and left with his friend.

Aoife stopped recording.

The girl returned toward the food counter.

Aoife followed at a distance that she hoped looked casual and suspected did not.

The food point sold late-shift meals, school snacks, electrolyte packets, two kinds of coffee and one thing labelled simply HOT SOUP because apparently the people who designed orbital cities eventually ran out of imagination.

The girl retied her apron strap and took a tray from a warming unit.

“You’re following me.”

“The corridor ended here.”

“That is a terrible defence.”

Aoife held up the recorder, still off.

“I’m making a piece about people our age living here.”

“Why?”

“Because most of what I’ve seen from Earth is about living in space.”

“That is where we live.”

“Yes.”

The girl slid the tray into a rack.

“You’re going to ask what I miss about Earth.”

“No.”

“What the view means to me.”

“No.”

“How gravity changed who I am.”

Aoife put the recorder on the counter between them.

“Do you know why the laundry queue is twenty people long?”

The girl stopped.

Aoife nodded toward the return bank.

“One side is down.”

“That’s not why.”

Aoife waited.

“The allocation block moved two households out of temporary units this morning, so everyone tried to do bedding before shift change. Then Return Three died.”

Aoife picked up the recorder.

“Can I record that?”

The girl frowned at her.

“You really want the laundry?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Aoife almost said because boring was the story.

The phrase arrived polished enough that she distrusted it immediately.

“Because it changed twenty people’s evening.”

The girl looked toward the queue.

A teenager near the back had sat on a bag of clothes and was doing homework on one knee. Two people ahead of him were arguing over whether there was time to leave and come back before a rehearsal slot.

“Fine,” the girl said.

Aoife turned the recorder on.

“What should I call you?”

The girl hesitated.

“Sela.”

Aoife marked it privately.

SELA — WORKING SOURCE / NAME CHECK BEFORE USE

“What do you want me not to ask?” Aoife said.

Sela looked at the recorder.

“Now?”

“If you know.”

“I don’t know everything you can make weird yet.”

“Then stop me when I get there.”

Sela started handing food packets to a woman in a transit uniform.

“Are you keeping the part where I’m working?”

“If it stays useful.”

“That sounds dangerous.”

“It means I don’t know what the piece is yet.”

Sela nodded, not quite agreeing.

Aoife recorded the next twenty minutes in pieces: a shift rather than an interview.

Sela complained that the late food packets had changed supplier and now opened from the wrong corner. A customer asked whether the coffee machine had been cleaned; Sela checked the indicator before answering. A friend arrived with a music file and tried to persuade her to listen to a new mix while Sela counted meal tokens. A message from Sela’s training group asked whether she could swap a morning block because an instructor had been rerouted to Ring Six commissioning support. She replied no because she was already working before class.

A route alert moved her return transfer by nine minutes.

“That’s the third one this week,” Sela said.

Aoife glanced at the recorder.

“You want that on?”

Sela shrugged.

“Everybody knows the routes are bad.”

“Bad compared to what?”

Sela gave her a look.

“See? That’s the Earth question.”

Aoife waited.

Sela tapped the route notice away.

“Bad compared to last month.”

Aoife left the silence after it.

Sela finished the shift at sixteen forty and disappeared into the staff side long enough to change out of the apron. She returned wearing a loose jacket with one sleeve clipped shorter and carrying a case no bigger than her forearm.

“Music?” Aoife asked.

“Sound kit.”

“For what?”

“Nothing you’re recording today.”

Aoife put the recorder away.

That got a small smile.

They walked toward a radial transfer.

Aoife recorded the station around them instead: a classroom move notice, someone swearing when the laundry return announced another delay, a child asking whether Ring Six had a better climbing room than Ring Four, a parent saying nobody knew because Ring Six was not open yet.

A couple near the transfer map were arguing over whose route home became worse if they stayed another hour together.

Sela listened for a few seconds.

“That is the part people never put in.”

“What part?”

“The part where you stop seeing somebody because their last good transfer is twenty-two ten.”

Aoife looked at the recorder.

Sela shook her head.

“Not them. Just the idea.”

“Ideas are harder to get consent from.”

“That sounds like your problem.”

The transfer arrived.

Sela stepped into the higher-gravity side of the car without looking at the floor markers. Aoife followed and had to correct half a step when the car settled.

Sela noticed and, mercifully, said nothing.

At the next interchange she stopped beside a narrow service corridor.

“This is where I leave you.”

Aoife looked past her. The corridor carried a maintenance-side access stripe and a public-route marker farther down.

“Can I come?”

“Not today.”

“What changes tomorrow?”

“You ask before following me to work.”

Aoife opened her mouth, then closed it.

“That seems fair.”

Sela shifted the sound case to her other hand.

“If you actually want the commissioning side, not the tour version, I can show you part of my route tomorrow.”

“On record?”

“Some of it.”

“What part isn’t?”

Sela’s expression changed—not much, but enough.

“I’ll tell you before we start.”

Then she left.

Aoife watched the service door close behind her.

Earth filled the end of the public corridor through a curved window.

Aoife recorded the laundry delay announcement instead.

* * *

The next day Sela chose a shared break room beside a service junction. The door opened every few minutes. People filled water bottles, left meal containers, checked shift messages and went back out. Nobody entering seemed interested in becoming part of anybody’s important conversation.

Aoife liked that.

Sela did not like the first table.

“The vent ruins audio.”

Aoife listened for three seconds and moved.

At the second table, a small environmental panel sat in the wall beside Sela’s shoulder.

Sela touched it.

The panel woke. A short two-tone service sound played. The airflow changed almost imperceptibly.

Aoife heard it because she was listening for room tone.

She looked at the panel.

Sela saw her look.

“That’s the boundary.”

Aoife put the recorder on the table but left it dark.

“Tell me.”

“You can say the room was adjusted.”

Aoife waited.

“Not why. Don’t show the panel. Don’t name the setting. Don’t connect it to me as some category.”

“Okay.”

“And don’t cut this part out and make a separate thing about it later.”

“Without asking again, no.”

Sela sat down.

“What happens if somebody else takes your piece and clips ten seconds anyway?”

Aoife did not answer immediately.

“I can control what I release and how I label it. I can’t promise nobody will ever repost public material.”

Sela’s face tightened.

“So the agreement ends when it leaves you.”

“No. My control ends. The risk doesn’t.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

Sela looked at the wall panel again.

Aoife let her.

“If that makes the risk too high,” Aoife said, “we leave this part out entirely.”

Sela was quiet long enough that the door opened, two workers filled bottles and left again.

“Who sees it before you put it out?” she asked.

“You see your excerpt. Current plan is a public youth documentary sequence—Earth and orbital audiences. Edited, not live. No standalone profile of you unless we separately agree.”

“Can I remove a line?”

“From your excerpt, yes.”

“Even if you like it?”

“Yes.”

“What if I just don’t like how I sound?”

Aoife thought about that.

“Ask. I’m not promising every reason automatically wins, but if the line changes what people can know about you, you get a meaningful veto.”

Sela frowned.

“That sounds more complicated than yes.”

“It is.”

Sela looked relieved by that, which Aoife had not expected.

Aoife pointed at the recorder.

“I want the boundary itself recorded so neither of us has to reconstruct it later.”

“Start there.”

Aoife turned the recorder on.

The room tone settled around the small red indicator.

“Context agreement,” she said. “Public edited youth documentary sequence. Sela reviews her excerpt before use. I may state that this room’s environment was adjusted. I do not state why, do not name or show the profile or setting, and do not connect the adjustment to a body, medical or identity category. No standalone reuse of this material without separate permission. Camera stays off in this room. Sela can request removal from her excerpt before release, and we resolve that before publication.”

She stopped.

Sela listened to the playback.

At the end she pointed at the device.

“Keep that whole part together.”

“I will.”

Aoife marked the clip:

S1 CONTEXT — KEEP TERMS INTACT

Only then did she ask, “Why are you carrying a sound kit to a food shift?”

“That isn’t Ring Six.”

“I noticed.”

The sound kit belonged to a small group that mixed school performances and youth events. Sela was not the main performer. She preferred the part where everybody complained afterward that a different channel had been too loud.

“Do you ever agree?” Aoife asked.

“After export.”

They talked about the food counter. About a training block Sela had nearly missed twice because temporary transfer patterns had changed after Ring Six delays. About a friend who had stopped staying late at rehearsals because the only reliable route home added twenty-eight minutes. About the odd local definition of close: physically near, operationally three transfers away.

Aoife asked what Ring Six meant to her.

Sela’s face closed slightly.

Aoife heard the question after it left her mouth and disliked how large it sounded.

Sela answered anyway.

“It means one less transfer between training and work if the service corridor opens the way they said.”

Aoife nodded.

“My question was worse than your answer.”

“Then use the answer.”

Sela shifted in the chair.

The wall panel made the same soft two-tone sound again. Aoife’s recorder caught it. Neither of them reacted.

“What do people from Earth get wrong about the delay?” Aoife asked.

“That everybody here wants the same thing.”

“How?”

“My friend Taren wants them to open anything that cuts his commute. His mother wants another month of testing because she doesn’t trust the mixed-environment controls. My manager wants the service corridor because she is paying people to arrive late. One of my instructors wants the training rooms because our schedule is a mess.”

Sela picked at a worn edge on the sound case.

“I want all of those things depending on which hour you ask.”

Aoife let the sentence sit without trying to improve it.

“What about right now?”

“Right now I want to get from this room to rehearsal without the route changing again.”

Her wrist display flashed.

ETHAN: rough thought — if you’re building around ordinary life, maybe cut between the same route at work / school / night instead of grouping by person

Aoife read it and put her wrist down.

Sela raised an eyebrow.

“Problem?”

“Unsolicited editing.”

“Bad?”

“Annoyingly not.”

Sela waited, expecting more.

Aoife did not give her more.

The door opened.

Two workers came in arguing about a missing tool authorization. They filled water containers. One of them tapped the environmental panel on the way out.

“Leave it,” Sela said.

The worker stopped.

“Sorry.”

He restored the previous setting. The same two-tone service sound played.

“Thanks,” Sela said.

The door closed.

Aoife had a question ready. The recorder light was visible between them. Sela had just spent several minutes defining what did and did not belong inside that light.

Aoife left the question where it was.

Sela watched her for a moment.

“You can come farther tomorrow,” she said.

“Where?”

“Service side. Not commissioning floor. I’m not getting you access I don’t have.”

Aoife marked the change in her notes without turning it into a victory.

The access mattered less than why Sela had offered it.

* * *

The strongest line came after Aoife turned the recorder off.

They were leaving the break room. Sela had reviewed the clips, removed one answer because it sounded, in her words, “like I’m applying to represent the entire station,” and approved the rest for a rough excerpt.

Aoife packed the recorder.

The room reset after they stood. The two-tone service sound played again.

Sela waited for the door to open.

Aoife asked, without thinking, “Does that route really only add forty minutes?”

Sela’s hand stopped on the strap of her sound case.

The recorder was dark.

Sela looked down the empty corridor.

“For everyone else, maybe.”

Aoife waited.

Sela rubbed one thumb along the edge of the case.

“For me, one of the transfers means I spend the next hour pretending it didn’t cost me anything.”

The sentence landed before Aoife could protect the edit from it.

Laundry delay. Food shift. Three transfers. Forty minutes on the public schedule. Then that sentence.

The dark recorder light became the most important thing in the corridor.

Aoife did not reach for it.

“Okay,” she said.

Sela’s expression hardened.

“That’s all?”

“What do you want me to say?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then I’m not going to invent something.”

Sela looked away.

The door opened. They stepped into the corridor.

After five metres Sela said, “That wasn’t for the piece.”

“I know.”

Aoife wished she had another answer because that one sounded too small.

She did not improve it.

They separated at the interchange.

Aoife went home with the best line she had heard all week and no recording of it.

That should have simplified the edit.

It did not.

---

At 22:41 Aoife had thirty-seven minutes of usable sound and a sequence that felt twelve seconds too short.

She knew exactly where the twelve seconds belonged.

Her temporary room was narrow enough that the work surface folded over part of the bed. She had learned to edit with one knee against the wall and her feet under a chair that technically belonged to the host household.

On the screen, the sequence began with the snack dispenser releasing an empty cup.

Then:

Sela: It means one less transfer between training and work if the service corridor opens the way they said.

Laundry return warning.

A teenager complaining about a rehearsal slot.

Food packets opening from the wrong corner.

A route change.

Sela laughing about the sound group.

The brief environmental tone sat under two clips. Aoife barely noticed it. It belonged to the room like ventilation.

She trimmed a pause.

Restored it.

Trimmed a different one.

The sequence still wanted the line she did not have.

Aoife could paraphrase it. She could build toward it without quoting. She could ask Sela tomorrow if she would say something similar on record. She could tell herself that the idea existed elsewhere in the interview.

She did none of those things.

Instead she let the route chime run four seconds longer than she normally would have, then cut to the broken snack dispenser.

The transition was worse.

She left it.

Ethan called at 22:58.

Aoife considered not answering. Then she remembered the coffee in Dublin and answered anyway.

His face appeared against a background she did not recognize. Adult-track orientation room, maybe. Too clean. Too much glass.

“You’re working,” he said.

“You called me.”

“You answered.”

“Still evaluating that decision.”

He smiled.

“What do you have?”

Aoife hesitated, then sent him the rough sequence.

“Listen. Don’t reorganize my life.”

“I was going to say audio.”

“Same warning applies.”

He put one earbud in.

Aoife watched him listen.

That was always unpleasant. Not because he reacted badly. Because he reacted visibly before he decided what to say, and Aoife could usually see the argument arriving.

At the snack-dispenser opening he smiled.

At the laundry queue he stopped smiling.

At Sela’s commute answer he leaned closer to the screen.

Then the route chime ran four seconds too long.

His eyebrows moved.

He finished the clip.

“The middle is strong.”

Aoife waited.

“The transition after the route alert is missing something.”

“Yes.”

“You could bridge it with—”

“There is a reason it is missing.”

Ethan stopped.

Aoife said nothing else.

He looked at the timeline she had shared, then at her.

“Source boundary?”

“Yes.”

“Okay.”

Aoife had prepared herself to defend it. The unused defence sat awkwardly in her mouth.

Ethan pointed at the earlier part of the timeline instead.

“The snack machine is good.”

“I spent six minutes recording it.”

“That is commitment.”

“Professional commitment.”

His smile changed.

Book One. Virginia. Evidence cards. Too much coffee and not enough sleep.

Aoife smiled back before she could decide whether to.

Ethan said, “I still think the route structure could work.”

“Of course you do.”

“But not if it breaks the source boundary.”

“For once, your restraint is inspirational.”

“It’s exhausting.”

For a few seconds neither of them said anything.

It was easy.

Then Ethan’s wrist flashed with another message. His eyes dropped. Adult-track something. He turned the display away slightly—not secretively enough to count as hiding it, just enough that Aoife could not read.

The ease changed shape.

She did not ask.

Not tonight.

“I’m sending Sela the cleaned excerpt,” Aoife said.

“Now?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want me to—”

“No.”

He stopped himself and laughed.

“Right.”

That helped more than an apology would have.

They ended the call two minutes later.

Aoife sent Sela the excerpt with the agreed terms attached above it—not hidden in a link, not summarized, the actual terms.

Sela responded eleven minutes later.

SELA: remove 04:12–04:18

Aoife checked. It was a line about her manager losing staff time to route changes.

AOIFE: because it identifies the work point?

SELA: no because she’ll complain i made her sound reasonable

Aoife laughed.

AOIFE: removed

A second message arrived.

SELA: rest is fine

Then:

SELA: snack machine deserves more time

Aoife moved the machine from six seconds to eight.

Not because Sela had editorial authority over the whole piece.

Because she was right.

Aoife exported the approved excerpt.

SOURCE S1 — CLEANED / APPROVED / CONTEXT TERMS ATTACHED

She checked it once more.

The camera had never come out. The profile had never been named. The setting had never been shown. The off-recorder line was nowhere in the sequence. Sela had reviewed what remained.

Aoife leaned back against the wall.

The version was smaller than the one she could have made.

It was also the one Sela had agreed to exist inside.

Her device buzzed.

OLLIE: question when you’re awake

Aoife looked at the time.

AOIFE: dangerously subjective

OLLIE: in the interviews / room audio — did people use the same words before environmental changes? pressure / dry / slow / wrong air / anything like that

Aoife read the message twice.

Not because the question was difficult.

Because it made her hear the interviews in a different order.

Sela talking about routes. The break-room door. Other voices from the corridor. The repeated service tone.

She opened her notes, not the finished edit.

AOIFE: maybe. not enough to say same thing. i can check exact wording tomorrow

Three dots appeared.

OLLIE: good. exact wording only

Of course.

Aoife closed the approved excerpt and left it alone.

The room around her hummed with the host household’s night setting. Somewhere beyond the wall, a transfer passed through a lower band and changed pitch as it went.

Aoife heard it.

She did not turn on the recorder.
