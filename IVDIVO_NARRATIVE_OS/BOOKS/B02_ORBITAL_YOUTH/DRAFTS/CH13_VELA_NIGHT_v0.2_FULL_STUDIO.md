# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 13 — VELA NIGHT

Aoife finished her Earth coursework under a table while somebody tuned a percussion frame above her head.

The assignment had been designed for a desk. The food volume had no available desks.

Every table within two transfer zones had been occupied by people eating, waiting for performers, applying face paint, repairing costume pieces, doing homework, pretending not to do homework, or defending a seat for someone who was allegedly “two minutes away.”

Aoife had taken the floor.

Her tablet rested on her knees.

The question on screen asked her to compare two models of public participation in post-crisis civic institutions.

Above her, a local teenager said, “Hold that.”

Aoife raised one hand without looking.

A metal bar landed in it.

“Not like that.”

“How?”

“Straight.”

“It is straight.”

“It is emotionally straight.”

Aoife looked up.

Iri was crouched on the bench above her, fastening a narrow percussion rail to a flexible frame that would later be carried through three gravity bands.

“You have stolen Ethan’s vocabulary.”

“I do not know what that means.”

“Good.”

Aoife adjusted the bar.

Iri tightened the clamp.

A low note travelled through the frame and into Aoife’s wrist.

“Again,” Iri said.

“I have a deadline.”

“You have a hand.”

“Both can be true.”

Iri hit the rail once with two fingers.

The note came back cleaner.

“Done.”

Aoife lowered the bar and returned to her assignment.

Iri looked at the screen upside down.

“What is that?”

“Punishment.”

“You came to orbit to do Earth school.”

“Apparently Earth remains emotionally attached to me.”

“That sounds unhealthy.”

A message appeared on her tablet.

ETHAN: where are you

AOIFE: academically underground

ETHAN: location

AOIFE: food volume 3, beneath people with instruments

ETHAN: that is not map language

AOIFE: then you are not ready for local life

Iri leaned down.

“Tell him West Market Three.”

“No.”

“He will find you in six minutes.”

“I want to see what happens.”

“That is cruel.”

“Documentary instinct.”

Iri climbed off the bench with the frame.

Their cuff gave a coarse load pulse as they stepped through the warmer edge of the food zone. Iri glanced at it, shifted the frame to the other shoulder, and continued without comment.

Aoife finished the last paragraph.

The system accepted the assignment at 17:58. Vela Night’s first public route opened twelve minutes later. Aoife closed the coursework and opened the recorder.

* * *

Vela Night did not begin.

It accumulated.

A transit corridor acquired three musicians.

A wall that had been ordinary at sixteen hundred began emitting slow bands of light that moved opposite the ring’s rotation.

A market counter removed half its daytime menu and added five late-service items arranged by compatibility rather than cuisine.

Two maintenance workers opened a floor panel in the middle of everything and became a temporary island that people flowed around without slowing.

A child painted one side of their face silver and refused the other side on political grounds known only to the child.

Then the first percussion group crossed a radial connector and the sound changed with the gravity band.

Enough people looked up to change the corridor’s pace.

Aoife recorded the musicians for seven seconds.

Then turned the camera toward the two workers closing the floor panel while the crowd stepped around them.

One worker noticed.

“Why us?”

“Because everyone else is filming them.”

The worker pointed at the performers.

“They are more attractive.”

“Probably.”

“Good documentary.”

They closed the panel.

A transit guide changed from normal service to event flow.

Nobody stopped walking.

The city had not become a festival venue.

It had remained a city while a festival occupied it.

Aoife liked that better.

Ethan found her at 18:19.

He was carrying two food wraps and the expression of someone who had been betrayed by geography.

“West Market Three,” he said.

“You solved it.”

“I asked someone.”

“Character growth.”

He handed her a wrap.

Aoife took it.

“What is this?”

“You did homework instead of eating.”

“I ate.”

“You sent a picture of a drink.”

“The drink contained calories.”

“That is not a meal.”

“You have become my host.”

“No.”

“Good. I already have one.”

They moved with the crowd toward the first performance route.

Aoife opened the wrap.

It was the same filling she had stolen from her host’s kitchen two days earlier and given Ethan during the study block.

She looked at him.

Ethan kept walking.

“You remembered.”

“What?”

“The sandwich jurisdiction case.”

He smiled.

“That was a serious legal matter.”

“You said it depended on jurisdiction.”

“It did.”

“You were emotionally close to making lunch into orbital law.”

“I have recovered.”

“Evidence?”

“I brought you food without a regulatory framework.”

Aoife took a bite.

“Conditional improvement.”

The floor changed slightly under them as the route began to move outward along the ring.

Ethan’s hand went to the side rail before he looked for it—better than arrival day, still not local. Aoife noticed because she had done exactly the same thing half a second earlier. Neither said anything.

At the next junction the crowd split around a suspended light installation shaped like a loose ribbon. The ribbon twisted slowly through an open volume, and the light travelled along it as if the structure were unrolling into the distance.

Aoife raised the recorder.

Then lowered it.

“What?” Ethan asked.

“Nothing.”

“You did the camera thing.”

“What camera thing?”

“You see something beautiful and immediately become suspicious.”

“It is a useful instinct.”

“Is it?”

Aoife looked at the ribbon.

Below it, three technicians were arguing with a queue because the best lower viewing rail had been reserved in blocks before the event.

A local girl waved her booking screen at them.

“I have had this for three weeks.”

A technician said, “And the section is over capacity.”

“Then remove the visitor block.”

Someone behind her said, “Yes.”

Someone else said, “No, my cousin is in that block.”

The argument split instantly.

Aoife raised the recorder again.

“This,” she said.

Ethan looked from the ribbon to the booking argument.

“Of course.”

“Beautiful view. Booking problem.”

“You said you were trying not to turn everything into a thesis.”

“I am filming people complaining.”

“Healthy.”

They kept moving.

* * *

Samir’s exhibition was scheduled for 18:42 in a linked sport volume two bands outward.

At 18:37, Maya sent:

MAYA: we are there

At 18:38:

MAYA: correction we are in wrong there

SAMIR: impressive

MAYA: there are six entrances

SAMIR: seven

MAYA: I hate this city

LEA: no you don’t

MAYA: private chat violation

Aoife showed Ethan.

He smiled.

“Which entrance?” he asked.

“Apparently not theirs.”

They reached the sport volume at 18:41.

Their assigned viewing area did not exist.

More accurately, it existed physically and had been absorbed socially.

A group of local families had spread across the marked rail because the adjacent view block had been reduced after an equipment inspection. Nobody intended to move. The event system suggested two alternate positions.

One was twelve minutes away.

The other had a partial sightline.

Ethan opened the booking layer.

Aoife closed it with one finger.

“No.”

“We have a reservation.”

“So do they, probably.”

“We can check.”

“Or we can stand there.”

She pointed to a narrow upper crossing where people were already leaning between support ribs.

“That is a terrible view.”

“It is a view.”

“We booked this.”

Aoife looked at him.

Ethan stopped.

“Right.”

He closed the layer.

“Growth,” Aoife said.

“Do not document it.”

They took the bad view.

Maya and Lea arrived from the opposite side with Davi behind them carrying three drinks and one expression too pleased with itself.

Maya saw Aoife first.

“You found it.”

“We used reading.”

“Hostile.”

“Planet education works.”

Maya pointed at Davi.

“He started that.”

Davi said, “I learned it from locals.”

Lea said, “No, you did not.”

“Cultural exchange.”

The exhibition began before the argument finished.

Samir’s game still did not have a name Aoife trusted herself to pronounce correctly.

The object moved through linked volumes at different effective gravity. Players released it near one band and met it in another. Timing mattered more than force. The strongest Earth instinct—run directly toward the thing—was often wrong.

Samir had learned enough not to do that every time.

Not enough to look local.

He missed his first handoff by almost a meter.

Maya shouted his name anyway.

Lea did not.

“Why aren’t you supporting him?” Maya asked.

“I don’t know him.”

“You have met him twice.”

“That is not how teams work.”

“Terrible culture.”

Samir recovered on the next transition.

A local player crossed early, pulling the defender with them. Samir waited half a beat longer than he had in practice and entered the lower band behind the movement instead of fighting it.

The object reached him cleanly.

He redirected rather than held it.

Someone beside Aoife said, “Better.”

The voice beside Aoife did not know him as R-14. It had only seen the play.

Aoife turned the recorder away from Samir and caught Maya reacting instead.

Maya noticed.

“Delete that.”

“You have not seen it.”

“I know my face.”

Aoife deleted it.

Maya looked suspicious.

“You actually deleted it.”

“Yes.”

“That is less fun.”

“Make better faces.”

At the far side of the volume, one biologically embodied nonhuman player wore a different momentum-class marker on the shoulder harness. The referee adjusted one entry boundary for that class before the next run. Nobody explained anything. The crowd complained about the delay because the crowd had priorities.

Samir’s team lost by two transitions.

He was smiling when he came off.

A teammate hit his shoulder and said something Aoife could not hear.

Samir answered without looking toward the Earth group.

Maya waved both arms until he finally noticed.

He pointed at her and then at the floor, a gesture Aoife understood as stop doing that.

Maya waved more.

Samir walked away with his team.

Ethan said, “Are we offended?”

“No,” Aoife said.

Maya said, “Yes.”

Lea said, “Why?”

“He chose them.”

“He is playing with them.”

“Still.”

Aoife watched Samir disappear into the side corridor with the team. He had wanted somewhere nobody cared about R-14; apparently that included somewhere he did not immediately return to the people from R-14. She said nothing.

* * *

At 19:12, Ollie was still at work.

At 19:13, Maya sent him a picture of Samir’s empty position after the exhibition.

MAYA: you missed him being almost competent

SAMIR: I am in this chat

OLLIE: test request accepted

MAYA: nobody asked

OLLIE: you asked where I was

MAYA: emotionally different

ETHAN: 20:40?

OLLIE: still likely

AOIFE: illegal word

Ollie did not answer.

They left the sport volume without him.

Maya went with Lea and Davi toward a food route.

Samir remained with his team.

Aoife and Ethan followed a moving light installation inward until it intersected with a performance corridor.

For twenty minutes they were accidentally alone together inside several thousand people.

It happened because nobody else wanted the route they chose.

The main transit spine had become slow with performers and spectators. Ethan suggested the shortest alternate. Aoife refused because the shortest alternate required two badly phased transfers and everyone had learned something this week.

They took a longer outer path.

The crowd thinned.

The wall beside them opened into a view corridor.

Earth appeared beyond the glass.

Aoife did not reach for the recorder.

Ethan noticed.

“You’re ill.”

“I already filmed Earth.”

“When?”

“Dublin transfer.”

“That was Earth from Earth.”

“Still Earth.”

“This is objectively different.”

“You sound like a tourism board.”

They stopped anyway.

The planet occupied more of the window than Aoife expected, too close to become the neat blue marble cameras usually made of it.

Cloud systems. Night edge. Lines of light. Weather large enough to erase the idea of countries from this distance and not large enough to erase anything that happened inside them.

Ethan stood beside her.

He did not say anything intelligent.

Aoife appreciated him for almost a minute.

Then a voice behind them said, “Ethan?”

He turned.

A local girl from the Youth Cohort study block was coming toward them with two other people.

Aoife recognized her as the one who had argued with the instructor about credit transfers and won half a point nobody else understood.

“Hi,” Ethan said.

“You’re coming to the civic simulation tomorrow, right?”

Ethan’s face changed into the version of itself that liked structured problems.

“I thought visitors were observers.”

“You can take a resident-side role if somebody sponsors the slot.”

“Really?”

“Yes. We need one more.”

They immediately began discussing time windows.

Aoife looked back at Earth.

It remained annoyingly impressive.

The local girl said something about decision thresholds.

Ethan leaned closer to see her schedule.

Aoife took a bite of the food wrap she had forgotten she was still carrying.

It was cold.

Aoife resented Ethan for approximately three seconds, which was unfair enough to irritate her further.

The girl looked at Aoife.

“Are you joining?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“I have documentary work.”

“During the simulation?”

“Yes.”

Aoife had no documentary work during the simulation.

Ethan glanced at her.

One second too long.

Aoife hated him for noticing.

The girl said, “Okay. Ethan, I’ll send the slot.”

She left with her friends.

Ethan’s band buzzed.

Aoife started walking.

“You do have documentary work?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“What?”

“Documentary work.”

“Specific.”

“I learned from Ollie.”

Ethan walked beside her.

“You could have said no because you didn’t want to.”

“I did say no.”

“You invented work.”

“I have work generally.”

“You are acting strange.”

Aoife stopped.

“That is an ambitious sentence from you.”

“What did I do?”

“Nothing.”

He waited.

Aoife became more annoyed.

“Do not wait like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like the answer is going to arrive if you are patient.”

“I was giving you time.”

“I do not want time.”

“What do you want?”

Aoife had no acceptable answer.

A percussion route entered the corridor before she needed one.

The first impact hit through the floor.

Then another answered from above.

People moved toward the sound.

Aoife turned.

Saved by infrastructure.

“Come on,” she said.

Ethan looked like he knew the conversation had not ended.

He followed anyway.

* * *

Iri’s performance group had lost its booked lower rail.

This became clear because Iri was arguing with a volunteer in front of it.

“We booked twelve meters.”

“You have eight.”

“We cannot turn the mid-band frame in eight.”

“The view block expanded.”

“Why?”

“Visitor allocation.”

Iri looked past the volunteer.

A cluster of off-station guests occupied the rail with event badges that gave them a clear sightline across the open volume.

Iri said something that Aoife did not record because the volunteer’s expression suggested it was excellent.

The volunteer said, “I did not create the allocation.”

“I know.”

“Then stop explaining it to me like I did.”

Iri stopped.

“Fine.”

Iri turned toward the group without looking any happier.

“Eight meters. Reverse entry. Drop the third rotation.”

One performer said, “We rehearsed three.”

“We have eight meters.”

“We can squeeze it.”

“No.”

“We can.”

Iri pointed at the outer edge.

“You want to take a frame into spectators?”

“No.”

“Then two rotations.”

The group changed the sequence in under a minute.

No one thanked the visitors.

Aoife raised the recorder.

Iri saw her.

“No allocation commentary.”

“I did not ask.”

“You had the face.”

“Everyone keeps accusing me of faces.”

“Learn control.”

The performance began.

The first frame moved inward through a light gravity band while two smaller percussion rails stayed outer. The rhythm separated spatially. One beat arrived through air, another through the floor, another through the handrail where spectators held it.

Iri entered last.

They were not better at this because they worked maintenance.

They were simply good.

Aoife saw it as soon as Iri began.

At work, Iri’s attention narrowed toward failure.

Here it widened toward people.

They watched the next performer, the rail position, the crowd opening, the sound returning from the wall, and somebody at the side who was clearly a friend because Iri smiled at them before the next strike.

They did not mention Ollie, Ring Six or resident reports. They argued about timing.

The group reached the first transition.

The effective gravity changed.

The frame dipped.

One performer compensated too early.

Iri adjusted the next beat instead of the frame.

The whole rhythm bent around the mistake.

The audience heard it as part of the piece.

Aoife knew it was not because Iri swore after the final strike.

She kept recording.

The crowd moved with them.

Not as planned.

The route guide expected spectators to hold at the rail while the performers crossed to the next volume.

Instead, the best view opened on the far side and half the group followed immediately.

A food queue broke apart.

Two people abandoned one transfer and cut through the side corridor.

Someone else saw them and copied.

Within thirty seconds, a path that had been nearly empty became the main route.

The system adapted.

A route arrow changed.

Then another.

A service cart waited because the human flow had taken priority.

The next partition remained open longer.

Aoife filmed the crowd rather than the performers.

Ethan leaned close enough to speak without shouting.

“That wasn’t on the event map.”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“Because the map is changing.”

He looked at her screen.

People streamed through the side corridor in a pattern that had not existed a minute earlier.

A local teenager climbed onto a divider to get a better look at the performers.

Their friend told them to get down.

They jumped to the other side instead.

The divider separated two route flows.

The teenager landed safely, looked triumphant, and discovered their return transfer doors closing three meters away.

“No,” they said.

Their friend laughed so hard they had to hold the rail.

“You said it was faster.”

“It was.”

“You missed it.”

“I looked good.”

“No one saw you.”

“I saw me.”

The transfer left.

The teenager checked the next one.

Twenty-two minutes.

Their triumph collapsed.

Aoife did not film their face.

She filmed the route board changing above them.

The teenager’s embarrassment was so ordinary that Aoife almost smiled. She kept the camera down.

“Criminal.”

“I said 20:40 earliest.”

“We live here for weeks. Stop making every gathering final.”

Ollie stepped closer to the public board.

The side corridor was carrying three times its predicted flow.

A service lane paused.

Ollie’s expression changed.

Aoife said, “No.”

He looked at her.

“You do not know what I’m doing.”

Ethan said, “Ollie.”

Samir had left.

Iri’s group was moving to another volume.

The moment they had almost managed to be five people in one place was already gone.

Ten seconds.

* * *

By twenty-two hundred, Vela Night had spread across enough of the city that no one person could have described it accurately.

Aoife stopped trying.

She recorded pieces.

A cleaner scraping temporary adhesive from a handrail while two teenagers argued over a missed view slot beside him.

A food worker switching one serving surface to a different compatibility mode without interrupting a conversation about someone’s breakup.

Two children asleep under coats while their adults watched a light route pass overhead.

A nonhuman resident wearing a compact environmental-support collar waited in exactly the same queue as everyone else and complained that the person ahead had booked two fabrication demos and was only using one.

Iri’s percussion group eating after performance, still arguing about the missing third rotation.

Samir laughing with his new team at something Aoife could not hear.

Maya and Lea sitting on the floor at the edge of a crowded market volume, Davi talking with somebody else, Maya pretending not to notice and failing.

Ethan beside Aoife, carrying the recorder case because the strap had started digging into her shoulder two hours earlier.

He had taken it without discussion.

She had let him.

Neither had returned to the civic-simulation girl.

Aoife knew that meant nothing.

She also knew she had noticed.

Ollie stood at a food counter comparing two late options and, for once, not looking at a diagnostic screen.

His band buzzed.

He checked it.

“Work?” Aoife asked.

“Test slot.”

“When?”

“Tomorrow.”

He read the time, locked the band, and kept the food in his other hand.

A moving light route passed through the market volume.

Everyone looked up at different times.

The lights crossed the ceiling, dropped along the wall, broke across a transparent floor section and continued below them into another gravity band where a second crowd cheered half a second later.

Aoife reached for the recorder.

Then stopped.

Ethan said, “Camera thing?”

“Yes.”

“Why not?”

“I have enough.”

“You never say that.”

“I am experimenting.”

They watched without recording.

Ten seconds later, a route announcement redirected everyone because the performance corridor had overfilled.

The crowd complained and moved anyway.

Ollie laughed.

Three hundred people had just invalidated another clean movement assumption by deciding a light was worth following.

Aoife heard it.

She did not need the recorder to remember.
