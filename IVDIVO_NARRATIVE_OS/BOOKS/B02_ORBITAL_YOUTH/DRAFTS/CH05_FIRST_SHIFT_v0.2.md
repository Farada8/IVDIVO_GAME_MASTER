# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 5 — FIRST SHIFT

Ollie’s first job in orbit was to prove that fifty-eight things were where the system already said they were.

He had expected something slightly better.

Not important.

Better.

Ring Six service access did not look unfinished so much as undecided. One wall had final panels. The next still showed service seams, temporary labels, and the pale rectangles where equipment covers would go after inspection. A crawler camera feed occupied the largest screen in the bay, showing a machine moving along the exterior hull with the casual confidence of something that could not fall anywhere useful.

Ollie looked at it once.

Then Mara Saye put a scanner in his hand.

“Inventory row C.”

He looked at the scanner.

“Okay.”

“Read the physical tag. Read the custody record. Confirm the seal. Do not confirm from the screen because the screen agrees with itself.”

“I know.”

Mara looked at him.

Ollie corrected.

“Right.”

“Better.”

She pointed toward a run of shallow tool lockers and service cabinets.

“Rows C through F. After that, cleaning verification on the environmental access panels.”

Ollie waited.

Mara waited too.

“That’s it?”

“For the next hour.”

“Oh.”

A local apprentice at the next station made a small sound.

Not quite a laugh.

Ollie looked over.

The apprentice was roughly his age, maybe a little older, wearing a work vest with two clipped access tabs and a narrow personal regulator band crossing one wrist. The regulator gave a brief amber pulse. The apprentice reached up, reduced the local vent by one step, and went back to checking a tool tray.

Mara said, “Iri, if you have spare attention, use it on your own list.”

“I was.”

“You made a sound.”

“That was respiratory.”

Mara looked at Iri’s regulator.

Iri looked at it too.

“Mostly.”

Ollie turned back to row C.

The first cabinet contained three torque drivers, two inspection lights, a fiber scope, four coupling tools, and a foam slot labeled EMPTY BY DESIGN.

He scanned the tag.

CABINET C-11
CUSTODY: COMMISSIONING
SEAL: VERIFIED
LAST CHECK: 05:43

He opened the physical label card.

C-11.

He counted the tools.

Three.

Two.

One.

Four.

Empty slot.

He confirmed.

The second cabinet was the same.

The third was almost the same.

By C-18, he understood why Mara had assigned it to him.

Because it was boring enough to reveal whether he thought he was too good for it.

He did not.

That was not the problem.

The problem was that every confirmed cabinet made the crawler screen more interesting.

It moved over a seam and deployed a narrow inspection arm.

Ollie watched the reflection in the cabinet door.

“Exterior seal check,” Iri said without looking up.

“I know.”

“Then stop trying to do it from over here.”

“I’m not.”

“You’re using a reflection.”

“That still counts as looking.”

“Yes.”

Ollie scanned C-19.

“Do apprentices get exterior diagnostics?”

“Crawler diagnostics.”

“That’s what I meant.”

“No, it wasn’t.”

Ollie looked at Iri.

Iri kept working.

“What do you get?”

“Today?”

“Yes.”

“Tool recovery, two calibration chains, and because the universe hates me, visitor access support after fourteen hundred.”

“Visitor access?”

“People who think service corridors are tours.”

“I’m not on a tour.”

Iri finally looked at him.

“I know. You have a scanner.”

Ollie scanned C-20.

“Do you always answer questions like that?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“Sometimes I ignore them.”

That turned out to be true.

Ollie asked three more questions over the next twenty minutes.

Iri answered one.

The one answer was about why some tool lockers had physical custody cards in addition to digital tracking.

“Because tags are allowed to be wrong,” Iri said.

“Systems too.”

“People first.”

“Fair.”

“Also people move tools.”

“That seems fixable.”

Iri gave him a look that suggested Ollie had accidentally said something very Earth.

He did not ask what.

At C-27, the digital record said:

FLEX COUPLING TOOL — REV C
SERIAL 88-411-C
SEAL VERIFIED

The physical tool looked right.

Same grip.

Same jaw width.

Same locking collar.

Ollie almost confirmed.

Then he saw the witness mark on the collar.

Rev C used two narrow engraved lines across the locking sleeve so wear could be checked without removing the cover.

This one had one.

He turned the tool over.

The physical serial ended in B.

He checked the cabinet label.

C-27.

He checked the custody card.

Rev C.

He scanned again.

Rev C.

Ollie looked toward Mara.

She was three stations away talking to an adult technician while eating nothing from an open food container beside her elbow.

He could call her.

Or he could ask Iri first.

“Is there a reason a Rev B would be in a Rev C custody slot?”

Iri did not turn.

“Yes.”

Ollie waited.

Iri kept working.

“What reason?”

“You asked if there is one.”

Ollie looked at the tool again.

“Is there a good reason?”

Iri came over.

The regulator on Iri’s wrist shifted from amber to green as they moved away from the warmer vent.

Iri took the tool without touching Ollie’s scanner.

“Physical serial?”

“B.”

“Record?”

“C.”

“Seal?”

“Current.”

Iri looked at the witness mark.

“Don’t use it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were holding it like you wanted to.”

“That is how tools work.”

Iri put it back in the foam.

“Log mismatch.”

“Could just be the wrong tag.”

“Could.”

“Or stock swap.”

“Could.”

“Or somebody replaced the tool and updated one layer but not custody.”

Iri looked at him.

“You ask a lot of questions when you already know the answer.”

“I don’t know the answer.”

“You know the choices.”

“That is different.”

“Good.”

Iri went back to the other station.

Ollie opened the discrepancy form.

It had six fields.

He filled four and stopped at:

CAUSE

Options:

UNKNOWN
STOCK TRANSFER
LABEL ERROR
CUSTODY UPDATE FAILURE
OTHER

He selected UNKNOWN and submitted the form before he could improve the answer into a guess.

* * *

Mara did not congratulate him.

She took the tool out of C-27, checked the serial, checked the digital record, called another technician, and asked for the receiving log.

The technician found a transfer from three days earlier.

Two nearly identical coupling tools had arrived in the same kit.

One Rev B.

One Rev C.

The Rev C tool had been routed to a calibration station.

The Rev B had gone into C-27.

The cabinet record had been updated from the shipment sheet instead of the physical serial.

Nothing had failed.

Nothing was damaged.

The wrong tool had not been used.

Mara closed the receiving log.

“Good catch.”

Ollie waited.

That appeared to be all of it.

“Is the Rev B unsafe?”

“No.”

“Then why separate them?”

“Different locking sleeve. Different inspection interval. Different failure signature.”

“So if someone uses the record later—”

“They inspect it as the wrong revision.”

“And if the record propagates to replacement stock—”

“They can order the wrong part.”

Ollie nodded.

Mara looked at him.

“You understand why I gave you inventory now?”

“Yes.”

“Say it.”

He hated that.

“Because hidden errors survive when everyone assumes the boring layer is correct.”

“Close enough.”

Iri, at the next station, said, “Also because nobody gives a first-day visitor a live calibration chain.”

Mara looked over.

“Thank you, Iri.”

“You’re welcome.”

Ollie said, “I’m not a visitor.”

Iri continued working.

Mara’s mouth moved slightly.

“You are on a youth residency from Earth.”

“I’m assigned here.”

“Both things can be true.”

That was annoyingly similar to something Ethan would say.

Ollie looked at the open food container.

“You should eat that.”

Mara looked down as if she had only now discovered it.

“I will.”

“You said that twenty minutes ago.”

“I did not.”

“I heard you tell the technician.”

“That was a different meal.”

Ollie looked at the container.

Mara closed it.

“Do you want more work?”

“Yes.”

“Then stop auditing my lunch.”

She handed him a second access tab.

It opened exactly one additional system.

Not the crawler feed.

A calibration-support panel for environmental partition actuators.

Ollie tried not to look pleased.

He failed.

Mara saw.

“This is not authorization to change anything.”

“I know.”

“You will compare commanded position, local physical marker, and returned state. If they disagree, you stop and call me.”

“What tolerance?”

The corner of Mara’s mouth moved.

“Now that is a useful question.”

She sent the range to his display.

Iri looked over.

“Congratulations.”

The word sounded different from Iri.

Less like approval.

More like information.

Ollie clipped the second tab beside the first.

On the far wall, the crawler reached the edge of its exterior inspection sector and folded its arm.

A technician at the console tagged one image for review.

Nobody put on a suit.

Nobody went outside.

The machine moved to the next seam.

Ollie opened the calibration list.

Twenty-four actuators.

Command.

Marker.

Return.

The work was still mostly boring, only now the boring part sat one permission layer closer to the system he had wanted to touch.

Ollie opened the calibration list before Mara finished walking away.

* * *

At 12:14, Ollie’s tablet vibrated inside the locker where personal devices had to stay during active service work.

At 12:19, it vibrated again.

At 12:31, it did it three times in a row.

Iri looked toward the locker.

“Emergency?”

“No idea.”

“You can check on break.”

“I know.”

“You say that a lot.”

“You say that I say things a lot.”

Iri considered.

“Fair.”

They finished the calibration set.

Twenty-three matched.

One returned a value two tenths inside the acceptable band but had a physical marker sitting almost at the outer edge.

Ollie stared at it.

Iri said, “No.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You’re thinking about opening the cover.”

“I’m thinking about why the marker is biased.”

“Your tab does not open the cover.”

“I know.”

“There.”

“What?”

“You did it again.”

Ollie stopped.

Then he logged:

WITHIN CERTIFIED TOLERANCE — PHYSICAL MARKER HIGH
RECHECK NEXT CYCLE

He sent it to Mara.

No drama.

No access request.

Mara acknowledged with a single check mark.

Iri said, “That was almost normal.”

“Thanks.”

“It wasn’t praise.”

“Good.”

Break lasted eighteen minutes.

Ollie retrieved his tablet.

The first message was from Ethan.

ETHAN: How’s first shift?

The second:

ETHAN: Maya says asking that is annoying. Ignore Maya.

Then the group chat.

MAYA: he is WORKING

SAMIR: observed

AOIFE: ethnographic breakthrough

ETHAN: I asked one question.

MAYA: leader inspection

ETHAN: stop calling it that

OLLIE: alive

Three replies appeared instantly.

Then Ethan sent privately:

ETHAN: Heard you caught a traceability problem. Nice. Knew maintenance was the right choice for you.

Ollie read it.

He smiled.

Then became irritated that he had smiled.

Ethan was not his supervisor.

Ethan had not assigned him here.

Ethan did not get to stand outside the choice and certify that it had been correct.

The message had also been kind.

That was inconvenient.

Ollie typed:

Thanks, boss.

He looked at it.

Deleted it.

Typed:

It was a tag mismatch.

Ethan replied:

Still counts.

Ollie put the tablet down harder than necessary.

Iri was sitting at the far end of the break ledge with a drink pouch and something that smelled sharply of citrus and salt.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You put your tablet down like it had an opinion.”

“It did.”

“Bad opinion?”

“Accurate opinion.”

Iri nodded.

“Worst kind.”

Ollie looked over.

That was the first thing Iri had said all day that sounded like it might have been offered to him rather than merely near him.

Before Ollie could answer, Iri’s own device chimed.

Iri checked it and swore.

“What?”

“My workshop block moved.”

“Why?”

“Visitor fabrication group got extended.”

Ollie looked toward the fabrication schedule posted across the bay.

“You had a personal slot?”

“Had.”

“For what?”

Iri took another bite.

“No.”

“What?”

“You ask one question and then build six behind it.”

“I asked what you were making.”

“Yes.”

“So?”

“No.”

Ollie waited.

Iri smiled without looking at him.

Apparently ignoring questions could also be deliberate.

The shift alarm sounded.

Break over.

Mara passed them carrying the same unopened food container.

Iri pointed at it.

“You’re losing that argument.”

Mara kept walking.

“I have a launch coordination call in four minutes.”

“That is not eating.”

“The launch coordination call is why civilization does not deserve food.”

Ollie said, “I thought commissioning was the hard part.”

Mara stopped.

“Commissioning is engineering. Launch-facing work involves people asking whether a wall will look finished in photographs.”

She looked toward Ring Six.

“It will not.”

Then she continued down the service bay.

Iri watched her go.

“She hates the opening ceremony.”

“There’s an opening ceremony?”

“There is now.”

“Why?”

Iri looked at him.

“See? Visitor.”

* * *

The first shift ran forty-one minutes late.

The delay had nothing to do with Ollie. He checked the handover log once anyway, just to make sure.

A handover arrived with an unresolved actuator review and two missing custody confirmations from another bay. Nobody could close the chain until the incoming team physically accepted both.

Ollie could have left at scheduled end.

His youth placement did not require him to stay.

Mara told him so.

“You’re done.”

“The handover isn’t.”

“It will be.”

“I can finish the custody side.”

“You can.”

He waited.

Mara looked at him.

“That was not a request for volunteers.”

“Oh.”

“You have a study requirement tomorrow morning.”

“I know.”

“You are looking at me like that fact is offensive.”

“It’s not.”

“It will be when you ask for an extra diagnostic block next week.”

Ollie stopped.

Mara had already turned to the incoming technician.

He stayed another twelve minutes anyway because his own assigned cabinet row had one open acceptance item.

Then he left.

By the time he crossed into the public service loop, the group call had ended.

The chat contained a photograph of Maya making a terrible face, Samir half out of frame, Aoife apparently arguing with someone behind the camera, and Ethan holding something that might have been a bowl.

Underneath:

MAYA: ollie died for industry

SAMIR: unconfirmed

AOIFE: no body, no statement

ETHAN: We can call again.

Ollie looked at the time.

He had a host dinner in twenty-two minutes.

Forty minutes of coursework after that if he wanted tomorrow morning clear.

He sent:

OLLIE: tomorrow

Then he opened his residency account.

His first placement hours had released a small discretionary materials allowance.

The program information insisted this was not wages.

It could be used for:
- local food/import supplement;
- recreation;
- personal fabrication materials;
- approved workshop booking.

Ollie had been in orbit less than two days.

He had not bought anything for fun.

He had noticed that his charger housing had a hairline crack near the strain relief.

He selected:

FABRICATION MATERIAL — STANDARD POLYMER
WORKSHOP BOOKING — 22 MIN

A warning appeared.

NEXT AVAILABLE PERSONAL FABRICATION BLOCK:
SATURDAY 06:40

He stared.

Saturday.

06:40.

He accepted.

A voice beside him said, “That is upsetting.”

Ollie turned.

Iri had come through the service door behind him.

“What?”

“You just spent your first allowance on a machine.”

“It’s not pay.”

“That was not the accusation.”

“My charger housing is cracked.”

“Tragedy.”

“It will fail at the strain point.”

“Eventually.”

“That is when things fail.”

Iri’s regulator flashed amber again. They moved instinctively toward the cooler side of the service loop.

Ollie noticed.

He did not ask.

Iri checked the workshop board.

“My block is still gone.”

“You can take mine after I finish.”

Iri looked at him.

“You have twenty-two minutes.”

“I need eight.”

“You booked twenty-two.”

“That’s the minimum block.”

Iri considered this.

“What do you want for the rest?”

“Nothing.”

“That is suspicious.”

“It’s fourteen minutes.”

“Workshop time is workshop time.”

Ollie shrugged.

Iri looked at him for another second.

Then:

“Maybe.”

It was not thanks.

Ollie liked it better.

His work band chimed.

NEW ASSIGNMENT — NEXT SHIFT
RING SIX OCCUPANCY SETTLING VERIFICATION
07:20
REPORT TO M. SAYE

Ollie opened it immediately.

Resident movement.

Live environmental settling.

Diagnostic support.

Not command.

Not repair authority.

Still more than inventory.

Iri read his face.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You look terrible at nothing.”

Ollie showed the assignment.

Iri read it.

“Settling run.”

“You’ve done one?”

“Three.”

“What should I—”

Iri lifted one hand.

“No.”

Ollie stopped.

Iri smiled.

“Tomorrow.”

Then Iri turned toward the ringline.

Ollie stood in the service loop with a Saturday fabrication booking, unfinished coursework, a missed group call, and an assignment that started before anyone he knew would willingly be awake.

He checked the message again.

RING SIX OCCUPANCY SETTLING VERIFICATION

For the first time since arrival, the work ahead was exactly the kind he had come for.

He put the tablet away before Ethan could ask how the shift went.
