ORBITAL YOUTH
Chapter 20 — OSCILLATION

By the next morning, Ring Six had acquired laundry.

Ollie noticed because it had not been in any commissioning model.

A narrow drying rack had appeared inside the first occupied residential connector, clipped to a wall section that had been empty the night before. Three shirts, two towels and something small enough to belong to a child moved gently in the controlled airflow.

Across the common volume, somebody had stuck a handwritten label over a temporary storage marker.

TEA / NOT TOOLS.

Underneath, in different handwriting:

THIS MEANS YOU, SERVICE.

Ollie read it twice.

Ren came up behind him.

“You are inspecting laundry now?”

“I am observing occupancy.”

“You are reading a towel.”

“I am reading the system around the towel.”

“That is worse.”

The occupancy display showed R6-A at thirty-seven percent of planned density.

Below the forty-two-percent cap.

The disputed partition overlap remained blocked by commissioning control.

Reviewed route combinations only.

Enhanced monitoring active.

Resident and personal-interface reports visible on the same event rail as fixed-system changes.

Everything Ch19 had authorized was still true.

That mattered less to the residents than the fact that someone had already lost a cooking lid.

A household argument about it moved through the common volume while Ollie checked the first morning cycle.

The building trace was clean.

Not perfectly flat.

Occupied systems were never flat.

A school-route departure had created a small counterflow at 07:42.

A food delivery had held the east service access open nine seconds longer than predicted.

One resident had turned back because a child had forgotten a bag.

The local environmental correction had taken 1.6 seconds longer than the empty-state estimate.

All inside the authorized profile.

No disputed fixed lag.

No propagation.

No clustered reports.

Ollie saved the cycle.

Iri arrived carrying a service pouch and a folded strip of fabric.

Ollie looked at the fabric.

“No.”

Iri stopped.

“What?”

“I am not asking what that is.”

“Good.”

“You bring too many objects to work.”

“You inspect laundry.”

Ren made a noise that was almost a cough.

Ollie turned to him.

“Go away.”

“I am working here.”

“Tragic.”

A household cart crossed the common volume behind them.

The driver slowed at the route marker, checked the authorized service lane and took the longer path.

No one complained.

That was new.

Ollie watched the route state update.

SERVICE EAST — ACTIVE.

RESIDENTIAL CONNECTOR A — OCCUPIED.

COMMON VOLUME — OPEN.

PARTITION B — LOCKED UNTIL RETURN SETTLE.

The sequence was clean.

He moved on.

* * *

At 09:18, the first report appeared.

RESIDENT REPORT — EAR PRESSURE / SHORT DURATION.

No fixed-system alert.

Ollie opened the timestamp.

The resident had been walking from the common volume toward connector A after a service cart passed through the east lane.

Personal interface: no medical alarm.

Local adaptation recommendation: minor.

He marked it.

UNRESOLVED — WATCH.

Iri saw the rail update from across the bay.

They came over.

“Where?”

“Common to A.”

“What was moving?”

“Food cart. Normal route.”

“Door hold?”

“Nine seconds.”

“Long?”

“Allowed.”

Iri looked at him.

“That was not my question.”

“Longer than nominal. Still inside the reviewed range.”

Iri nodded and went back to the service line.

At 09:22, a second report appeared.

PERSONAL INTERFACE — LOCAL AIRFLOW ADAPTATION HIGHER THAN BASELINE.

Different resident.

Different route.

Same common volume.

Ollie pulled the fixed trace wider.

Nothing crossed the reduced stop threshold.

The return manifold showed a small correction and recovery.

His model projected settlement.

No prohibited overlap.

No fixed lag signature.

No reason for propagation.

He checked the first report against the second.

Four minutes apart.

Not the same-class threshold yet.

Mara arrived before he called her.

She had the reports open on her own display.

“Status?”

“Two early reports. No fixed threshold movement. Common volume involved in both.”

“Model?”

“Recovery.”

“Confidence?”

Ollie hesitated.

“Moderate.”

“Why not high?”

“Occupied service variation.”

Mara nodded.

“Freeze new household entry. Keep current residents in place unless safety changes. Service only.”

The housing channel protested within twenty seconds.

Mara ignored the first message.

The second came marked operational.

She answered that one.

INBOUND STAGING PAUSED — REPORT CLUSTER REVIEW.

No evacuation.

No alarm.

Just the next household waiting outside the occupied boundary with a cart full of bedding.

Ollie watched them stop.

A child sat on the cart.

The adult pushing it checked the delay notice and sighed.

The ring continued around them.

At 09:25, fixed systems still had not crossed threshold.

Ollie ran the sequence reconstruction.

The common volume correction had completed.

Partition B had remained locked exactly as required.

The east service door had closed.

There was no overlap.

The model returned:

RECOVERY EXPECTED.

Iri came back fast.

Not running.

Fast enough that Ollie noticed.

“You felt it?” he asked.

Iri put the service pouch down.

“Pressure change. Then the air felt wrong.”

“Wrong how?”

“Same as before.”

“Before which event?”

“The Ch15 report.”

“That did not escalate.”

“I know.”

Ollie hated how quickly the answer had arrived.

Iri looked at the trace.

“I am reporting it.”

“Yes.”

They entered the event themselves.

Third same-class report inside the short window.

The report logic changed color.

REVIEW THRESHOLD MET.

Mara said, “Reduce resident movement. Service hold. No nonessential crossings.”

The route system issued the instruction.

People slowed.

Not instantly.

A resident in connector A was already halfway back to the common volume for a bag left near the food area.

A waste-service cart was already committed to the east service lane.

The operator saw the hold notice and stopped at the boundary.

The cart was too long to clear the door behind it without reversing.

“Stay,” the local service coordinator told them. “Do not reverse across the occupied line.”

The operator stayed.

The east service door remained held open around the cart.

Allowed under the service safety rule.

Ollie saw the door state.

SERVICE EAST — HELD / OCCUPIED.

He checked the partition sequence.

Partition B remained locked.

Good.

The resident from connector A reached the common volume and picked up the forgotten bag.

A child followed two steps behind before an adult called them back.

The child stopped.

Turned.

The resident turned too.

Movement reversed.

The common-volume occupancy sensor updated.

The local environmental correction extended.

Two seconds.

Three.

Still inside occupied-state authority.

The waste cart operator asked, “Can I clear forward?”

The service coordinator checked the route.

The reviewed alternate exit through service C was available.

“Forward to C. Slow.”

The cart moved.

The east service door stayed open until the rear cleared.

At the same moment, a maintenance worker opened the C access to receive it.

Both doors were reviewed.

Both actions were allowed.

No prohibited partition moved.

Ollie watched the state list.

EAST SERVICE — OPEN.

COMMON RETURN CORRECTION — ACTIVE.

SERVICE C — OPEN.

PARTITION B — LOCKED.

His model still showed no overlap.

Then the return-manifold trace lifted.

Small.

Ollie leaned toward the display.

“Hold.”

Mara was already there.

“How much?”

“Below fixed stop.”

The line rose again.

The system corrected.

It came down.

Then up.

Not the old lag shape.

A pulse.

Recovery.

Another pulse.

Iri grabbed the edge of the station.

Ollie looked at them.

“What?”

“Again.”

Their personal interface lit.

LOCAL ADAPTATION — RAPID CHANGE.

Across the resident rail, two more reports appeared almost together.

EAR PRESSURE.

BREATHING RESISTANCE / TRANSIENT.

The fixed system remained under formal stop by less than one band.

Mara did not wait.

“Pre-stop. Close inflow. Prepare local isolation.”

The safety channel acknowledged.

The waste cart cleared service C.

East service began closing.

Service C began closing.

The common return correction tried to settle both volumes.

Ollie watched the state list collapse back toward the approved configuration.

It should have recovered.

The model said it would.

The return-manifold line rose again.

This time it crossed the reduced stop threshold.

The building finally agreed with the people.

Mara said, “STOP. R6-A isolate. Occupied evacuation profile.”

Everything moved at once.

* * *

The alarm was not loud.

That was the first thing Ollie noticed.

A voice cut through the common volume.

R6-A OCCUPANCY STOP.
FOLLOW LOCAL EXIT GUIDANCE.
DO NOT USE CLOSED SERVICE ROUTES.

The route markers changed.

West residential exit turned green.

East service turned red.

Connector A showed amber because its local pressure state had not settled enough for full release.

The adults moved before anyone looked at Ollie.

Good.

Mara was already on the safety channel.

“Count occupied.”

“Thirty-one inside R6-A.”

“Staff?”

“Nine service and commissioning.”

“West route capacity?”

“Twenty-four immediate. Second release after connector equalization.”

“Start first flow.”

The resident coordinator took the common volume.

“Households west. Bags only if already in hand. Leave carts.”

Someone asked about medication.

“Take it.”

A child tried to go back for a school case.

Their parent caught their shoulder.

“No.”

The child shouted that they needed it tomorrow.

The parent pulled them toward west.

Ollie stood at the diagnostic station because that was where Mara had left him.

The return trace pulsed again.

Lower amplitude.

Then higher.

Coupled oscillation.

He knew the shape now.

He had never seen it arrive this way.

The fixed lag had not preceded it.

The people had.

“Connector A not releasing,” safety said.

“Reason?” Mara asked.

“Pressure differential unstable. Auto-hold.”

“How many?”

“Seven residents, two staff.”

The west route took the first group out.

Lea’s household was in it except for her older brother, who had gone back to connector A for a work bag before the stop.

Lea saw the connector status.

“My brother is in A.”

The resident coordinator said, “He is counted.”

“I know he is counted.”

“He has staff with him.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“No. It is the answer I have.”

Lea looked toward Ollie.

Not because he had authority.

Because he knew the layout.

He pointed at the connector display.

“Secondary release is through west once the local differential settles. Do not go east. It is closed.”

Lea started toward the west waiting mark.

That was all he could do.

A service partition in the common volume cycled halfway and stopped.

The oscillation had thrown the pressure difference across its safety range.

The partition reopened automatically.

A maintenance worker beside it swore.

“Track fault?”

“Pressure interlock,” another worker said.

They moved a tool trolley away from the opening.

Too late.

The partition tried to close again as the differential dropped.

The trolley had one wheel inside the marked clearance line.

The maintenance worker grabbed it.

The next pressure pulse hit.

The partition safety system reversed.

The trolley swung sideways.

Its upper frame struck the worker’s forearm and knocked them into the wall.

They went down.

No dramatic sound.

Just metal, a short cry and the trolley rolling until another worker caught it.

Mara turned once.

“Medical common volume. One injury.”

A service tech was already kneeling beside the worker.

“Conscious. Arm injury. Bleeding minor.”

“Move when route clear.”

The injured worker said something Ollie could not hear.

The tech answered, “No, you are not finishing the shift.”

The return trace crossed threshold again.

A local equipment rack lost power.

Two interface panels went dark.

Emergency guidance remained live on the independent route strip.

The common volume lights shifted to evacuation mode.

Ollie looked at the state history.

East service open.

C open.

Return correction active.

Partition B locked.

No prohibited overlap.

Still no prohibited overlap.

“How?” he said.

Mara heard him.

“Later.”

Connector A finally moved from amber to green.

“Release A,” safety said.

The door opened.

Seven residents came out with two staff.

Lea’s brother was third.

He had the work bag.

Lea hit him in the shoulder with both hands and then hugged him before he could decide which response was appropriate.

“Why did you go back?”

“My work key was in it.”

“You are an idiot.”

“I need the key.”

“You are still an idiot.”

The resident coordinator moved them west.

The injured maintenance worker went next with medical support.

The final occupied count dropped.

Thirty-one.

Twenty-two.

Fourteen.

Six.

Zero residents.

Staff followed.

Mara stayed until safety confirmed the occupied sector clear.

Then she left too.

Ollie looked at the diagnostic station.

Nobody had told him to go.

He almost stayed because the data was still coming.

Iri grabbed his sleeve.

“Out.”

“I need the state history.”

“It will still exist.”

“The service sequence—”

“Ollie.”

He looked at them.

The route strip behind Iri was green.

“Out.”

He went.

* * *

Ring Six shut behind them at 09:41.

The occupied label disappeared first.

HABITATION — LIMITED / ACTIVE

became

OCCUPANCY SUSPENDED — SAFETY REVIEW.

Then access permissions changed.

Ollie’s commissioning credential went from active to review hold.

He stared at it.

Mara was beside the temporary command station talking to safety operations.

The injured maintenance worker had been taken to medical with what the local update called a probable forearm fracture and soft-tissue injury.

No life threat.

The equipment damage was still being counted.

One service partition track was offline.

A local interface rack had failed during the oscillation.

Two household carts remained inside because evacuation staff had left them where they were.

The residents did not care about the equipment count yet.

They cared about where they were sleeping.

Housing had reactivated temporary allocations automatically.

Not all of them still existed in the same configuration.

One household’s backup unit had been assigned to another staged move that morning.

The relocation team was fixing it in real time.

The school-route coordinator sent a message reversing the cancellations from the previous day.

Lea stood with her family beside three carts and one bag that had made it out because her brother had nearly missed evacuation to retrieve it.

Her mother was arguing with a housing coordinator.

Her brother was arguing with Lea.

The younger child had recovered the school case after staff brought out essential bags.

Nobody looked relieved enough for the fact that everyone was alive.

Ollie moved toward them.

Lea saw him.

He stopped.

She did not come over.

She looked at his credential display.

REVIEW HOLD.

Then at him.

Then away.

That was worse than anger.

He hated the thought as soon as it formed because it made her reaction about him.

Mara came over.

“You are off the system.”

“I saw.”

“That is procedural.”

“I know.”

“Do not make me regret saying that.”

Ollie closed his credential display.

“What happened?”

“We do not know yet.”

“The prohibited overlap never occurred.”

“I know.”

“The sequence stayed inside the authorized route set.”

“Yes.”

“The stop threshold worked.”

“Yes.”

He heard how desperate the list sounded.

Mara did too.

She did not soften.

“Those can all be true.”

“And the model can still be wrong.”

“Yes.”

Ollie looked back at the sealed ring.

“The transition was not in the model.”

Iri had come up behind them.

They were dirty from the evacuation route and still carrying the folded strip of fabric from the morning.

Ollie had forgotten it existed.

Iri said, “It was in the people.”

Ollie turned.

Iri did not explain.

They looked at the event history visible on the public review screen.

Three resident reports.

Then two more.

Then the first fixed threshold crossing.

The order was impossible to ignore now.

Mara said, “Nobody assigns cause before review.”

Iri nodded.

Ollie said nothing.

A housing worker called Lea’s household name.

They had a temporary unit again.

Different sector.

Different route.

Her mother pushed the first cart.

Lea took the second.

The younger child carried the school case.

Her brother carried the work bag.

They moved away from Ring Six.

Behind them, two service workers rolled out an empty medical trolley.

On Ollie’s display, the model remained locked behind REVIEW HOLD.

The resident-report rail was still visible.

Five entries before the building crossed its threshold.
