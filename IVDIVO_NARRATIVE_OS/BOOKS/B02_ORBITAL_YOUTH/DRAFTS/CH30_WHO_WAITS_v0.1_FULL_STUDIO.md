# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 30 — WHO WAITS

Aoife spent the first forty minutes carrying other people’s things and discovering that nobody packed for temporary housing in the same way.

One family had labeled every container.

Another had written NOTHING IMPORTANT on three boxes containing, respectively, school materials, medication chargers and a plant that objected to being moved by folding its leaves against the lid.

A boy younger than Maya had packed six shirts and no sleep clothes.

His mother found this out in the corridor.

“You had one job.”

“I had several jobs.”

“You had one bag.”

“That contained several decisions.”

“It contains six shirts.”

“They were difficult decisions.”

Aoife looked away before he noticed her smiling.

Her recorder stayed inside her bag.

That should not have felt like an action.

It did.

Nadia had assigned her to a temporary-support table near the route-transfer point, which sounded administrative and turned out to mean being asked every question nobody knew where else to ask.

Where does this box go if the room is not cleared yet?

Can school equipment go ahead of the household?

Does a laundry booking survive a relocation date change?

If two people move and two wait, which address does a delivery system believe?

Can somebody visit the opened segment if their own household is still temporary?

The last question produced three answers in nine minutes.

“Yes, during permitted guest periods.”

“No, if the route crosses the closed service boundary.”

“Maybe, if the receiving household has an active guest allocation and the visitor does not trigger the density cap.”

The woman asking stared at Aoife.

“So which is it?”

Aoife checked the route.

“Your sister’s room is in Three.”

“Yes.”

“You are in temporary housing on the other side of Five.”

“Yes.”

“The direct route crosses Five, so no. The long route through One is allowed during guest hours.”

“How long?”

Aoife told her.

The woman closed her eyes.

“That is ridiculous.”

“Yes.”

“Are you allowed to say that?”

“I am not employed here.”

“That explains the honesty.”

Aoife printed the long-route token anyway.

The woman took it.

“Still going.”

“To see your sister?”

“To use her washing machine.”

Aoife looked at the two bags of laundry beside her.

“Important distinction.”

“Extremely.”

There was a story in that.

Aoife felt the shape of it immediately.

A ring partially opens; people cross twenty-seven extra minutes for laundry; infrastructure reveals what home actually means.

Opening image: laundry bag against polished orbital wall.

Cut to route map.

Voice over—

She stopped herself.

The woman was already gone.

Aoife moved the next box.

* * *

By midday she had acquired tape, a temporary route marker and a small argument with an eight-year-old about whether one stuffed animal counted as essential transport weight.

“It weighs almost nothing,” the child said.

“That is not the issue.”

“What is the issue?”

“The bag has to close.”

“It closes if you sit on it.”

“You are not traveling inside the bag.”

“I could.”

The child’s father said, “Please do not give her ideas.”

“I think she already has them.”

The stuffed animal traveled.

Three boxes of stored kitchen equipment did not.

Aoife carried a school case to a transfer shelf and found a girl her own age sitting on the floor beside it, reading tomorrow’s assignment off a wrist display.

“You moving today?” Aoife asked.

The girl shook her head.

“My brother is.”

“Split household?”

“Until Four opens. Or until we give up and take two places farther apart.”

She tapped the school case.

“That is his.”

“You are carrying it?”

“He forgot it.”

“Useful sibling.”

“He is twelve. His brain is decorative.”

Aoife laughed.

The girl looked at her bag.

“You’re the Earth documentary one.”

Aoife’s hand went instinctively toward the strap.

Not to take the recorder out.

To make sure it was still closed.

“Yes.”

“Are you filming this?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

The question caught her harder than suspicion would have.

“Because I’m helping.”

“You can do both.”

“I know.”

The girl waited.

Aoife disliked how much that silence resembled an interview pause.

She said, “I don’t want everything I understand to become material.”

The girl shrugged.

“Okay.”

No approval.

No lesson.

She went back to the assignment.

Aoife picked up the school case.

“Where does your brother need this?”

The girl sent the destination.

Aoife left.

Halfway through the transfer she caught herself composing the exchange in her head.

She changed routes just to give herself something else to pay attention to.

The alternate path was slower and crowded with move carts.

That helped.

* * *

The person Aoife had once called Source S1 in her notes was standing beside a stack of sealed storage bins when Aoife came back.

She almost missed them because they were not waiting for her.

They were arguing with a logistics clerk about a damaged latch.

“It held when it left temporary storage.”

“It does not hold now.”

“I can see that.”

“So mark it.”

“I need the transfer record.”

“The transfer record will not make the latch close.”

“It will make somebody else replace it.”

The source looked at the bin.

Then at the clerk.

“Fine.”

Aoife kept walking.

“Farada.”

She stopped.

Nobody else on Vela called her that.

The source was looking at the tape dispenser in her hand.

“You have the wide seal?”

Aoife checked.

“Yes.”

“Give it.”

She handed it over.

The source resealed the bin, then pressed the damaged latch twice as if a third opinion might change it.

It did not.

“Temporary fix,” Aoife said.

“I know.”

They both paused.

Aoife nearly smiled.

The source noticed.

“Do not.”

“Wasn’t going to.”

“That was a lie.”

“Small one.”

The source handed the tape back.

For a few seconds they stood beside the bins with no interview, no approval screen and no agreed frame between them.

Aoife wanted to ask six questions.

She asked none.

The source looked toward the open-segment route where two move carts were waiting for clearance.

“My room is not in the first phase.”

Aoife nodded.

“I heard.”

“You did not hear from me.”

“No.”

Another pause.

Then the source said, “Temporary housing is mostly waiting for somebody else’s timetable to become your timetable.”

Aoife looked at them.

The source looked irritated immediately.

“What?”

“That is good.”

“I did not say it for you.”

“I know.”

The source exhaled through their nose.

“You may use that later.”

Aoife did not move.

The recorder was in her bag.

She could have taken it out in two seconds.

“Later?” she asked.

“If you still think it matters later.”

“Context?”

“Waiting. No body detail. No room detail. No implication that I represent anyone.”

Aoife nodded.

“Understood.”

The source picked up the smaller bin.

Aoife reached for the larger one.

“I can carry that.”

“I know.”

Aoife took it anyway.

They walked together as far as the temporary-storage junction.

Nothing else important was said.

That was probably why it mattered.

* * *

Tomas’s new route began with a chair.

Aoife had expected a transport map.

Instead she found him sitting outside a service planning office with his supported forearm resting across his stomach and a cup balanced between two fingers of his other hand.

“You following me?” he asked.

“No.”

“Disappointing.”

“Nadia said you could show me the changed commute.”

“That is following with paperwork.”

“More respectable.”

“Much worse.”

He stood.

The light-duty assignment had moved him away from manual service access and into scheduling support. The work itself was easier on his arm.

Getting to it was not.

Before the incident he had used a short maintenance transfer that was now restricted during Ring Six staging.

The new route added one transfer and twelve minutes on a good cycle.

“Today?” Aoife asked.

“Seventeen.”

“Why?”

“Move traffic.”

They joined a corridor full of families, service carts and people trying to look as though they knew which temporary arrows applied to them.

Tomas nodded toward an opened service connection.

“That helps.”

“You opposed partial opening.”

“I opposed opening on the old model.”

“Not the same thing.”

“No.”

The opened connection cut six minutes from the second half of his route.

The first half was still worse because relocation traffic had taken over one of the efficient transfers.

“So better or worse?” Aoife asked.

Tomas looked at her.

“You have been spending too much time with Ethan.”

“That is offensive.”

“Then stop asking for one answer.”

He took a slower handrail turn to protect the injured arm.

“Work is easier. Route is worse. The opened connector helps. Move traffic hurts. My arm is still annoying. I get home later than before. Next week might be different.”

Aoife nodded.

“Terrible documentary structure.”

“Good.”

They reached the next transfer.

A household from the Ch29 waiting list was there with two storage bags and no move authorization.

Aoife recognized the older resident who had argued in Ethan’s consequence review.

They recognized her too.

“Still not recording?”

“No.”

“Shame. I have become more dramatic.”

Their household had supported partial opening once the segment plan existed.

Their own allocation had not cleared.

An accessibility route still crossed a closed segment.

“So people are moving around you,” Aoife said.

“Yes.”

“Regret supporting it?”

The resident looked through the transparent transfer wall toward a cart carrying somebody else’s boxes.

“Today? A little.”

Tomas said, “That is allowed.”

“I know.”

The resident adjusted one of the storage bags.

“My sister got a shorter care route because Three opened. I am still sleeping in a room where the wall wakes me at five because the neighbors start shift then.”

They shrugged.

“Both are true.”

Aoife felt the story trying to form again.

Supporter left behind by policy they supported.

Contradiction as hook.

She hated how quickly her brain did it.

The resident noticed her expression.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That looked like a camera face.”

Aoife laughed despite herself.

“Apparently I have one.”

“You do.”

“Sorry.”

“I did not say stop having it.”

The transfer arrived.

Tomas stepped in.

The resident stayed behind for a different route.

Aoife followed Tomas.

On the other side, an opened corridor cut straight through what had been a long detour the week before.

People were already using it badly.

A child stopped in the center to retie a shoe.

A delivery worker complained about the new turn radius.

Two teenagers sat where a route notice said not to sit.

Someone had taped a handwritten arrow under the official one because the official one was technically correct and practically useless from the approach angle.

Tomas looked at it.

“Home.”

Aoife smiled.

“Can I quote that?”

He looked at her.

She raised both hands.

“Joke.”

“Your timing needs work.”

“So does the route.”

“Better.”

At the end of the corridor, her device buzzed.

ETHAN: deadline moved up

Then:

ETHAN: need to answer adult-track placement tonight

Aoife read it once.

Did not reply immediately.

Tomas was already walking toward the service office.

Behind them, one family was moving into Ring Six.

Another was waiting for a route that did not exist yet.

The same opening had made both facts possible.

Aoife put the device away and picked up the next box.
