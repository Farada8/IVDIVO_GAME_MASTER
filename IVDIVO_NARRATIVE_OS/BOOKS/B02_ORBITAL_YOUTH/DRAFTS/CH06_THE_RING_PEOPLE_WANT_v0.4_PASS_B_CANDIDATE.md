# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 6 — THE RING PEOPLE WANT

Ring Six smelled new.

Not clean. New.

Clean was filtration and disinfectant and a maintenance team doing its job. New was polymer sealant still losing its factory smell, wall fabric nobody had leaned against yet, floor grip with every ridge sharp, and a public corridor with no scratches where people cut corners badly.

The lights were warmer than the service bay.

The walls were not.

Ring Six had not decided what kind of place it was yet.

Ollie followed Iri through a residential junction and tried not to stare.

“You can stop pretending,” Iri said.

“Pretending what?”

“That you don’t want to stare.”

“I’m observing.”

“Visitor.”

Ollie let that go.

The junction opened into a small civic volume built around a shallow vertical garden. Real plants, not projected ones. A maintenance rail ran up behind the foliage where most people would never notice it unless someone left the access panel open.

Someone had.

Beyond the garden were three food counters, only one operating. A youth room with movable walls. A laundry bank. Parcel lockers. A narrow staircase toward a view gallery with a temporary barrier across the entrance.

Two children were sitting on the barrier anyway.

One of them had a shoe off.

Nothing about the place felt like a station brochure.

Ollie liked it immediately.

“Why is the view gallery closed?”

“Booking calibration.”

A scheduling strip beside the stairs showed twenty-minute room slots.

Ollie stopped walking.

“You schedule the view?”

“Two hundred people. One room.”

“Earth restaurants have better priorities.”

Iri looked at him.

“You would choose food over stars?”

“Yes.”

“Fair.”

They crossed toward the laundry bank.

Half the machines were dark. The others carried temporary test tags.

WATER RECOVERY LOOP — RESIDENTIAL LOAD TEST ONLY

Ollie read one automatically.

Iri said, “You read with your whole face.”

He looked away from the tag.

“I don’t know what that means.”

“It worked.”

Today was not a maintenance shift. Mara had made that clear before they entered.

Occupancy settling verification.

The system did not need Ollie to find something. It needed him to watch people use things.

He preferred machines that admitted when they needed him.

At the far side of the civic volume, a woman in a gray housing vest was dealing with a delivery crew.

“That cart goes to temporary storage until Sector Six-C is released.”

“It’s assigned.”

“To a household that is not moving today.”

“They booked the freight slot.”

“The room failed final access verification yesterday. Temporary storage.”

“We don’t have capacity.”

“I released two staging cages at oh-six-hundred.”

The crew lead checked his display.

“One.”

The woman leaned over the screen.

One cage still showed occupied.

“Who put school furniture in freight staging?”

A voice behind Ollie said, “Probably a school.”

A girl about Ollie’s age stood beside collapsible storage boxes, arms folded.

“Lea.”

“Nadia.”

“Do you know anything about this?”

“No.”

Nadia waited.

Lea shifted her bag higher on one shoulder.

“I have practice.”

“You don’t.”

“I could.”

Nadia Park looked at the boxes instead.

“Those yours?”

“Some.”

Lea pointed. “Those three. That one. Not that one. That one is Tomas.”

A man sitting on one of the boxes lifted a hand without standing.

“Mine.”

“Get off the freight,” Nadia said.

“I’m not freight.”

“The box is.”

“It knows me.”

Nadia pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose and kept working.

Mara came through the service access with a tablet under one arm.

“Nadia. Six minutes.”

“Not while this crew is trying to put school furniture in family storage.”

Mara looked past her.

A long low cart blocked half the junction.

She pointed at the clear side of the corridor.

“Move the cart there. Keep the seam open.”

The crew moved it.

Nadia watched them obey.

“This is why people think you run everything.”

Mara was already checking the service seam.

“Start in five.”

She looked at Ollie and Iri.

“Why are you standing there?”

“Observing residents,” Iri said.

“Poorly,” Ollie added.

Nadia glanced at him.

“You’re the Earth maintenance placement.”

Ollie hesitated.

“That sounds worse when you say it.”

“It usually does.”

She held out a hand.

“Nadia Park. Relocation.”

“Ollie.”

“I saw your placement file.”

From beside the boxes, Lea said, “You’re with Maya?”

“Yeah.”

“She’s in the youth transit channel.”

“What did she do?”

“Spent ten minutes telling people the fastest route wasn’t the fastest route.”

Ollie nodded.

“That sounds right.”

Lea went back to her messages.

Nadia pointed toward her.

“Lea. Future resident, if the universe stops adding forms.”

Lea lifted two fingers.

“Tomas. Also future resident, if his employer doesn’t fire him before we get him closer than forty-eight minutes from his shift.”

Tomas stayed on the box.

“Thirty-nine if the ringline behaves.”

“It doesn’t,” Nadia said.

Ollie looked at the stack around them.

“You’re moving today?”

Lea laughed.

“We have been not moving today for eleven days.”

“Eight,” Nadia said without looking up.

“Eleven emotionally.”

Nadia sent the freight crew toward temporary storage.

“Today is a realistic occupancy run. Bags, route changes, laundry load, meal service, ordinary door use. If it behaves, tomorrow gets larger.”

“And if it doesn’t?” Ollie asked.

“People stay where they are while the old-sector overhaul takes another service corridor away from us.”

“How long?”

“Twenty-three days before the next closure.”

Lea said, “Twenty-two if you count the day we lose moving things twice.”

Nadia kept walking.

“I count operational days.”

“I live in all of them.”

That ended the exchange.

Ollie looked toward the unopened residential corridor.

* * *

Lea’s assigned room was small.

Not unpleasant. Small.

The sleeping surface folded out over storage. The desk occupied a wall recess. One panel could become wardrobe depth or extra floor space, but not both at once.

Lea demonstrated this as if she had personally designed the flaw.

“Desk open, closet annoying. Closet open, desk annoying. Bed open, everything annoying.”

“You picked this room?” Ollie asked.

“I picked this sector.”

“Why?”

She pointed through the doorway.

The corridor curved toward a radial lift two junctions down.

“School is one transfer. My sister’s training block is two. My mother’s shift is one ringline plus service loop. And I can get to the west youth bay without crossing the spine.”

“View?”

“Wall.”

“You don’t care?”

“I care less than sleeping.”

Nadia, in the hall, said, “That goes in the brochure.”

Lea opened a storage panel.

“Also this is bigger than temporary.”

“How many people are in temporary with you?”

“Four.”

“In one room?”

“In two rooms pretending to be three.”

Tomas leaned against the opposite wall.

“I have one room pretending to be a hallway.”

Lea pointed at him.

“He chose commute over privacy.”

“I chose employment over privacy.”

“That sounds less fun.”

“It is.”

Ollie looked at Tomas.

“You commute forty-eight minutes?”

“Today.”

“From Old Ring Two?”

Tomas smiled.

“Try the map at shift change with one service loop closed.”

Nadia added, “And a child who has to be dropped in a different gravity band first.”

Tomas nodded.

“That too.”

“You have a child?” Ollie asked.

“My sister has a child. I have mornings.”

He left it there.

Mara called from the corridor.

“Occupancy group one.”

Everyone moved.

Ollie had expected people with badges walking prescribed routes.

There were badges.

There were routes.

The people ruined the neat part immediately.

Lea forgot one storage box and went back.

Tomas stopped at a food counter that was supposed to be closed because somebody had decided the test needed actual service demand.

A woman with two children entered the laundry volume early.

One child found the vertical garden and refused to leave.

A delivery cart arrived from the wrong side because the intended service loop was occupied by cleaners.

Nadia let the mess happen.

Mara watched the boundary conditions.

Ollie looked between them.

“This is allowed?”

Nadia kept pace with the family in front of her.

“This is living here.”

Mara checked the load display.

“Still inside limits.”

The environmental panel on Ollie’s wrist showed the ring settling inside expected bands.

Door cycles.

Local temperature.

Humidity.

Airflow demand.

Nothing interesting.

Good.

He still wanted something to happen.

Iri stood beside an environmental transition watching people rather than the display.

Ollie followed their attention.

Lea had slowed just past the transition.

Not stopped. Slowed.

One hand touched the side of her neck.

Then she kept walking.

Temperature normal.

Humidity normal.

Pressure differential normal.

Flow correction normal.

“Lea,” Nadia called. “You okay?”

“Yeah. Dry for a second.”

Mara checked the environmental panel.

Iri’s wrist regulator pulsed amber.

Ollie saw it.

Iri adjusted nothing.

“What did yours do?”

“Local correction.”

“For dry?”

“For the change.”

The fixed display stayed green.

Mara said, “Log the report.”

Ollie entered:

RESIDENT REPORT: BRIEF DRY / THROAT SENSATION
FIXED ENVIRONMENTAL DATA: WITHIN BAND

His eyes returned to Iri’s regulator.

“Can review export its timing?”

Iri pulled the sleeve back over the display.

“If they need it, they can ask me.”

Ollie left the question there.

The group continued.

No alarm.

No second complaint.

By the laundry bank, the moment had almost disappeared into ordinary movement.

Then the fixed humidity return shifted.

A small correction.

The system compensated.

Green stayed green.

Ollie opened the trace.

The change had appeared after Lea’s report.

“How many seconds?”

Mara checked the event rail and sent him the timestamp pair.

“Eleven. Keep it as timing, not a theory.”

Ollie compared the two marks.

Iri’s regulator had already returned to green.

Eleven seconds fit inside too many harmless explanations to deserve a name. A local personal regulator responding before an averaged fixed sensor was exactly the kind of thing a local regulator might do.

The occupancy group reached the youth room.

The movable wall opened.

Someone on the other side had left three chairs in the wrong place.

Two people laughed. A child claimed one.

Lea sat on another and pulled up the housing layout.

“This wall moves?”

“During approved configuration windows,” Nadia said.

“So not when I want.”

“No.”

“That is oppression.”

“Put it in writing.”

“I will.”

Tomas dropped into the third chair.

“If this opens by next month, I get thirty minutes of my life back every day.”

Nadia glanced at his route profile.

“Closer to forty.”

“Don’t oversell it.”

“You’re the one living the commute.”

“Exactly.”

Lea looked at Ollie.

“Does this count as a successful test?”

Ollie glanced at Mara.

She kept reading the run data.

Everything remained green.

One subjective report.

One local regulator pulse.

One delayed fixed correction.

No threshold breach. No fault.

“So far,” Ollie said.

Lea groaned.

“That is such an engineer answer.”

“I’m not an engineer.”

“Somehow worse.”

Nadia checked the time.

“Group two in four minutes.”

Lea stood.

“Do we get to stay?”

“Not today.”

“I have boxes.”

“You have boxes everywhere.”

“That is basically residency.”

Nadia pointed toward the exit without slowing the next group’s prep.

Lea left.

Tomas followed more slowly.

At the doorway he looked back at the chair, not the wall or systems, as if he had already decided where it belonged.

Ollie watched him go.

Then he reopened the trace.

Eleven seconds.

Mara closed it from her side of the shared display.

“Eleven seconds isn’t a discovery.”

“It’s a comparison point.”

“Then compare it if it happens again.”

“Next group?”

Mara nodded toward the transition.

Ollie moved into position.

Iri stayed where they were for another moment, looking down the route Lea had taken.

“Same path?” they asked.

“Mostly.”

Iri glanced at the service divergence on the floor map.

“Then don’t call it the same run later.”

Ollie marked the route difference before the second group entered.

This group had more people.

Someone carried groceries. Someone else had a folded laundry bag. A boy argued with his father about whether a wall panel counted as a desk if nobody wanted to use it as one.

Nadia was already redirecting a delivery cart.

Mara was already telling somebody to keep the service seam clear.

Ring Six looked less new.

Only slightly.

Ollie watched the people first.

Then the display.
