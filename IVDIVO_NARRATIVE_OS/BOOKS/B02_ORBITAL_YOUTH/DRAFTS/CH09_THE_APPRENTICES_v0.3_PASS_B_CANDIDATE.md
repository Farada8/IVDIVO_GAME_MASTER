# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 9 — THE APPRENTICES

Ollie lost his workshop slot before he reached the workshop.

The notice appeared while he was still on the ringline.

PERSONAL FABRICATION BLOCK — 06:40  
STATUS: REALLOCATED  
PRIORITY ACCESS: COMMISSIONING SUPPORT

He stared at it, then opened the detail.

The slot had not been taken by Ring Six. It had been taken by his placement.

His access profile had been moved to a commissioning-support block later in the day, and the system had automatically released the personal slot to another apprentice.

13:20 — SHARED FABRICATION / COMMISSIONING SUPPORT  
ACCESS LEVEL: YOUTH PLACEMENT — LIMITED

Better access. Worse time. And not his.

He checked the name attached to the released 06:40 block.

IRI.

Of course.

The ringline doors opened.

Ollie got out.

Iri was not at the maintenance bay.

That was unusual enough that Mara looked at the wall clock twice.

“Where is Iri?”

Nobody answered.

The local apprentices at the first station were already working.

One of them, Kalen, glanced toward the service entrance.

“Missed the transfer.”

Mara said, “Iri does not miss transfers.”

Kalen shrugged.

“Today apparently disagrees.”

Ollie clipped in.

“What happened?”

Kalen kept calibrating the rack.

“Good morning to you too.”

“Morning. What happened?”

“Missed transfer. That’s all I know.”

Then Kalen noticed Ollie’s access tab.

“You got fabrication support?”

“Yeah.”

“Today?”

Ollie nodded.

Kalen turned back to the rack.

“Nice.”

It did not sound nice.

Ollie looked at the other apprentices.

Nobody volunteered to translate.

He had been in the bay long enough to know when a silence belonged to work and when it belonged to him.

This one belonged to him.

Mara handed him a physical checklist.

“Environmental transition run archive. Compare yesterday’s movement sequence against service-state logs.”

“I was already on that.”

“Yesterday you were making notes. Today you have a defined comparison.”

Ollie opened the list.

Samir’s Service Four observation had been added to the bottom as an external timing note.

No names. No team data.

SERVICE TRANSFER 4  
21:38  
SEQUENCE IRREGULARITY / DIRECTION REVERSAL  
LOCAL AIRFLOW CHANGE REPORTED BEFORE SYSTEM CORRECTION  
TIMING ESTIMATE: <10 SEC

Ollie looked up.

“This is Samir’s.”

“You logged it,” Mara said. “Review retained it as a non-certified reference.”

Ollie checked the label.

REFERENCE OBSERVATION — NON-CERTIFIED

“You wanted another timing source,” Mara said. “You have one observation.”

“That doesn’t prove anything.”

“It isn’t being asked to.”

Kalen made a small sound from the calibration rack.

Mara turned her head.

“Something useful?”

Kalen shook his head and kept working.

Ollie took the checklist to the side station.

He could feel Kalen’s annoyance behind him.

He ignored it for thirty seconds.

Then stopped.

“What did I take?”

Kalen did not turn.

“What?”

“My placement access. What did it displace?”

Kalen scanned a tool and returned it to its slot before answering.

“I asked for a support block last cycle.”

“What happened?”

“Waitlist.”

“And this cycle?”

Kalen tapped Ollie’s temporary access color with one finger.

“Your cohort arrived.”

Ollie put the checklist down.

“I said workshop scheduling could be fixed.”

“You said that before you had priority access.”

“That wasn’t—”

Kalen picked up the next calibration tool.

Ollie stopped trying to repair the sentence.

“Did I take your block?”

“Not a named block. Priority moved above local credit requests.”

“I didn’t make that rule.”

Kalen’s scanner chirped twice before he answered.

“I know.”

“Then why are you mad at me?”

Kalen finally looked over.

“Because I waited six weeks for access you got in six days.”

That landed without needing explanation.

Ollie opened his mouth, then closed it.

Kalen went back to work.

Mara called from across the bay.

“Kalen. Ollie. The rack is still winning.”

They both returned to their stations.

Ollie did not ask whether the youth-residency showcase schedule was fair.

Not yet.

* * *

Iri arrived twenty-three minutes late.

Not running.

That somehow made it worse.

The service door opened, Iri came through, clipped in, and went straight to Mara.

“Missed handoff.”

Mara checked the time.

“My cousin’s host transfer changed,” Iri said. “Parent shift moved. Replacement host wasn’t at the expected ringline.”

“And the route?”

“I assumed Service Two was still faster.”

Kalen called from across the bay, “It was not.”

Iri gave him a look.

Ollie said, “You took yesterday’s shortcut.”

“It was a good shortcut yesterday.”

“Today?”

“Educational.”

“You could have messaged.”

“I did.”

Mara checked her display. A notification appeared, then another.

“It arrived eleven minutes ago.”

“I sent it twenty-six minutes ago.”

“Route network queue?”

“Maybe.”

Mara’s expression shifted into work mode.

“Show me after this block. You lose twenty-three credited minutes today; recover Thursday.”

Iri nodded and clipped on a work tab.

Ollie waited until Mara moved away.

“Your cousin?”

“Yes.”

“You do host handoffs?”

“Sometimes.”

“How old?”

Iri kept clipping in.

Ollie understood the answer before Iri gave one.

“Right. None of my business.”

Iri glanced at the fabrication schedule.

“My block came back.”

“The 06:40?”

“It was mine before it was yours.”

Ollie checked the history and found the earlier reservation.

“Okay.”

Iri looked almost disappointed by the lack of argument.

Ollie said, “Percussion rig?”

That changed Iri’s face.

“How do you know about that?”

Ollie immediately regretted the question.

“You had a rehearsal booking last week. Youth performance volume. Same day as the workshop block.”

“You went looking after I stopped answering you.”

“It was on the public schedule.”

“That does not make it less looking.”

Ollie felt heat rise into his face.

He started to defend the search, heard what it would sound like, and stopped.

“Sorry.”

Iri tightened the regulator band at the wrist.

“That works.”

Ollie was starting to hate approval delivered in two words.

“What are you making?”

Iri looked at him.

Ollie lifted both hands.

“Never mind.”

They worked.

* * *

The archive comparison did not make the problem cleaner.

That was beginning to feel personal.

Ollie aligned four traces:

Ring Six first occupancy.

Ring Six controlled run three.

Ring Six controlled run four.

Samir’s Service Four observation.

The building logs gave him door states, flow correction, local demand, averaged return.

The people gave him phrases.

Dry. Warm. Pressure. Wrong. Too fast.

Not always the same words. Not always the same order.

Kalen passed behind him carrying a tool case.

“You’re doing it again.”

“What?”

“Waiting for reality to organize itself for you.”

He kept walking before Ollie could answer.

Iri joined from the other side.

“What are you comparing?”

“Reported onset against fixed response.”

Iri leaned closer without touching the screen.

Ollie had built a side table.

REPORT LANGUAGE  
ESTIMATED ONSET  
LOCAL INTERFACE RESPONSE  
FIXED RETURN SHIFT  
DOOR STATE  
MOVEMENT ORDER

Iri pointed at the first column.

“The words are here. Your calculation never reads them.”

“Because ‘felt dry’ is not a calibrated value.”

“So what are you using it for?”

“Timing, maybe. Once I know whether three different words describe the same kind of change.”

Iri’s regulator pulsed amber.

The bay had warmed slightly as a service cart opened the adjacent partition.

Iri stepped half a pace toward the cooler side without looking.

Ollie noticed.

“What did that feel like?”

“Warm.”

“How warm?”

“Annoying.”

“That isn’t a unit.”

“It is enough to move me half a step.”

Ollie looked at the regulator log.

“I’m trying to make this usable.”

“For the model?”

“For comparing events.”

Iri tapped the report-language column.

“Then compare what people do too.”

Ollie pulled the previous run beside it.

“If three people use different words for the same environmental change, I still need to know whether they mean the same thing.”

“Ask when. Ask what they did. Don’t start with the building’s answer.”

“The building has a clock.”

“So do local interfaces.”

“People don’t have eleven-second clocks.”

Iri reached toward the regulator log, then stopped before touching Ollie’s screen.

“Yesterday my interface responded at 09:52:14.2. I felt the transition before the regulator.”

“How much before?”

“I don’t know.”

Ollie marked the missing value instead of filling it.

Iri watched him do it.

“If four people say ‘wrong’ before your fixed return changes, maybe the useful question is not whether they agree on the word.”

“Maybe.”

Iri waited.

Ollie hated maybe.

He typed:

HUMAN REPORTS — UNCALIBRATED / TIMING VALUE POSSIBLE

Iri read it.

“Ugly.”

“Accurate.”

“Keep it.”

The Samir observation had no body interface, no humidity number, no certified fixed onset. Just sequence, timing estimate and airflow change.

Not proof.

Enough to keep beside the others.

* * *

At break, the apprentices ate in the older service corner Iri liked.

It had bad seating.

Iri had been right.

The food was better. Not dramatically; enough to make the famous view rooms seem like an inefficient use of surface area.

Ollie sat with Iri, Kalen, and two apprentices he only knew by sight.

Nobody explained who sat where. Ollie took the empty seat.

Kalen looked at his tray.

“You buy the cheapest thing every time.”

“I like it.”

“You haven’t tried anything else.”

One of the other apprentices leaned over to inspect the meal.

“That is fear with a receipt.”

Ollie kept eating.

Kalen nodded toward his temporary tab.

“He has a tab now.”

“Temporary,” Iri said.

“Still.”

The word landed differently than before.

Status.

Ollie almost asked what the tab meant to all of them.

Kalen saw it coming.

“Eat lunch.”

Ollie ate lunch.

Thirty seconds passed.

One of the apprentices said, “It means if you screw up, someone else has to explain why you had access.”

Kalen said, “Trusted enough to be dangerous.”

Iri kept eating.

Ollie did not ask for a third definition.

A message flashed on Iri’s band.

Iri checked it and swore.

“Rehearsal?” Kalen asked.

“Moved.”

“Again?”

“Performer volume lost one lane.”

“To what?”

“Relocation staging.”

Kalen looked at Ollie.

“Your ring.”

Ollie pointed at his tray. “I was eating lunch successfully.”

“Briefly.”

Iri put the band down harder than necessary.

Ollie said, “When is the performance?”

“Vela Night.”

“You’re playing?”

“No. We’re moving equipment in silence for forty minutes.”

Kalen laughed.

Iri let him finish.

“Yes.”

“What do you play?”

Iri looked at the table.

Ollie waited, then returned to his food.

“Low-g frame percussion,” Iri said eventually.

Ollie had no idea what that meant.

He did not ask.

Iri noticed.

“That hurt, didn’t it?”

“Yes.”

The break alarm sounded.

Kalen stood.

“Commissioning visitor gets the diagnostic station.”

Ollie stood too.

“I can take inventory.”

Kalen paused with his hand on the station tab.

“You wanted this.”

“So do you.”

“Why give it up?”

Ollie picked up the inventory scanner.

“You’re faster on the calibration chain.”

Kalen took the station without thanking him.

That felt more honest.

Iri stayed beside Ollie for another second.

“You still have the afternoon block.”

“Yeah.”

Then Iri went to calibration and Ollie opened the first cabinet.

The work was boring.

He did it anyway.

* * *

At 15:06, Mara posted the next apprentice access schedule.

Everyone looked. Nobody pretended not to.

Two diagnostic-support blocks were open for the next commissioning cycle.

One at 17:10 on Vela Night.

One at 19:00.

The later block was better. It included live transition calibration.

The earlier one was mostly prep, verification and tool custody before the public event increased transit load.

Ollie tapped 19:00.

PRIORITY ELIGIBLE — YOUTH COHORT PLACEMENT

Underneath:

LOCAL APPRENTICE CREDIT REQUEST — KALEN

Ollie stopped.

Kalen was looking at a different screen.

Not at him.

Ollie opened the 17:10 block.

No competing credit request.

It ended after the first Vela Night performance window had begun.

He had promised Maya he would at least try to show up before she decided all engineers were socially defective.

He looked at the 19:00 block again.

Better work. Worse choice.

Mara came up beside him.

“You can take either.”

“Does my priority override Kalen’s request?”

“Yes.”

Kalen said from the other station, “You can use my name while discussing my future.”

Ollie turned.

“Do you need nineteen hundred?”

“Yes.”

Ollie selected 17:10.

COMMISSIONING PREP / TRANSITION SUPPORT  
VELA NIGHT — 17:10

Kalen’s screen chimed.

He looked down, then at Ollie.

“You wanted it.”

“So did you.”

“That easy?”

“No.”

Kalen held Ollie’s gaze for a second, then accepted the block.

“Thanks.”

Ollie shrugged because anything else would make it larger than it was.

Iri came over and read the schedule.

“You realize the performance route closes at eighteen thirty.”

“Yes.”

Iri looked offended on behalf of leisure.

“That is terrible planning.”

Ollie pointed at the morning route-delay notice still attached to Iri’s work tab.

Iri removed it.

“Unrelated.”

Kalen laughed.

Mara said, “If everyone is finished diagnosing each other, actual work remains.”

They went back to it.

Kalen took the calibration station Ollie had wanted.

Ollie took the inventory scanner.

Iri argued quietly with someone over rehearsal equipment.

Samir’s Service Four timing remained in the non-certified reference column.

The resident reports still did not fit cleanly into Ollie’s model.

He scanned the next cabinet.
