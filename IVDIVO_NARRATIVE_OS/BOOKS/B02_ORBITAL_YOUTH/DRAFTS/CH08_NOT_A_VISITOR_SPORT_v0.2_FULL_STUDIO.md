# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 8 — NOT A VISITOR SPORT

Samir missed the first pass because he moved in the direction the ball was actually going.

This was apparently wrong.

The ball crossed the middle band, drifted sideways from where his eyes wanted it to be, and passed half a meter behind his hand.

Someone on the outer deck shouted, “Planet!”

Samir caught the rail before the floor could finish becoming a wall.

The ball hit the recovery mesh.

A horn sounded.

Possession changed.

Samir stayed on the rail for one extra second.

The recreation volume curved around him in three broad bands.

The outer band felt close to normal gravity and looked almost like a gym floor if he ignored the way it continued up both sides.

The middle band narrowed and tilted inward.

The innermost lane sat closer to the rotation axis, where weight dropped enough that players moved as much by grips and controlled pushes as by running.

Bright gate frames marked the scoring route.

No one had explained any of this to him in words longer than five seconds.

He was grateful.

“Again,” Kesh called.

Kesh was the nearest thing the group had to a captain. Maybe there was an official title. Samir had not asked.

The booking clock above the entry gate read:

37:12 REMAINING

They had started with forty-two minutes.

Two minutes had disappeared while a previous group cleared equipment.

Three had gone to body-class and safety checks.

Nobody seemed willing to donate another one to Samir’s education.

Good.

A player on the inner band pushed off a grip, crossed toward him, and released the ball.

Samir watched the player this time.

Not the ball.

The player’s body shifted across the rotating frame.

The pass looked wrong before it left the hand.

Samir waited.

The ball curved into the place the player had been aiming at.

He caught it against his chest.

“Better,” Kesh said.

Samir had one legal hold count before he had to move it.

“Three.”

He looked outward.

“Two.”

A teammate was climbing toward the heavier band.

“One.”

Samir threw.

Too hard.

The teammate caught it and immediately lost footing.

The ball went free.

A synthetic-bodied player on the other team reached it first.

The body was compact and human-shaped, with dark composite surfaces at the forearms and calves and a plain fabric jersey over the torso. A small body-class marker glowed at the shoulder.

Before practice, Kesh had checked the marker, checked the grip limiter, and said, “Green.”

That had been the entire discussion.

Now the player caught the ball one-handed, used a rail to redirect without planting a foot, and sent it toward the inner gate.

Samir followed.

Badly.

He pushed inward too fast.

The floor seemed to slide sideways.

His shoulder met padded wall.

The wall won.

A horn sounded again.

Someone laughed.

Samir got his feet under him.

The synthetic-bodied player passed on the return route.

“Planet.”

Samir looked at the jersey number.

“Seven.”

“Nera.”

“What?”

“My name.”

Samir nodded. “Nera.”

Nera laughed and missed the next grip.

Kesh yelled, “Play.”

They played.

* * *

Crossband had a simple rule and a large number of ways to make the rule difficult.

Move the ball through three gates in order.

At least one possession had to cross from one gravity band to another.

No player could hold for more than three counts.

No pushing another body off a route.

No blocking a handrail.

No launch from the inner band above the marked impulse limit.

That last rule had a different enforcement setting for different body classes.

Samir learned this because Nera got warned once.

“Limiter,” Kesh called.

“It is on.”

“Then stop pretending your wrist weighs nothing.”

“It almost does.”

“Not for us.”

Nera adjusted something at the forearm and kept moving.

Nobody stopped to discuss fairness.

Samir liked that too.

His third run was worse than his second.

He over-braked entering the outer band and gave away a gate.

His fourth was better.

By the fifth, he understood that local players did not watch where somebody was.

They watched what gravity was about to make that person capable of doing.

Kesh moved inward.

A defender followed.

Samir saw the problem before Kesh reached the middle band.

If Samir threw now, the defender would close the line.

If he waited until Kesh looked open, Kesh would be too light to brake into the catch.

Samir held.

“Three.”

Kesh glanced at him.

“Two.”

The defender shifted outward half a step.

Samir threw before Kesh was ready.

Kesh’s hand snapped up.

The ball arrived exactly as Kesh crossed into the higher-weight edge of the middle band.

Kesh caught.

One turn.

One step.

Outer gate.

Horn.

Score.

For the first time, nobody said planet.

Samir moved back toward his start mark.

He wished that had lasted longer.

Nera caught his sleeve.

“Wait.”

Samir stopped.

Nera’s faceplate was not a face, exactly. It carried simple eye and mouth cues because, apparently, people still liked knowing where someone was looking.

Nera was looking at him.

“You’re Rahman.”

Samir felt the attention in the room shift toward him.

Kesh glanced over from the gate.

Another player said, “Who?”

Nera hesitated.

Samir knew the hesitation.

He had seen it on Earth.

The moment when somebody realized they had recognized a person from something they probably should not make that person explain.

Usually they explained anyway.

“The Contact cohort,” Nera said. “The profile thing.”

There it was.

Samir looked at the booking clock.

24:08.

Twenty-four minutes left.

He could leave.

He had already learned enough to come back another day.

Maybe.

A girl near the equipment rack said, “You don’t have to ask him about that.”

“I wasn’t—”

Samir said, “I can answer.”

The girl stopped.

Nera stopped.

Kesh did not stop resetting the gates.

Samir said, “Yeah. That was me. I’m not talking about the profile stuff here.”

Nera nodded once.

“Okay.”

The girl looked as if she wanted to add something kind.

Samir hoped she would not.

She did not.

Kesh pointed at him.

“Your outer brake is terrible.”

Samir looked over.

“I noticed.”

“You noticed after the wall.”

“The wall was persuasive.”

“Again.”

The game resumed.

Practice resumed.

Nera gave him less space on the next possession.

Which Samir respected.

* * *

By the end of the booking, Samir’s left shoulder hurt and he had stopped thinking of the outer band as down.

Mostly.

The final score was not close.

Kesh’s side lost by four.

Samir had contributed to two of those four.

He had also made the cleanest middle-band handoff of the last ten minutes.

Nobody mentioned that when they were packing.

They mentioned the wall.

“First session record,” Nera said.

“For what?”

“Fastest introduction to padding.”

“That wasn’t fast.”

“You did it in under six minutes.”

Samir handed over a grip strap.

Nera scanned it back into the equipment rack.

“Next slot is Tuesday.”

Kesh said, “If we keep it.”

“Why wouldn’t we?”

“Relocation staging wants two evening volumes next week.”

Nera groaned.

“Again?”

“People need somewhere to put things.”

“They have freight decks.”

“Apparently freight decks contain freight.”

Nera looked personally betrayed.

Samir checked the booking board.

Tuesday was still listed.

19:20–20:02.

Forty-two minutes.

The slot suddenly looked valuable.

He saved it.

Kesh noticed.

“You coming back?”

Samir shrugged.

Kesh waited.

Samir said, “Probably.”

“That means yes.”

“It means probably.”

Kesh slung the ball bag over one shoulder.

“Planet language.”

Samir let that one go.

They left the recreation volume together.

The corridor outside felt too still.

His body kept expecting the gravity change to become part of a play.

Instead there were families, two maintenance workers, a food cart, and a man carrying a folded chair for no obvious reason.

Nera took the first transfer toward the east ringline.

Kesh and two others stayed with Samir.

Their normal route was closed.

A maintenance notice covered the junction display.

RINGLINE 3 — PARTITION SERVICE  
LOCAL REROUTE: SERVICE TRANSFER 4

Kesh swore.

“How bad?” Samir asked.

“Depends.”

“On?”

“Whether everyone else has also discovered Service Four.”

They had.

* * *

The reroute was safe and annoying.

Too many people wanted the same narrow transition.

A family stopped to reorganize bags before the entry mark.

Someone coming the other way held the partition because an older man was still crossing.

A maintenance cart waited behind them.

The door opened.

Three people crossed.

Kesh went next.

Samir followed.

The corridor beyond was warmer.

His shoulder objected when he changed grip.

Behind him, somebody called a name.

One of Kesh’s teammates stopped halfway through the transition and turned back.

The partition remained open.

Two people on the warm side moved forward anyway.

A worker told them to hold.

They did not hear.

The local airflow changed against Samir’s face.

Small.

Dry first.

Then warm.

He looked up.

The transition status strip was still green.

Nothing happened.

Kesh was already walking.

Samir stayed where he was.

“Samir.”

“Wait.”

“For what?”

Samir watched the strip.

The ventilation pitch changed.

The status display pulsed once.

Then settled.

Samir checked the time on his band.

21:38:12.

He looked back through the partition.

The order had been:

three strangers.

Kesh.

Samir.

Teammate reverses.

two forward.

door held.

He ran it again in his head.

Crossband had spent forty-two minutes punishing him for losing track of order.

Kesh came back two steps.

“What?”

“Did you feel the air change?”

Kesh frowned.

“When?”

“Before the vent corrected.”

“Maybe.”

Samir did not press.

He moved on.

At the next junction, he messaged Ollie.

SAMIR: service transfer 4. 21:38. maybe nothing.

Ollie answered faster than Samir expected.

OLLIE: what happened?

SAMIR: airflow changed before system correction.

A pause.

OLLIE: how long?

SAMIR: didn’t time start exactly. under ten maybe.

OLLIE: occupancy?

Samir looked back at the crowd.

SAMIR: lots.

OLLIE: order near you?

Samir sent:

SAMIR: 3 across / Kesh / me / 1 reversed / 2 crossed opposite direction / partition held

The typing indicator appeared.

Stopped.

Appeared again.

OLLIE: names not needed. exact door hold time if you have it?

SAMIR: no

OLLIE: any local interface alert?

SAMIR: not mine

OLLIE: can you ask the others what they noticed?

Samir stopped walking.

Kesh nearly walked into him.

“Again?”

Samir moved aside.

He typed:

SAMIR: no

Three dots.

OLLIE: okay

Samir waited.

Nothing else came.

He put the phone away.

Kesh said, “Problem?”

“No.”

“You stopped.”

“I was messaging someone.”

“That usually causes stopping.”

Samir looked at him.

Kesh smiled.

Samir said, “You sound like Ethan.”

“I don’t know who that is.”

“Good.”

They reached the next transition.

This one curved inward.

Samir watched Kesh take the grip before the floor shifted.

He did the same.

His feet landed where he expected.

Kesh noticed and said nothing.

Samir’s band buzzed once before they separated.

CROSSBAND — TUESDAY SLOT CONFIRMED  
19:20–20:02

Under it, Nera had posted to the team channel:

PLANET RETURNS. WALL WARNED.

Samir stared at the message for a second.

Then typed:

SAMIR: wall needs training

He sent it before he could decide whether that was too much.

Three reactions appeared almost immediately.

None of them had anything to do with Contact.

Samir put the band away and headed home.
