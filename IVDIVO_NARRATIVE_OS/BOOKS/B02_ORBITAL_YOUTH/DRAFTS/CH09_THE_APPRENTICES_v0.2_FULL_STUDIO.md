# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 9 — THE APPRENTICES

Ollie lost his workshop slot before he reached the workshop.

The notice appeared while he was still on the ringline.

PERSONAL FABRICATION BLOCK — 06:40  
STATUS: REALLOCATED  
PRIORITY ACCESS: COMMISSIONING SUPPORT

He stared at it.

Then opened the detail.

The slot had not been taken by Ring Six.

It had been taken by him.

More precisely, by his placement.

His access profile had been moved to a commissioning-support block later in the day, and the system had automatically released the personal slot to another apprentice.

Ollie checked the replacement.

13:20 — SHARED FABRICATION / COMMISSIONING SUPPORT  
ACCESS LEVEL: YOUTH PLACEMENT — LIMITED

That was better access.

Worse time.

And not his.

He looked at the name attached to the released 06:40 block.

IRI.

Of course.

The ringline doors opened.

Ollie got out.

Iri was not at the maintenance bay.

That was unusual enough that Mara looked at the wall clock twice.

“Where is Iri?”

Nobody answered.

The local apprentices at the first station were already working.

One of them, Kalen, glanced toward the service entrance.

“Missed the transfer.”

Mara said, “Iri does not miss transfers.”

Kalen shrugged.

“Today has apparently expanded the possible.”

Ollie clipped in.

“What happened?”

Kalen looked at him.

“Good morning to you too.”

“Sorry. Morning. What happened?”

“No idea.”

“That sounded like you knew.”

“I know Iri is late.”

“That is not what—”

Kalen pointed at Ollie’s access tab.

“You got fabrication support?”

Ollie looked down.

“Yes.”

“Today?”

“Yes.”

Kalen’s mouth tightened.

“Nice.”

It did not sound nice.

Ollie knew that tone.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You said nice.”

“Yes.”

“You meant something else.”

Kalen turned back to the calibration rack.

“Earth training.”

“What?”

“Very observant.”

Ollie looked at the other apprentices.

Nobody volunteered to help.

He had been in the bay long enough to know when a silence belonged to work and when it belonged to him.

This one belonged to him.

Mara handed him a physical checklist.

“Environmental transition run archive. Compare yesterday’s movement sequence against service-state logs.”

“That’s what I was doing.”

“No. Yesterday you were making notes. Today you have a defined comparison.”

Ollie opened the list.

Samir’s Service Four observation had been added to the bottom as an external timing note.

No names.

No team data.

Just:

SERVICE TRANSFER 4  
21:38  
SEQUENCE IRREGULARITY / DIRECTION REVERSAL  
LOCAL AIRFLOW CHANGE REPORTED BEFORE SYSTEM CORRECTION  
TIMING ESTIMATE: <10 SEC

Ollie looked up.

“Mara.”

“Yes.”

“This is Samir’s.”

“I know.”

“He sent it to me.”

“And you logged it.”

“I didn’t ask to add it to commissioning evidence.”

“It is not commissioning evidence.”

He looked again.

REFERENCE OBSERVATION — NON-CERTIFIED

Mara said, “You wanted another timing source. You have one observation.”

“One.”

“Yes.”

“That doesn’t prove anything.”

“Excellent.”

Kalen laughed once.

Mara looked over.

“Something useful?”

“No.”

“Then continue not helping.”

Ollie took the checklist to the side station.

He could feel Kalen still being annoyed behind him.

He ignored it.

For thirty seconds.

Then stopped ignoring it.

“What did I take?”

Kalen did not turn.

“What?”

“My placement access. What did it displace?”

Kalen kept working.

“Ask the system.”

“I’m asking you.”

“That’s new.”

“What is?”

“Asking before telling us how access works.”

Ollie put the checklist down.

“I haven’t told you how access works.”

Kalen looked at him.

“You told Iri the other day that workshop scheduling was fixable.”

Ollie remembered.

“That was a general statement.”

“That made it better.”

“No.”

“Correct.”

Kalen scanned a tool.

“Your group gets priority support blocks because Ring Six commissioning is on a youth-residency showcase schedule.”

“I didn’t make that rule.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you mad at me?”

Kalen considered.

“Because you keep benefiting from things you didn’t make and then acting surprised when somebody else notices.”

That landed.

Ollie opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Kalen nodded once.

“Better.”

Mara called from across the bay.

“Kalen.”

“Working.”

“Ollie.”

“Working.”

“You are both doing a poor imitation.”

Ollie picked up the checklist.

He did not ask whether the showcase schedule was true.

Not yet.

* * *

Iri arrived twenty-three minutes late.

Not running.

That somehow made it worse.

The service door opened, Iri came through, clipped in, and went straight to Mara.

“Missed handoff.”

Mara looked at the time.

“I noticed.”

“My cousin’s host transfer changed.”

“Why?”

“Parent shift moved. The replacement host wasn’t at the expected ringline.”

“And?”

Iri looked annoyed.

“I assumed Service Two was still faster.”

Kalen called from across the bay, “It was not.”

Iri looked at him.

“No.”

Ollie said, “You took a bad shortcut?”

“I took a good shortcut yesterday.”

“That is different.”

“Today it became educational.”

“You could have messaged.”

“I did.”

Mara checked her display.

A notification appeared.

Then another.

She looked at Iri.

“It arrived eleven minutes ago.”

“I sent it twenty-six minutes ago.”

“Route network queue?”

“Maybe.”

Mara’s expression changed.

Not concern.

Filing.

“Show me later.”

Iri nodded.

Mara said, “You lose twenty-three minutes from today’s credited block.”

“I know.”

“You can recover them Thursday.”

“I know.”

“Stop saying you know.”

Iri looked toward Ollie.

“He does it more.”

“That is not a defense.”

Iri clipped on a work tab.

Ollie waited until Mara moved away.

“Your cousin?”

Iri looked at him.

“Yes.”

“You do host handoffs?”

“Sometimes.”

“How old?”

“No.”

“That’s not private.”

“It is now.”

Ollie stopped.

Iri looked at the fabrication schedule.

“My block came back.”

“You got mine.”

“I had it first.”

“I know.”

Iri smiled.

“Good.”

Ollie looked at the schedule again.

“You need it for the percussion rig?”

Iri stopped smiling.

Ollie realized he had guessed correctly.

“How do you know about that?”

“Kalen said—”

“Kalen did not.”

“No. He said nothing.”

“Then?”

“You had a rehearsal booking last week. Youth performance volume. Same day as the workshop block.”

Iri stared at him.

Ollie said, “It was on the public schedule.”

“That is worse.”

“Why?”

“You looked.”

“It is a schedule.”

“You looked because you wanted an answer to a question I didn’t answer.”

Ollie felt heat rise into his face.

“That’s not—”

It was.

He stopped.

Iri waited.

Ollie said, “Sorry.”

Iri looked at him for a second.

Then clipped the regulator band tighter at the wrist.

“That works.”

Ollie was starting to hate approval delivered in two words.

“What are you making?”

Iri looked at him.

Ollie lifted both hands.

“Never mind.”

“Also better.”

They worked.

* * *

The archive comparison did not make the problem cleaner.

That was beginning to feel personal.

Ollie aligned four traces:

Ring Six first occupancy.

Ring Six controlled run three.

Ring Six controlled run four.

Samir’s Service Four observation.

The building logs gave him door states, flow correction, local demand, averaged return.

The people gave him phrases.

Dry.

Warm.

Pressure.

Wrong.

Too fast.

Not always the same words.

Not always the same order.

Kalen passed behind him carrying a tool case.

“You’re doing the face again.”

“What face?”

“The one where reality has failed to organize itself.”

“That is not my face.”

“It is.”

Ollie ignored him.

Iri joined from the other side.

“What are you comparing?”

“Timing.”

“Of what?”

“Reported onset against fixed response.”

Iri leaned closer but did not touch the screen.

Ollie had built a side table.

REPORT LANGUAGE  
ESTIMATED ONSET  
LOCAL INTERFACE RESPONSE  
FIXED RETURN SHIFT  
DOOR STATE  
MOVEMENT ORDER

Iri read it.

“You put language outside the model.”

“It is in the table.”

“Outside the calculation.”

“Because ‘felt dry’ is not a calibrated value.”

“No.”

“So I can’t weight it like humidity.”

“I didn’t say you should.”

“Then what?”

Iri pointed at the column.

“You decided the thing people say belongs after the real data.”

“It’s not after. It’s beside.”

“Your model does not use it.”

“Not yet.”

“Why?”

“Because I don’t know what it means.”

Iri’s regulator pulsed amber.

The bay had warmed slightly as a service cart opened the adjacent partition.

Iri stepped half a pace toward the cooler side without looking.

Ollie noticed.

“What did that feel like?”

Iri looked at him.

“Work.”

“No, the transition.”

“Warm.”

“How warm?”

“Annoying.”

“That isn’t a unit.”

“It is to me.”

Ollie exhaled.

“I’m trying to make this usable.”

“For whom?”

“For the model.”

Iri looked at him.

Then at the screen.

“That is not the same answer.”

Ollie felt the argument becoming larger than the data and pulled it back.

“Okay. If three people use different words for the same environmental change, I need a way to know they mean the same thing.”

“Then test that.”

“How?”

“Ask them when.”

“I am asking when.”

“No. You are asking the building when, then deciding the person was early or late.”

“That’s because the building has a clock.”

“So do people.”

“Not eleven-second clocks.”

Iri reached for the regulator log.

Stopped before touching Ollie’s screen.

“Yesterday my interface responded at 09:52:14.2.”

“Yes.”

“I felt the transition before the regulator.”

“How much before?”

“I don’t know.”

“That is the problem.”

“No. That is a different problem.”

Ollie looked at the trace.

Iri continued.

“If four people say ‘wrong’ before your fixed return changes, maybe the useful question is not whether they agree on the word.”

Ollie said, “Maybe.”

Iri said nothing.

Ollie hated maybe.

He typed a note:

HUMAN REPORTS — LOW CONFIDENCE / TIMING ONLY

Iri saw it.

“That.”

“What?”

“You did it again.”

“What?”

“You made ‘hard to measure’ mean ‘less real.’”

“I made it mean lower confidence.”

“Same thing in your model.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“No, because confidence is about—”

“Repeatability.”

“Yes.”

“Then repeat it.”

Ollie stopped.

Kalen passed again.

“Is this the part where one of you wins?”

“No,” both said.

Kalen nodded.

“Healthy.”

He kept walking.

Ollie looked back at the table.

The Samir observation had no body interface.

No humidity number.

No certified fixed onset.

Just sequence, timing estimate, airflow change.

It also looked uncomfortably similar.

Not enough to use as proof.

Enough to keep.

He changed the label.

HUMAN REPORTS — UNCALIBRATED / TIMING VALUE POSSIBLE

Iri read it.

“That is uglier.”

“It is more accurate.”

“Good.”

Ollie looked at the word.

Uncalibrated.

It felt safer.

That was probably a warning.

He left it anyway.

* * *

At break, the apprentices ate in the older service corner Iri liked.

It had bad seating.

Iri had been right.

The food was better.

Not dramatically.

Enough to make the famous view rooms seem like an inefficient use of surface area.

Ollie sat with Iri, Kalen, and two apprentices he only knew by sight.

Nobody explained who sat where.

Ollie took the empty seat.

Kalen looked at his tray.

“You buy the cheapest thing every time.”

“It is food.”

“That was not a defense.”

“I like it.”

“You have not tried anything else.”

“That is how liking works.”

One of the other apprentices said, “No, that is how fear works.”

Ollie looked at Iri.

“Do all local apprentices attack lunch choices?”

“Only visitors.”

Kalen said, “He has a tab now.”

“Temporary.”

“Still.”

The word landed differently than before.

Not accusation.

Status.

Ollie looked at the group.

“What does the tab mean to you?”

Kalen groaned.

“No.”

“What?”

“Do not turn lunch into an interview.”

“I asked one question.”

“You always ask one question.”

Iri said, “Then six.”

“Fine.”

Ollie ate.

Thirty seconds passed.

One of the apprentices said, “It means if you screw up, someone else has to explain why you had access.”

Kalen said, “It means you’re trusted enough to be dangerous.”

Iri said, “It means nothing if the work is bad.”

Ollie looked at them.

Nobody added a fourth answer.

Good.

A message flashed on Iri’s band.

Iri checked it.

Then swore.

“Rehearsal?” Kalen asked.

“Moved.”

“Again?”

“Performer volume lost one lane.”

“To what?”

“Relocation staging.”

Kalen looked at Ollie.

“Your ring.”

“It is not my ring.”

“Tell Nadia.”

“She knows.”

Iri put the band down harder than necessary.

Ollie said, “When is the performance?”

“Vela Night.”

“You’re playing?”

“No. We’re moving equipment in silence for forty minutes.”

Kalen laughed.

Iri looked at Ollie.

“Yes.”

“What do you play?”

Iri looked at the table.

Ollie waited.

Then stopped waiting.

Iri said, “Low-g frame percussion.”

Ollie had no idea what that meant.

He did not ask.

Iri looked at him.

“That was painful, wasn’t it?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

The break alarm sounded.

Kalen stood.

“Commissioning visitor gets the diagnostic station.”

Ollie stood too.

“I can take the inventory side.”

Kalen looked at him.

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

“Why?”

Because Kalen had made it clear the access hierarchy was real.

Because Ollie did not want to be the person who took the best work and then pretended everyone had chosen equally.

Because he could do inventory.

He said only:

“You’re faster on the calibration chain.”

Kalen studied him.

Then handed over the inventory scanner.

“Correct.”

Ollie took it.

The others left.

Iri stayed.

“You wanted that station.”

“Yes.”

“You gave it up.”

“I still have the afternoon block.”

“That is not the same.”

“No.”

Iri looked at him.

“Better.”

Ollie pointed the scanner at Iri.

“Say that again and I’m logging you as equipment.”

Iri laughed.

Not the respiratory sound.

Actually laughed.

Then left for the calibration station.

Ollie opened the first cabinet.

The work was boring.

He did it anyway.

* * *

At 15:06, Mara posted the next apprentice access schedule.

Everyone looked.

Nobody pretended not to.

Two diagnostic-support blocks were open for the next commissioning cycle.

One at 17:10 on Vela Night.

One at 19:00.

The later block was better.

It included live transition calibration.

The earlier one was mostly prep, verification, and tool custody before the public event increased transit load.

Ollie tapped 19:00.

The system showed:

PRIORITY ELIGIBLE — YOUTH COHORT PLACEMENT

Underneath:

LOCAL APPRENTICE CREDIT REQUEST — KALEN

Ollie stopped.

Kalen was looking at a different screen.

Not at him.

That made it worse.

Ollie opened the 17:10 block.

No competing credit request.

It ended after the first Vela Night performance window had already begun.

He had promised Maya he would at least try to show up before she decided all engineers were socially defective.

He looked at the 19:00 block again.

Better work.

Worse choice.

Mara came up beside him.

“You can take either.”

“Kalen needs nineteen hundred.”

“Kalen requested it.”

“Does my priority override?”

“Yes.”

Kalen said from the other station, “You can just say my name while discussing my future.”

Ollie looked over.

“Do you need it?”

“Yes.”

“Then take it.”

Kalen frowned.

“That easy?”

“No.”

“What’s the hard part?”

“I want it.”

Kalen waited.

Ollie selected 17:10.

The system confirmed.

COMMISSIONING PREP / TRANSITION SUPPORT  
VELA NIGHT — 17:10

Maya’s name immediately appeared in his head.

So did the better diagnostic block.

He disliked both.

Kalen’s screen chimed.

Kalen looked down.

Then at Ollie.

“Thanks.”

Ollie shrugged.

“Don’t make it weird.”

“You made it weird by explaining how much you wanted it.”

“I answered your question.”

“That was your mistake.”

Iri came over and read the schedule.

“You took event prep.”

“Yes.”

“You know the performance route closes at eighteen thirty.”

“I know.”

“You will miss the opening.”

“I know.”

Iri looked almost offended on behalf of leisure.

“That is terrible planning.”

Ollie pointed at Iri.

“You took a shortcut that added twenty-three minutes this morning.”

“That was transport.”

“This is scheduling.”

“Different failure class.”

Kalen laughed.

Mara said, “If everyone is finished diagnosing each other, actual work remains.”

They went back to it.

Ollie took the inventory scanner.

Kalen took the calibration station.

Iri started arguing quietly with someone over rehearsal equipment.

Samir’s Service Four timing still sat in the non-certified reference column.

The resident reports still did not fit cleanly into Ollie’s model.

Nothing had been solved.

But one thing was clearer than it had been that morning.

Access had a cost even when nobody intended one.

Ollie scanned the next cabinet.
