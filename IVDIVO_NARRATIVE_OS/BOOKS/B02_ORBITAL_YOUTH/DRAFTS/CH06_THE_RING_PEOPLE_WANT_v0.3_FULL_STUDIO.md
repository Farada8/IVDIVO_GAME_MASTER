# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 6 — THE RING PEOPLE WANT

Ring Six smelled new.

Not clean.

New.

The difference was obvious as soon as Ollie stepped through the service threshold.

Clean was filtration and disinfectant and a maintenance team doing its job. New was polymer sealant that had not finished losing its factory smell, wall fabric nobody had leaned against yet, floor grip with every ridge still sharp, and a public corridor with no scratches where people cut corners badly.

The lights were warmer than the service bay.

The walls were not.

Ring Six had not decided what kind of place it was yet.

Ollie followed Iri through a residential junction and tried not to look like someone seeing it for the first time.

Iri noticed.

“You can look.”

“I am looking.”

“You’re trying not to.”

“That is still looking.”

“Visitor.”

Ollie ignored that.

The junction opened into a small civic volume built around a shallow vertical garden. Real plants, not projected ones. A maintenance rail ran up behind the foliage where most people would never notice it unless someone left the access panel open.

Someone had.

Ollie noticed.

Beyond the garden were three food counters, only one operating. A youth room with movable walls. A laundry bank. A row of parcel lockers. A narrow staircase leading toward a view gallery that currently had a temporary barrier across the entrance.

Two children were sitting on the barrier anyway.

One of them had a shoe off.

Nothing about the place felt like a station brochure.

That made Ollie like it immediately.

“Why is the view gallery closed?” he asked.

“Booking calibration.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“It does if two hundred people want the same window.”

“You book windows?”

“You book rooms with windows.”

“That’s worse.”

Iri glanced at him.

“Your planet has restaurants with reservations.”

“That is not the same.”

“Because food is more important than stars?”

“Yes.”

Iri considered.

“Fair.”

They crossed toward the laundry bank.

Half the machines were dark.

The other half had temporary test tags clipped to the handles.

Ollie read one automatically.

WATER RECOVERY LOOP — RESIDENTIAL LOAD TEST ONLY

“You’re not allowed to inspect that,” Iri said.

“I’m reading.”

“You read with your whole face.”

“I don’t know what that means.”

“It means stop.”

Ollie stopped.

Not because Iri had authority.

Because Mara had said the same thing fifteen minutes earlier.

Today was not a maintenance shift.

Today was occupancy settling verification.

Which meant the system did not need him to find something.

It needed him to watch people use things.

That seemed less precise.

He disliked that before they had even started.

At the far side of the civic volume, a woman in a gray housing vest was arguing with a delivery crew.

Not loudly.

Efficiently.

“That cart goes to temporary storage until Sector Six-C is released.”

“It’s already assigned.”

“It is assigned to a household that is not moving today.”

“They booked the freight slot.”

“And the room failed final access verification yesterday. Put it in temporary storage.”

“We don’t have capacity.”

“You have capacity because I released two staging cages at oh-six-hundred.”

The crew lead checked his display.

Then looked at her.

“You released one.”

“I released two.”

“One is still showing occupied.”

“By what?”

He turned the display.

The woman leaned in.

“Who put school furniture in freight staging?”

A voice behind Ollie said, “Probably a school.”

The woman looked up.

A girl about Ollie’s age was standing beside a stack of collapsible storage boxes, arms folded.

“Lea.”

“Nadia.”

“Do you know anything about this?”

“No.”

“You answered very quickly.”

“I have practice.”

“You don’t.”

“I could.”

Nadia Park looked at the storage boxes.

“Those yours?”

“Some.”

“Which some?”

Lea pointed.

“Those three. That one. Not that one. That one is Tomas.”

A man sitting on one of the boxes lifted a hand without getting up.

“Mine.”

Nadia looked at him.

“You are not supposed to sit on freight.”

“I’m not freight.”

“The box is.”

“It knows me.”

Nadia closed her eyes for half a second.

Ollie liked her too.

Mara arrived from the service access with a tablet tucked under one arm.

“Nadia.”

“Mara.”

“We are starting in six minutes.”

“We are starting when this crew stops trying to put school furniture in family storage.”

“That is not my system.”

“It becomes your system when nobody can reach the test corridor.”

Mara looked past her.

A long low cart blocked half the junction.

She looked at the crew.

“Move it.”

The crew moved it.

Nadia pointed at Mara.

“That is why people think you’re in charge of everything.”

“I am not.”

“Correct.”

Mara looked at Ollie and Iri.

“Why are you standing there?”

“Observing residents,” Iri said.

“Poorly,” Ollie added.

Nadia looked at him.

“You’re the Earth maintenance placement.”

Ollie hesitated.

“That sounds worse when you say it.”

“It usually does.”

She held out a hand.

“Nadia Park. Relocation.”

“Ollie.”

“I know.”

From beside the boxes, Lea said, “You’re with Maya?”

Ollie looked over. “Yeah.”

“She’s in the youth transit channel.”

“What did she do?”

“Spent ten minutes telling people the fastest route wasn’t the fastest route.”

Ollie nodded. “Yeah.”

Lea went back to her messages.

Nadia pointed toward Lea.

“Lea. Future resident, if the universe stops adding forms.”

Lea lifted two fingers.

“Tomas. Also future resident, if his employer doesn’t fire him before we get him closer than forty-eight minutes from his shift.”

Tomas remained seated on the box.

“Thirty-nine if the ringline behaves.”

“It doesn’t.”

“That is true.”

Ollie looked at the boxes again.

“You’re moving today?”

Lea laughed.

“No.”

Nadia said, “They are not.”

Lea said, “We have been not moving today for eleven days.”

“Eight.”

“Eleven emotionally.”

Nadia ignored that.

“Today is a realistic occupancy run. Bags, route changes, laundry load, meal service, ordinary door use. If it behaves, tomorrow gets larger.”

“And if it doesn’t?” Ollie asked.

Nadia looked toward the residential corridor.

“Then people keep living where they are until the old-sector overhaul takes another service corridor away from us.”

“How long?”

“Twenty-three days before the next closure.”

Lea, from beside the boxes, said, “Twenty-two if you count the day we lose moving things twice.”

Nadia looked at her.

“I don’t.”

“You should.”

Ollie looked back at the unopened residential corridor.

* * *

Lea’s assigned room was small.

Not unpleasant.

Small.

The sleeping surface folded out over storage. The desk was built into a wall recess. One panel could become either wardrobe depth or extra floor space but not both at the same time.

Lea demonstrated this as if she had personally designed the flaw.

“Desk open, closet annoying. Closet open, desk annoying. Bed open, everything annoying.”

“You picked this room?” Ollie asked.

“I picked this sector.”

“Why?”

She pointed through the doorway.

The corridor curved away toward a radial lift two junctions down.

“School is one transfer. My sister’s training block is two. My mother’s shift is one ringline plus service loop. And I can get to the west youth bay without crossing the spine.”

“View?”

“Wall.”

“You don’t care?”

“I care. I just care less than sleeping.”

Nadia, standing in the hall, said, “Please write that on every housing advertisement.”

Lea opened a storage panel.

“Also this is bigger than temporary.”

“How many people are in temporary with you?” Ollie asked.

“Four.”

“In one room?”

“In two rooms pretending to be three.”

Tomas leaned against the opposite wall.

“I have one room pretending to be a hallway.”

Lea pointed at him.

“He chose commute over privacy.”

“I chose employment over privacy.”

“That sounds less fun.”

“It is.”

Ollie looked at Tomas.

“You commute forty-eight minutes?”

“Today.”

“From where?”

“Old Ring Two.”

“That’s not forty-eight minutes.”

Tomas smiled.

“On the map?”

Ollie stopped.

Tomas continued.

“Try it at shift change with one service loop closed.”

Nadia said, “Or with a child who has to be dropped in a different gravity band first.”

Tomas nodded.

“That too.”

“You have a child?” Ollie asked.

“My sister has a child. I have mornings.”

That was all he said about it.

Mara called from the corridor.

“Occupancy group one.”

Everyone moved.

Ollie followed.

The test was not what he had expected.

He had expected people with badges walking prescribed routes.

There were badges.

There were prescribed routes.

The people immediately made them less prescribed.

Lea forgot one of her storage boxes and went back.

Tomas stopped at a food counter that was supposed to be closed because someone had decided the test should include actual service demand.

A woman with two children entered the laundry volume early.

One child discovered the vertical garden and refused to leave.

A delivery cart arrived from the wrong side because the intended service loop was occupied by a cleaning team.

Nadia did not stop any of it.

Mara did not either.

Ollie looked between them.

“This is allowed?”

Nadia said, “This is living here.”

Mara said, “Within limits.”

Those were not the same answer.

Both mattered.

The environmental panel on Ollie’s wrist showed the ring settling inside expected bands.

Door cycles.

Local temperature.

Humidity.

Airflow demand.

Nothing interesting.

That was good.

He knew it was good.

He still wanted something to happen.

Iri stood beside one of the environment transitions, watching the people instead of the display.

Ollie noticed.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re doing the thing.”

“What thing?”

“Looking like something is wrong.”

“I’m watching.”

“At what?”

Iri pointed with two fingers.

Not at a panel.

At Lea.

She had stopped just past the transition.

Not stopped completely.

Slowed.

One hand touched the side of her neck.

Then she kept walking.

Ollie looked at the display.

Temperature normal.

Humidity normal.

Pressure differential normal.

Flow correction normal.

“Lea,” Nadia called. “Problem?”

Lea turned.

“No.”

“You stopped.”

“Felt dry for a second.”

Nadia looked at Mara.

Mara looked at the environmental panel.

Iri’s wrist regulator pulsed amber.

Ollie saw it.

Iri adjusted nothing.

“What did yours do?” Ollie asked.

“Local correction.”

“For dry?”

“For fast change.”

The fixed display remained green.

Mara said, “Log subjective report.”

Ollie did.

RESIDENT REPORT: BRIEF DRY / THROAT SENSATION
FIXED ENVIRONMENTAL DATA: WITHIN BAND

He looked at Iri’s regulator.

“Can that export timing?”

Iri looked at him.

“Not to you.”

“I mean generally.”

“Yes.”

“Could we compare—”

“Not yours.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

Ollie stopped.

The group continued.

No alarms.

No second complaint.

By the time they reached the laundry bank, Ollie had nearly dismissed the whole thing.

Then the fixed humidity return shifted.

Not much.

A small correction.

The system compensated.

Green remained green.

Ollie opened the event trace.

The change had appeared after Lea’s report.

Not before.

“How far?” he asked.

Mara was already looking.

“Within expected settling.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I know.”

She sent him the timing.

Eleven seconds.

Ollie looked at Iri.

Iri did not say anything.

The regulator on Iri’s wrist had returned to green.

Eleven seconds fit inside too many harmless explanations to deserve a name.

A local personal regulator responding before an averaged fixed sensor was not surprising. That was what local systems were for.

The occupancy group reached the youth room.

The movable wall opened.

Someone on the other side had left three chairs in the wrong place.

Two people laughed.

A child claimed one.

Lea sat on another and pulled up the housing layout on her tablet.

“This wall moves?”

Nadia said, “During approved configuration windows.”

“So not when I want.”

“No.”

“That is oppression.”

“File it.”

“I will.”

Tomas dropped into the third chair.

“If this opens by next month, I get thirty minutes of my life back every day.”

“Forty,” Nadia said.

“Don’t oversell it.”

“I have your commute model.”

“You have a fantasy.”

Lea looked at Ollie.

“Does this count as a successful test?”

Ollie glanced at Mara.

Mara did not answer for him.

Everything was green.

One subjective report.

One local regulator pulse.

One delayed fixed correction.

No threshold breach.

No fault.

He said, “So far.”

Lea groaned.

“That is such an engineer answer.”

“I’m not an engineer.”

“That is an even worse answer.”

Nadia looked at the time.

“Group two in four minutes.”

Lea stood.

“Do we get to stay?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because you’re not residents yet.”

“I have boxes.”

“You have boxes everywhere.”

“That is basically residency.”

Nadia pointed toward the exit.

Lea left.

Tomas followed more slowly.

At the doorway, he looked back at the room.

Not the wall.

Not the systems.

The chair.

As if he had already decided where it belonged.

Ollie watched him go.

Then he looked at the environmental trace again.

Eleven seconds.

Mara closed the panel.

“Don’t fall in love with a number.”

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

“I want to know whether it repeats.”

“That is allowed.”

“Next group?”

“Yes.”

Ollie moved toward the transition.

Iri stayed where they were.

“What?”

Iri looked toward the corridor Lea had used.

“Same route?”

“Mostly.”

“Mostly isn’t same.”

“That’s the point.”

Iri’s expression changed slightly.

Not agreement.

Interest.

The second occupancy group entered Ring Six.

This one had more people.

Someone was carrying groceries.

Someone else had a folded laundry bag.

A boy was arguing with his father about whether a wall panel counted as a desk if nobody wanted to use it as one.

Nadia was already redirecting a delivery cart.

Mara was already telling somebody not to block the service seam.

The ring looked less new.

Only slightly.

Ollie took his position beside the transition.

He watched the people first.

Then the display.
