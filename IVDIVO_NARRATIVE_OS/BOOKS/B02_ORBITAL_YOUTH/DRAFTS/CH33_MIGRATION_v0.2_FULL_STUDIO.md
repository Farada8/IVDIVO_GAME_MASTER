# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 33 — MIGRATION

At 06:31, Ring Six smelled less like a new habitat than a delivery problem: packing foam, hot bread from a temporary food station, cleaning solvent, and somebody's damp laundry because a bag had split beside the transfer gate. Three shirts hung from a rail marked EMERGENCY ACCESS — KEEP CLEAR.

A child sat directly under the sign to retie a boot.

Ollie stepped around him and checked his band for the fourth time.

His old credential was still there.

`REVIEW HOLD — UNRESTRICTED COMMISSIONING ACCESS NOT RESTORED.`

Under it, added at 05:58:

`TEMPORARY ASSIGNMENT — MIGRATION WATCH / SEGMENT THREE.`

`READ ONLY.`

`INCIDENT MARK.`

`STOP REQUEST TO SUPERVISING OPERATOR.`

`NO CONFIGURATION AUTHORITY.`

He liked the temporary assignment more than he wanted to admit because it was narrow enough to understand exactly where his responsibility stopped.

Mara met him at the support desk outside the Segment Three resident entry. She had a cup in one hand and a move-day board in the other.

“Read, mark, request,” she said.

“I read the assignment.”

“Say it back.”

Ollie looked at her.

“Read state. Mark incident. Request stop if the approved local profile is left. Do not touch configuration.”

“Ring-wide routing?”

“No.”

“Housing allocation?”

“No.”

“Controller writes?”

“No.”

“Independent isolation?”

“No.”

Mara nodded and handed him the local board.

“Good. Now you may enjoy being useful.”

“That sounded almost kind.”

“It is six thirty in the morning. Do not make me repeat it.”

She moved on before he could answer.

The support desk was not inside the commissioning control room. It sat in a public-service bay where residents could see the same large route display as the move crews. Ollie had a deeper read-only layer on his band: Segment Three recovery state, service-transition order, local density estimate, environmental limits and approved restriction flags. He could change none of it; he could watch all of it.

Across the junction, Maya was carrying three temporary route markers and a cup of coffee she clearly regretted.

“You look dead,” Ollie said.

“Authority did this to me.”

“You have no authority.”

“Exactly. All the waking up, none of the power.”

A relocation worker called her name and pointed toward the South Four approach.

Maya lifted one route marker in salute and went.

Ollie was grateful that the conversation ended there.

At 06:42, the first household crossed into Segment Three.

Nobody applauded. A housing worker scanned four room credentials, a service worker checked the environmental profile, two adults pushed a cart, a teenager carried a school bag and a pillow, and a younger child carried nothing while complaining that everybody else had packed the wrong breakfast.

The door opened. They went through.

On Ollie's display, occupancy changed from zero to four. Pressure correction closed on schedule; the shared return network settled; the approved service delay cleared; resident route stayed green.

A second household arrived early and blocked the lane while trying to work out why one bedding crate had gone to Segment One.

“Do we leave the cart here?” the man pushing it asked nobody in particular.

Three people answered.

“No.”

“Yes, left bay.”

“Not that left bay.”

A housing runner came over, looked at the tag and said, “Storage side. You are residential side.”

The man stared at the two identical-looking corridors.

“They have the same number.”

“One has an S.”

“The S is six millimeters high.”

“That complaint has already been filed.”

“Good.”

They moved the cart. Ollie's density estimate rose for forty-three seconds and fell again without leaving the approved profile.

At 07:05, a service cart missed its assigned crossing by two minutes because somebody had put a folded room panel in front of the service lift. The cart operator arrived angry. The relocation volunteer responsible for the panel was angrier.

The service desk shifted the cart to the next approved window. That pushed a cleaning-unit transition later; the cleaning unit waited; a family waited behind it. A teenager asked why a machine got a better queue than a person.

“Because the machine weighs four hundred kilos and cannot climb over you,” the operator said.

The teenager considered this.

“Fair.”

Ollie watched the sequence recompute. The delayed service transition moved closer to a recovery window his model cared about, but the supervising operator had already inserted the required recovery hold. He kept his hand off the incident marker.

A minute later the cart crossed, the cleaning unit followed, and the family went through.

By 07:30, thirty-one people had entered Segment Three.

By 07:41, one had come back out because they had forgotten medication in temporary housing. A boy returned to the gate because his friend was still outside and he had promised to show him the room first.

Housing sent him back in.

“Your friend is moving in this afternoon.”

“He said morning.”

“His household window changed.”

“He did not message me.”

“Then your problem is social, not logistical.”

The boy looked offended by the classification. Ollie almost corrected it and decided the housing worker had enough problems.

At 08:12, the first food delivery entered the segment. At 08:19, somebody complained that the temporary food station had better bread than the permanent one. Five minutes later a resident filed a maintenance ticket because a bedroom light switched on half a second after the wall control.

Ollie opened the ticket automatically.

Ordinary local interface delay. Wrong subsystem. No relation to the Ring Six control issue.

He closed it, reopened it long enough to confirm somebody else owned it, then closed it again.

Iri appeared at the support desk at 08:37 in work clothes with a tool case.

“Why are you smiling at a maintenance ticket?” Iri asked.

“I am not.”

“You are.”

“The light is slow.”

“That sounds bad.”

“It is completely unrelated.”

Iri looked at the ticket.

“Then why are you happy?”

Ollie looked through the resident entry, where someone was arguing about a light in a room they had not been allowed to live in two weeks earlier.

“Because they are complaining about the room.”

Iri waited.

“Not because I want them unhappy,” Ollie added.

“I understood.”

“That is unusual.”

“I work with you.”

Iri picked up the tool case and left to deal with the light.

For the next hour, nothing important happened. Ollie watched people miss turns, carry too much, ask where laundry went and discover that the new rooms had less useful storage than the plans had suggested. It was one of the best hours he had spent on Vela.

* * *

At 10:16, Housing called the support desk about Room 3-214.

The household had been told the room was theirs that night: two adults, two children and one compact plant carrier containing something with pale leaves that kept pressing against the transparent side as if it objected to orbital bureaucracy.

Their original move window was 10:30. At 10:09, a delayed maintenance crossing had pushed one service cart into the same resident route period. The cart could wait. The problem was what came after it: a room-support unit scheduled for 3-214 needed the service path before the household could complete handoff.

Housing could move the people first and the support unit later.

The household did not want that.

“What does later mean?” the mother asked.

The housing worker said, “Current estimate, ninety minutes.”

“We were told the room was ready.”

“It is physically ready.”

“Then why is something still moving into it?”

“Environmental storage insert.”

“For the plant?” one child asked.

“For the room.”

The child looked at the plant.

“Plant thinks it is for the plant.”

The mother ignored that.

“We have a school route test at sixteen hundred. If we move at twelve, we still have to unpack enough to leave again.”

The housing worker nodded.

Ollie checked his board. The support unit could use Service Path B instead. Path B was longer but currently clear; the reason the system had not offered it was the extended recovery hold Segment Three used during resident peak.

If the unit waited through the hold, its estimated arrival was thirty-eight minutes later than the direct path and still before the household's route test.

Ollie placed one factual note on the housing request.

`PATH B AVAILABLE AFTER CURRENT RECOVERY HOLD — ETA +38 MIN IF OPERATIONS ACCEPTS.`

The supervising operator saw it.

“Why B?” she asked.

“Direct service path is competing with resident peak. B clears after the current recovery hold.”

“Any candidate-state overlap?”

“Not after the hold, if it leaves on the first allowed release.”

She checked independently, then called transit. Transit called housing. Housing asked the household whether they wanted to wait where they were or move to the room before the insert arrived.

The mother said, “We wait together.”

The support unit took Path B at 10:47.

At 11:31, Ollie was carrying one end of a food crate because the housing runner had discovered he possessed arms.

“You are the model person?” one of the children from 3-214 asked.

Ollie adjusted the crate.

“What model?”

“The ring model.”

“Part of it.”

“My mother said somebody made the ring late.”

“That was not me.”

“She also said somebody stopped it opening wrong.”

“That was many people.”

The child accepted this and walked ahead.

At the room door, the housing runner scanned the handoff. The family entered, with the plant carrier first because the younger child insisted.

The room was not impressive. Ollie had seen it in plans for weeks: two sleeping modules, one folding work surface, storage that looked generous until four people's actual possessions arrived, a narrow environmental cabinet and a window band showing a slice of the inner habitat structure.

The older child dropped a school bag beside the wrong door.

Their mother moved it with her foot.

“Not there. We need that route clear.”

“It is my bag.”

“It is everybody's door.”

The younger child put the plant on the folding work surface.

Their father said, “That is where we eat.”

“Plant eats light.”

“We eat food.”

“Plant was here first.”

“It arrived twelve seconds before us.”

“Still first.”

The housing runner laughed.

Ollie set the crate down. The mother turned to the runner.

“Thank you.”

“Transit did most of it.”

“You got us here.”

The runner pointed at Ollie.

“He carried the heavy box.”

The mother nodded to him.

“Thanks for the box.”

“You're welcome.”

Ollie left before somebody could find another box. On the way back, his band showed Segment Three occupancy at eighty-seven. He pictured the school bag blocking the new doorway and checked the next service window.

* * *

Peak migration began after lunch.

The schedule called it `HIGH THROUGHPUT WINDOW 2`. The corridor called it too many things in the same place.

A bedding cart arrived early because its previous handoff had taken less time than expected. A food service unit arrived late because another ring had held its freight transfer. Two households came together instead of eleven minutes apart because one family had waited for a grandparent. A school support group crossed the same junction because tomorrow's route orientation had been moved forward.

None of those events was a failure. Together, they changed sequence.

The supervising operator held the bedding cart. That pushed its service transition later. The food unit took the opening, moving a cleaning return. The two households slowed at the resident scan because one child's credential had not inherited the new room address correctly. Housing fixed it in three minutes. The school group used the outer route; Maya's temporary marker sent them correctly.

Ollie's recovery-margin display narrowed while staying inside the certified range. Residual return settled above the conservative floor, then above it by less, then wider again after a held service transition.

At 14:22, a resident-support report entered the local review rail.

`SEGMENT 3 / TRANSITION 14:21:17`

`REPORT: brief floor-pressure “soft step” sensation during service change.`

No medical event. No interface alarm. No fixed-system threshold crossing.

Ollie opened the event. The fixed controller state showed normal closure; physical return was still settling when the report occurred.

He tagged it:

`EARLY REPORT / EVENT ORDER RETAINED / NO PROFILE EXIT.`

Then he sent it to the supervising operator and left STOP REQUEST untouched.

She read it.

“Anything outside approved state?”

“No.”

“Anything requiring stop?”

“No.”

“Keep it separate.”

Ollie left the report rail open beside the recovery-state display.

At 14:39, another household crossed with no report at all during a tighter recovery interval. He kept that event beside the earlier report instead of promoting either one into a rule.

At 15:03, a service delay produced the closest approach yet to the Segment Three conservative boundary. The local display changed from green to amber-margin advisory.

Ollie's thumb hovered over STOP REQUEST.

The supervising operator was already looking at the same state.

“Hold next service release,” she said to the local controller.

The release held. Recovery widened. Amber cleared.

Ollie removed his thumb.

The operator glanced at him.

“You were going to request.”

“If it crossed.”

“It did not.”

“No.”

She nodded once and went back to work.

Ollie was relieved there had been nothing to prove.

By 16:00, Segment Three had dinner smells. Somebody had opened a door during a food delivery and complained that the corridor was too cold. Two teenagers sat on the floor beside a wall that still carried a temporary occupancy notice, playing music loudly enough to annoy a maintenance worker.

“Move,” the worker said.

They shifted three meters.

The worker stared.

They shifted another two.

The temporary occupancy notice disappeared behind one teenager's shoulder.

Ollie checked the board again.

Occupancy: 146.

Service substitutions: 7 active / 19 resolved.

Route delays: above plan, within contingency.

Recovery margin: reduced from morning baseline, within certified Segment Three operating profile.

Then the upper-right corner of the operations board changed.

`OLDER-SECTOR SERVICE / SHIELDING WORK`

`REDUCED REDUNDANCY WINDOW — 18:00–20:40`

`STATUS: PLANNED / PRE-CHECK ACTIVE`

Nobody in the room looked surprised. The work had been on the schedule since before the first household moved. What had not been on the schedule was seven active substitutions at sixteen hundred.

Mara returned to the local desk with two operations staff behind her.

“Actual load pass,” she said.

The supervising operator pushed the current migration profile to the larger board.

“Resident flow fourteen percent above planned peak at two crossings. Freight below planned total, worse clustering. Service substitutions still elevated.”

“Reduced-redundancy prediction?” Mara asked.

The operator ran the approved contingency model with the current load state. The predicted minimum margin fell and stopped above the certified floor.

Mara asked, “Any reason the model is invalid under current conditions?”

Ollie did not answer because she was not asking him.

The operations engineer said, “Current sequence classes are inside the validated segment profile. The clustering costs margin. It does not introduce a new state class.”

“Can the older-sector work move?”

“Not cleanly. We can delay forty minutes before it collides with the next service block. Beyond that we start carrying reduced capacity into tomorrow's first school and maintenance cycle.”

Mara looked at housing.

“How much migration remains at eighteen?”

“Lower than now. Not zero.”

The supervising operator said, “If we hold nonessential service transitions through the reduced window, predicted floor improves.”

“Cost?”

“Queues. Delayed deliveries. Two maintenance tasks shift.”

Mara looked at the board again.

“Do that. Re-run at seventeen thirty with actual flow. If the segment cannot hold the approved floor, reduced-redundancy work waits.”

No one argued.

At 16:17, another relocation cart entered the queue. The operator moved one service release ten minutes later. A housing runner changed a family's arrival route.

Somewhere inside Segment Three, somebody started drilling into a wall they had been told not to drill into. The maintenance ticket appeared before the sound reached the support desk.

Ollie glanced from the ticket to the reduced-redundancy board and back to the live recovery margin. Everything remained inside profile.

He placed one event marker on the clustered service sequence and left STOP REQUEST untouched.

Behind him, another room door opened for the first time.
