# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 29 — SEGMENTS

The first version of the plan opened Ring Six.

The second did not.

The third opened part of it and started an argument about laundry.

Ollie preferred the third.

He was not in Ring Six when they built it.

His review status still read:

REVIEW HOLD — NO UNSUPERVISED COMMISSIONING ACCESS.

The planning room was two rings away, so the residential sector existed for him as six irregular segment profiles, a service map and a consequence wall he could not walk into to verify for himself.

Mara stood beside the six profiles.

Nadia looked at the heading.

“Do not call them blocks.”

“I am not.”

“You were thinking it.”

Mara changed the heading to:

SEGMENT PROFILES.

Nadia let that survive.

The six numbered pieces had stopped being equivalent the moment the Ch27 result met the real city.

One had clean service separation but an ugly school transfer.

Another fit the deeper-recovery interlock well until deliveries and maintenance wanted the same crossing.

Segment Three could tolerate the conservative timing with little loss of ordinary residential movement, provided evening guest density and delivery windows stayed bounded.

Four could reproduce the candidate shared-loop state under the wrong sequence.

Five depended on Four for an efficient service path.

Six could avoid that dependency only by lengthening a school route and crowding an early-shift transfer.

Ollie enlarged Segment Three.

“Three can support the A-profile interlock without changing its common residential route.”

Mara asked for the occupancy limit.

He gave it.

“Guests?”

“Limited after twenty-one hundred until the second validation set.”

“Deliveries?”

“Staged outside resident peak.”

“School movement?”

“Inside Three, unchanged. External transfer still depends on One.”

Nadia pointed at the housing layer.

“So the room can fit while Tuesday morning does not.”

Ollie added the actual requirement instead of a slogan:

HOUSING COMPATIBILITY REQUIRES ROOM + ROUTE + SERVICE ACCESS.

They worked through the other profiles.

One proposed opening survived environmental review and failed on a daily care route.

A redundant-looking service path turned out to be the only practical late-food route once a common corridor went into reduced-density operation.

One guest rule would have required a sixteen-year-old resident to sign out of their own home to visit a friend two doors away after the cutoff.

Nadia killed that one immediately.

“Again.”

Ollie changed the rule.

By the time they stopped for food, the promise had shrunk to something a city might actually do.

Three segments likely certifiable.

A fourth possible under restricted service routing and additional review.

Two closed.

No one called it a successful opening.

Mara sent the candidate profile to safety.

Nadia sent the same profile to housing.

Ollie’s name travelled with it under:

MODEL SUPPORT.

Not decision.

Not authority.

He noticed the difference and went back to the map.

* * *

Housing allocation used the same wall and changed what every shape meant.

The safe segments became rooms.

The rooms became households.

A route that had looked like an acceptable twenty-three-minute penalty became a child leaving for school before the rest of the household woke.

A technically compatible room became useless when the daily care connection crossed a closed segment.

A household that could physically split across two safe rooms did not automatically want to live split across two safe rooms.

Nadia put up the first provisional list.

A resident representative asked, “First compatible in the queue?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because speed through the queue is not the only constraint.”

Another resident asked, “Need first?”

Nadia looked at the wall.

“Which need?”

Nobody answered quickly.

Ollie opened the compatibility matrix on his private panel.

Household size.

Environmental range.

Route access.

School location.

Shift location.

Accessibility.

Temporary burden.

Wait time.

He could sort it.

Hard constraints first.

Then burden.

Then wait.

Then route efficiency.

Seven households rose to the top and looked completely reasonable.

That was the problem.

Ollie closed the rank column.

Nadia saw the movement.

“What did you build?”

“A private sort.”

“For what?”

“To see whether an obvious order appeared.”

“Did one?”

“After I chose what mattered first.”

Nadia went back to the public list.

“That is why we are doing this here.”

She moved one household upward.

Ollie opened the case note.

Their temporary placement ended in nine days. Extension meant another temporary move before the Ring Six move, another school-route reset and another storage transfer.

The field had said nine days.

The household had said two moves.

Ollie added a visible flag.

Another household moved down because the only compatible first-phase room sat across a transfer closure from daily care.

A third could move immediately but would lose the work-route advantage that had made Ring Six useful to them.

One resident accepted that trade.

Another did not.

A housing officer joined remotely and asked whether two more people could fit if one family accepted split rooms.

“Physically,” Nadia said.

Ollie checked the service model.

“Operationally too.”

“Then why are they still red?”

Nadia opened the household preference.

“They declined the split.”

The officer nodded.

“Keep red.”

Ollie looked at his private panel.

His sorter would have moved them.

Nobody had stopped him because nobody had seen him think it.

He deleted the rule.

* * *

Lea arrived after school with her bag over one shoulder.

She did not sit.

Her household block was already open.

Two members fit the first phase.

Two did not.

One incompatible route depended on a closed segment. The other needed an environmental profile available only in a cluster not opening yet.

Housing had three possibilities on the wall.

WAIT TOGETHER.

TEMPORARY SPLIT / FIRST-PHASE PARTIAL MOVE.

REVIEW IF FOURTH SEGMENT CLEARS.

Lea read them twice.

“So we are eligible.”

“Partly,” Nadia said.

Lea’s mouth tightened.

“My mother can move.”

“Yes.”

“My brother can move.”

“Yes.”

“And I stay where we are.”

“If your household chooses the partial move.”

“Which means we split.”

Nadia let the sentence stand.

Lea dropped her bag onto a chair. It bounced off and landed on the floor.

She looked at Ollie.

“You said segments, not people.”

“I did.”

“This is people.”

Ollie looked at the open household block.

“Yes.”

“Can Four open?”

Mara answered from the other side of the wall.

“Not on current evidence.”

“When?”

“Unknown.”

Lea laughed once without finding anything funny.

Nadia said, “Your household can wait together. No one is forcing a partial move.”

Lea rubbed both hands over her face.

“That does not make either option good.”

“No.”

For a few seconds she stayed there, looking younger than she usually allowed herself to look.

Then she pointed at another household.

“What about them?”

Nadia opened it.

“Full household fit. School route works. One work-route penalty; they accepted it.”

“They go?”

“If final review holds.”

“And them?”

Another block.

“Wait.”

“Why?”

“Accessibility route crosses Five.”

Lea moved to the next.

For several minutes she went through the first-phase list.

In.

Wait.

Maybe.

Partial.

Not because she had calmed down. She wanted to see who the plan hurt when it was not her family.

At the bottom of the list she looked at the number of households that could move, then at her own split block.

“This is awful.”

Nobody corrected her.

She pointed at the first-phase total.

“But you are still doing it.”

“If safety approves the profile,” Nadia said.

Lea looked at Ollie.

He felt the explanation forming: why the profile was defensible, why partial capacity was still capacity, why waiting for everyone could keep everyone waiting.

He did not give it to her.

“Do not close it because we do not fit,” Lea said.

“I cannot open or close it.”

“You know what I mean.”

He did.

“I hate this plan,” she said.

Ollie left room for that too.

“And if you find a way to put us ahead because I helped you, I will hate you too.”

“That was not—”

He stopped.

Not a plan.

A want.

He had wanted the work to return something to someone he knew.

Lea picked up her bag. The strap had twisted under the chair leg.

She pulled once, hard enough to move the chair and not the strap.

Ollie lifted the chair.

Lea freed the bag.

“Thanks.”

At the door she asked Nadia when the final list would be released.

Nadia gave her the review window.

Lea nodded and left.

The first-phase map remained on the wall.

Three likely segments.

One conditional.

Two closed.

Some households moving.

Some waiting.

No clean way to make the categories coincide.

* * *

Across the room, Aoife was helping a resident find the correct temporary-route form on a public terminal.

Her recorder was clipped inside her bag.

When the resident left, Aoife came over.

“Move prep tomorrow?”

“For households likely to clear first phase,” Nadia said. “And support for people still waiting.”

“Can I help?”

Nadia glanced at the bag.

Aoife followed her eyes.

“I am not filming.”

“Then yes.”

Aoife took out her device.

Ollie expected the recorder app.

She opened her schedule.

“Where?”

Nadia sent the location.

Aoife put the device away.

Ollie looked back at Ring Six.

The plan was smaller than the one they had started with.

Enough rooms that tomorrow would be different for some people.

Not enough for everyone to call it home.
