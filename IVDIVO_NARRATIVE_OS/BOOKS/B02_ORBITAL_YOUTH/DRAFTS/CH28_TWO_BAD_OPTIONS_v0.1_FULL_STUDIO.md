# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 28 — TWO BAD OPTIONS

Ethan was asked to map the consequences.

He heard: find the answer.

Nobody had said that.

The task arrived through the Youth Cohort civic liaison queue at 07:16, while he was eating breakfast and trying to persuade a piece of fruit not to roll off the table every time the household floor shifted into its morning gravity adjustment.

RING SIX — OPERATIONAL PATH CONSEQUENCE MAP.

REQUESTED OUTPUT:

OPTION A — FULL-RECOVERY INTERLOCK.

OPTION B — STATE-AWARE CONTROL DEVELOPMENT.

DEPENDENCIES / COSTS / UNKNOWNS / AFFECTED GROUPS.

NO RECOMMENDATION REQUESTED.

Ethan read the last line.

Then opened a new workspace called:

R6 — OPTION COMPARISON.

He did not add *recommendation* to the title.

That counted as restraint for almost four minutes.

By 07:41 he had built the first scoring layer.

He deleted it before leaving for the civic work volume.

Then restored it from version history on the transfer.

Not because he intended to submit it.

Because a comparison without weights was just a list.

He told himself that until the transfer arrived.

* * *

Nadia Park had already filled one wall with households.

Not names.

Blocks.

Current temporary placement.
Required room compatibility.
School route.
Care route where relevant.
Work shift overlap.
Transfer burden.

Each household looked simple until Ethan opened it.

Then it became five schedules and a door.

Nadia pointed at the wall.

“That is the housing side.”

Ethan looked at the color bands.

“Current priority order?”

“Current eligibility order. Priority changes when the operating profile changes.”

“That is worse.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Nadia looked at him.

“For you?”

“For the map.”

“That is what I thought.”

Ethan connected the Ch27 released engineering summary.

No raw incident data.

No private support records.

Only the two operational directions and their current evidence ceilings.

Option A first.

FULL-RECOVERY INTERLOCK.

Certain service transitions could not begin until the relevant physical return network reached a much deeper recovery threshold than the old controller state had required.

The logic was simple enough to explain in one sentence.

The consequences were not.

Every additional wait changed the next movement slot.

A service cart held longer.

A door stayed unavailable longer.

A maintenance crossing moved.

Peak residential flow lost flexibility.

Some route conflicts became queues.

The queues then required staffing or scheduling.

Nadia watched Ethan move the first set of blocks.

“Do not optimize yet.”

“I am not optimizing.”

“You just moved three school routes to reduce service overlap.”

“That is consequence mapping.”

“You smiled.”

“I did not.”

“You made the face.”

Ethan had begun to hate that phrase.

He restored the school routes to their current positions.

“Fine. We map current consequence first.”

“Thank you.”

Under A, the first plausible occupied profile could be validated sooner because it depended less on a real-time estimator that had not yet proved itself operationally.

But the ring would not run as designed.

Not initially.

Its usable capacity fell.

Some household clusters still fit.

Others did not.

A family that needed two adjacent sleeping modules and a particular transfer relation to school could move only if enough nearby service windows stayed available.

A worker on an early maintenance shift might gain a room and lose a reliable route to work.

Nadia opened one cluster.

“Lea’s household.”

Ethan stopped.

The family’s temporary arrangement was still split across incompatible routes.

Under the reduced A profile, they might fit in an early return group.

Might.

“Probability?” Ethan asked.

Nadia gave him a look.

“Allocation is not weather.”

“Current eligibility likelihood.”

“Depends who else becomes eligible under the same profile.”

“So we model the queue.”

“Yes. But do not turn it into a promise.”

Ethan added:

LEA HOUSEHOLD — EARLY ELIGIBILITY POSSIBLE / NOT GUARANTEED.

Then Option B.

STATE-AWARE CONTROL DEVELOPMENT.

The Ch27 rig result suggested the controller could eventually estimate residual recovery state and block only combinations entering the candidate shared-loop window.

If it worked, Ring Six retained much more of the flexibility it had been designed for.

If it worked.

The phrase appeared in Ethan’s map six times.

He disliked all six.

B required more validation before anyone could reasonably use it as an occupied control authority.

The shared-loop estimator needed repeated test runs.

False positives needed measurement.

False negatives needed something more difficult than measurement because the dangerous escalation condition was still unknown.

The system could be excellent at recognizing the state they now knew how to reproduce and still miss whatever made Ring Six dangerous.

Ethan wrote:

VALIDATION TIME — UNKNOWN / LONGER THAN A.

Nadia said, “That will make housing angry.”

“Housing is not a person.”

“I am.”

“Are you angry?”

“Yes.”

“Useful.”

“Stop enjoying this.”

Ethan added the B consequences.

Ring Six remained shut longer.

Temporary housing continued.

School transfers continued.

Service staff continued carrying temporary routing load.

If B later passed, more households could return under the intended operating profile.

If it failed, they would have spent the additional validation period and still need A or something else.

Ethan stepped back from the wall.

Two bad options.

Good.

A map could handle that.

He opened the scoring layer again.

Nadia saw the new panel.

“What is that?”

“Normalization.”

“No.”

“You have not seen it.”

“I know what the word means.”

“Then you know why we need it.”

“No. We need consequences.”

“We have consequences. Now we compare them.”

“That is not what you were asked for.”

Ethan looked at the request.

NO RECOMMENDATION REQUESTED.

“I am not submitting the score.”

“Then why build it?”

“To know whether the consequences imply anything.”

Nadia folded her arms.

Ethan continued before she could answer.

“Safety margin. Housing displacement. Service disruption. Access impact. Validation uncertainty. Reversibility.”

“You forgot political anger.”

“That is not independent.”

“Good luck with that.”

He ignored her.

The first weighted pass favored B.

Not strongly.

Enough.

If the estimator reached a reasonable validation threshold, the expected housing and service cost over the full commissioning period fell below A’s persistent throughput loss.

Ethan stared at the result.

It looked sensible.

Which made him immediately suspicious of how good it felt.

He saved it privately.

Not for submission.

Just in case.

* * *

The affected-use review happened in a room designed for forty people and occupied by nine.

That helped.

No stage.

No audience seating.

One wall carried Ethan’s consequence map.

Another showed the current temporary housing routes.

Mara stood near the engineering summary with the expression of someone who had already attended two meetings that day and expected this one to try something stupid.

Tomas sat rather than stood.

His left forearm was still restricted inside a support brace, though the rest of him looked less injured than the fact of the brace made everyone want to assume.

He had been reassigned to service scheduling and light-duty planning until manual clearance changed.

Ethan had not met him before the incident.

That made the first conversation easier.

“You built the service queue model?” Tomas asked.

“With Nadia’s current staffing inputs.”

“Current meaning before or after they moved two people to relocation support?”

Ethan looked at Nadia.

Nadia said, “After this morning’s assignment.”

Tomas nodded.

“Then it is already old. One of them is back tomorrow unless medical changes.”

Ethan changed the staffing note.

“Useful.”

Tomas looked at the brace.

“Glad I could provide dramatic data.”

Ethan froze.

Tomas smiled slightly.

“That was a joke.”

“Right.”

“You are terrible at this.”

“I know.”

“Good. We have common ground.”

Lea arrived with her mother, who stayed for the opening and then left because her younger child had a school transfer she did not trust the temporary route to handle alone.

An older resident from another waiting household sat near the route wall. Their household had different access requirements and would not fit the first reduced-capacity profile as easily as Lea’s might.

Nobody represented “the residents.”

That also helped.

The review facilitator asked Ethan to walk through consequences.

“Not recommendation,” she said.

Ethan said, “Understood.”

Mara looked at him.

He ignored that.

Option A took six minutes.

He kept the engineering explanation short.

Wait longer for real physical recovery before certain service combinations.

Lower model dependence.

Faster path to an occupied profile if validation held.

Then the cost.

Reduced capacity.

More service staging.

Longer peak queues.

Some households return sooner.

Some remain temporary because the reduced profile cannot support their room/route combination yet.

The older resident interrupted there.

“Which households?”

Ethan opened the allocation layer.

“Not fixed until the profile is final.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No.”

The resident waited.

Ethan changed the map to show compatibility classes rather than household blocks.

The resident’s class moved into a later group under the current A simulation.

They looked at the wall.

“So ‘reopen sooner’ means someone else goes home sooner.”

Nadia said, “Under this version, yes.”

The resident leaned back.

“Then say that when you say faster.”

Ethan added a note.

FASTER REOPENING ≠ EQUAL RETURN DATE.

Lea was staring at her own household block.

It sat near the first eligible boundary.

“Are we in or not?” she asked.

Nadia answered.

“Not known until the capacity profile and allocation set are both fixed.”

“That is an excellent sentence for people who already have a room.”

Nadia did not flinch.

“Yes.”

Lea looked at Ethan.

“You have us as ‘possible early eligibility.’”

“Yes.”

“That means nothing.”

“It means you are not excluded by the current A profile.”

“Which means maybe.”

“Yes.”

“Write maybe.”

Ethan looked at the label.

He changed it.

LEA HOUSEHOLD — MAYBE EARLY / NOT PROMISED.

Lea nodded.

“That is uglier.”

“Yes.”

“Better.”

Ethan moved to B.

Longer validation.

Unknown reopening date.

Higher eventual capacity if the estimator proved reliable.

Fewer permanent throughput penalties.

More dependence on an incomplete model.

Mara interrupted before he reached his uncertainty slide.

“Say what B does not know.”

Ethan did.

“It can estimate the shared-loop state we reproduced in Phase One. It does not yet tell us which of those states escalate. It does not establish the role of resident or support timing. It does not have validated false-negative performance against the dangerous condition because we do not yet have a complete definition of that condition.”

The older resident said, “So B is smarter and knows less.”

Mara said, “B is more selective and needs more evidence.”

“That sounded nicer.”

“It was more accurate.”

Tomas asked, “What happens if A creates service queues?”

Ethan opened the service layer.

“At peak load, some service transitions shift to scheduled windows. The current model adds manual staging demand.”

“How much?”

“Depends on occupancy.”

“At the first occupied profile.”

Ethan gave the current range.

Tomas shook his head.

“That assumes we keep all current maintenance windows.”

“Yes.”

“We will not.”

“Why?”

“Because if service access starts waiting for deep recovery, somebody has to decide whether the cart waits, the maintenance waits or the resident route waits. The schedule can hide that by moving blocks. The corridor cannot.”

Ethan opened the staffing layer.

Tomas pointed at two mature-ring maintenance blocks.

“Those go first.”

“Why those?”

“Because they are easiest to move without immediate failure.”

“That creates deferred maintenance.”

“Yes.”

“So A shifts risk.”

Tomas looked at him.

“Everything shifts something.”

Ethan added the deferred maintenance cost.

His private weighted score changed.

B moved farther ahead.

He did not show it.

The review moved to hybrid possibilities.

Ethan had been waiting for that.

“What about A as interim operation while B validates in parallel?” he said.

It sounded good even before the wall finished drawing it.

Partial reopening sooner.

State-aware development continues.

Later transition to higher-capacity profile.

Lea said, “Move twice.”

Ethan turned.

“What?”

“If we get into the reduced profile and then the room allocation changes when B comes in.”

Nadia said, “Possible.”

“We move again.”

“Some households could.”

The older resident said, “And some of us wait through A and still wait for B.”

Tomas pointed at the service schedule.

“A needs its own validated operating pattern. B needs test capacity. Same people do a lot of both.”

Mara said, “Parallel does not mean free.”

Ethan looked at the hybrid layer.

It was still probably best.

He could model the transition cost.

He started doing it.

The facilitator said, “Ethan.”

He looked up.

“We need the consequences today. Not a third proposal.”

“It is not a third proposal. It is a sequence.”

Nadia closed her eyes for one second.

“That is extremely Ethan.”

Lea said, “Is that a technical category?”

Ethan looked at both of them.

Nobody apologized.

He stopped the hybrid calculation.

“Fine.”

The facilitator moved the review into affected priorities.

That was where Ethan’s clean map stopped behaving.

* * *

Lea ranked return date first.

Then school route.

Then not moving twice.

Not maximum capacity.

Not theoretical final efficiency.

“Why not capacity?” Ethan asked.

“Because I do not live in maximum capacity.”

“That is not what I meant.”

“I know.”

The older resident ranked access compatibility first, then predictability, then return date.

A faster reopening that excluded their household was not faster from where they were sitting.

Tomas ranked service stability above peak housing throughput because deferred mature-ring maintenance created costs elsewhere that Ring Six did not get to ignore.

Nadia refused to rank housing urgency against unknown safety failure.

“That is the decision,” Ethan said.

“No. That is one of the things the decision has to contain.”

“What is the difference?”

“The board can choose a weight. I am not going to pretend housing supplied it as a technical input.”

Ethan looked at Mara.

“Safety margin?”

Mara said, “Which one?”

“The relative value.”

“That is not a number I can give you.”

“You use risk thresholds.”

“Yes.”

“So there is a number.”

“For a defined hazard with evidence behind the threshold.”

“And here?”

“Here the false-negative behavior of B against the dangerous escalation condition is unknown.”

Ethan opened his private weighted layer.

He had assigned uncertainty a penalty.

A large one.

Not infinite.

Mara saw the layer before he could minimize it.

“What is that?”

Ethan hesitated.

“Private comparison.”

Nadia leaned forward.

“You built the score.”

“It is exploratory.”

Lea said, “Of course he built the score.”

Ethan expanded it because hiding it now would be worse.

SAFETY UNCERTAINTY.

HOUSING DISPLACEMENT.

SERVICE DISRUPTION.

ACCESS / EQUALITY.

VALIDATION TIME.

REVERSIBILITY.

Weights beside each.

Normalized values.

Expected cost bands.

At the bottom:

CURRENT PREFERENCE — B / MODERATE CONFIDENCE.

The room got quieter.

Ethan said, “It is not the submitted map.”

Lea pointed at HOUSING DISPLACEMENT.

“Why is that weight twelve?”

“It is normalized.”

“I can read twelve.”

“It is relative.”

“To what?”

Ethan opened the definition.

“Temporary household-days, route burden, school disruption and—”

“No. Why twelve?”

He stopped.

“Because I had to set a reference value.”

Lea looked at him.

“So you picked it.”

“I calibrated it against the other disruption categories.”

“You picked the calibration.”

“Yes.”

Mara pointed at SAFETY UNCERTAINTY.

“And this?”

“I gave unknown false-negative risk the largest penalty.”

“How large?”

He showed her.

“Why that large?”

“Because it is the least bounded risk.”

“That tells you why it deserves attention. Not the exchange rate against thirty households waiting.”

Ethan looked at the map.

Nadia pointed at ACCESS / EQUALITY.

“You counted households affected.”

“Yes.”

“Which households?”

“The distribution layer is separate.”

“Then your score can improve while the same group loses every time.”

Ethan felt the argument forming into pieces he knew how to answer.

Weights could be sensitivity-tested.

Distributions could be constrained.

Unknown risk could be bounded with scenarios.

He opened his mouth.

Then saw what he was about to do.

Build a better machine to hide the choice more carefully.

He closed the score.

Lea said, “That is not an answer.”

“No.”

“What are you doing?”

“Removing it.”

“Why?”

“Because the dependencies are mine to map. The weights are not.”

The sentence sounded almost too clean.

Ethan disliked it immediately.

He corrected.

“I mean—I can show what changes if you weight things differently. I cannot pretend the first weights came from the engineering.”

Mara said, “Better.”

Lea said, “Still annoying.”

“Also better.”

He reopened the consequence map without the scoring layer.

The wall looked less decisive.

Option A did not turn red.

Option B did not turn green.

The affected-priority notes stayed attached to the people who had made them.

Lea: RETURN DATE / SCHOOL ROUTE / AVOID SECOND MOVE.

OTHER HOUSEHOLD: ACCESS COMPATIBILITY / PREDICTABILITY / RETURN DATE.

SERVICE: STABILITY / STAFF LOAD / DEFERRED MAINTENANCE.

HOUSING: NUMBER STILL DISPLACED / SPLIT HOUSEHOLDS / ALLOCATION FAIRNESS.

SAFETY: EVIDENCE CEILING / REVERSIBILITY / UNKNOWN ESCALATION CONDITION.

Ethan looked at Lea’s line.

“Anything missing?”

She considered.

“Do not call ‘avoid second move’ convenience.”

“I didn’t.”

“You were going to.”

“I was going to call it relocation burden.”

“That is worse.”

“What do you want?”

Lea pointed.

“Do not make us move twice.”

Ethan typed exactly that.

LEA HOUSEHOLD PRIORITY: DO NOT MAKE US MOVE TWICE.

Nadia laughed once.

The facilitator did not remove it.

* * *

The review ended without choosing A or B.

Ethan found that deeply unsatisfying.

The facilitator accepted the consequence map and attached it to the next commissioning/civic decision packet.

The scoring layer was not included.

The hybrid sequence remained in notes as a possible longer-term strategy with explicit transition costs.

Nobody declared victory.

Tomas left first because his light-duty shift restarted in nineteen minutes.

The older resident left to catch a care transfer.

Lea stayed long enough to look at the final map.

“Which one do you think they’ll choose?” she asked.

Ethan looked at A.

Then B.

“I don’t know.”

Lea looked suspicious.

“You hate saying that.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“You are all becoming like Iri.”

“I don’t know what that means.”

“Exactly.”

Lea left.

Nadia started clearing the wall.

Ethan stopped her from removing the affected-priority notes.

“Those stay with the packet.”

“They were always staying.”

“Right.”

Nadia handed him the small strip from the bottom of his private score printout.

CURRENT PREFERENCE — B / MODERATE CONFIDENCE.

“Souvenir?” she asked.

Ethan took it.

He folded it once.

Then again.

Dropped it into recycling.

His band showed three messages from the group.

Maya was still arguing about her return window.

Samir had practice.

Ollie had sent nothing because Ollie was apparently becoming capable of not sending work updates to everyone.

Aoife had written:

AOIFE: alive after civic optimization?

Ethan typed:

ETHAN: no optimization

He deleted it.

Typed:

ETHAN: consequences mapped. no recommendation.

AOIFE: painful?

ETHAN: extremely

Three dots.

AOIFE: food later?

Ethan opened his schedule.

His first reflex was to find the best time for all five.

He closed the schedule.

ETHAN: when are you free?

Aoife replied with one time.

It did not work for everyone.

Ethan accepted it anyway.
