ORBITAL YOUTH
Chapter 19 — GO / NO-GO

Ollie knew the review was serious because nobody had asked him to make the slides prettier.

They had asked him to make them shorter.

He stood behind the second row of the commissioning room with a twelve-page timing summary reduced to three pages, two live traces and one diagram he still considered offensively simplified.

Mara looked over his shoulder.

“You added a fourth page.”

“It is an appendix.”

“It is visible.”

“It is available if asked.”

“You have discovered a loophole.”

“I have discovered responsible documentation.”

Mara removed the appendix from the shared view.

“You can be responsible when asked.”

The room was already filling.

Not with the people Ollie had expected when he first imagined a GO / NO-GO review.

There were engineers, yes.
Environmental safety.
Commissioning.
Service operations.
Housing allocation.
Two resident representatives.
One school-route coordinator who looked annoyed to be there and kept checking another screen.
An apprentice supervisor.
Three people Ollie did not know who seemed to have arrived because a delayed opening changed somebody else’s work.

Lea sat with an older member of her household near the side wall.

Iri stood behind the apprentice row and had brought no visible notes.

Ren had brought too many.

“What are those?” Ollie whispered.

“Questions.”

“You have eight pages.”

“I am curious.”

“You are dangerous.”

Mara looked at them.

Ren closed the notes.

The meeting began seven minutes late because one resident representative had been held by a childcare transfer and the board chair refused to start the resident section without them.

Ollie liked that.

Then he immediately disliked himself for liking anything about a meeting where his name appeared on the evidence packet.

The chair opened with the decision in one sentence.

“Today we decide whether Ring Six remains unoccupied, proceeds to another bounded test period, or enters staged partial habitation under restrictions.”

No speech.
No importance music.

A service coordinator asked whether the proposed restrictions had changed since the morning packet.

“Two,” Mara said. “Lower occupancy density in the first residential sector. Earlier stop threshold for unresolved resident or interface reports.”

The housing coordinator looked up.

“That second condition was already conservative.”

“It is now more conservative.”

“That has allocation consequences.”

“Yes.”

The chair said, “Then we will include those consequences.”

Ollie watched the room settle into the real argument.

It was not safety versus housing.

It was six different kinds of risk trying to use the same calendar.

The first technical presenter put up the anomaly history.

Four disputed fixed-system lag events before the controlled test.

No component failure.
No formal threshold breach.
Repeated embodied or personal-interface reports preceding some fixed response changes.

Then the Ch15 trace.

Cycle one.

The line Ollie had stared at for too long reappeared at room scale.

A fixed-system follow beginning 3.2 seconds after the transition event.

Inside the ordinary settling band.
Closing without propagation.

The conservative safety engineer pointed at it.

“This is why I am not prepared to call the predictor isolated.”

Nobody argued immediately.

Good.

The engineer continued.

“We changed the sequence and removed the disputed lag. That is useful. We also retained a smaller follow response in the first realistic cycle. It was allowed by the operating band, yes. But the existence of a smaller permitted response does not prove the larger events belong to a separate mechanism.”

Ollie felt the answer form before the engineer finished.

He did not say it.

Mara had made him practice not answering questions that had not been given to him.

It was a terrible skill.

The permissive interpretation came from service operations rather than housing.

That surprised him.

“If we treat any expected settling response as evidence against opening,” the service lead said, “then we have converted ‘within tolerance’ into a hidden failure condition. The disputed lag did not reproduce under the controlled sequence. The messier cycle did not reproduce it either. If the identified overlap is avoided and the stop threshold is lower, we have a defensible restricted profile.”

The safety engineer nodded once.

“I said delay, not indefinite closure.”

“How much delay?” the housing coordinator asked.

“Enough to test another class of mixed route substitution.”

“Two weeks?”

“Probably.”

“Probably is doing work there.”

“So is ‘another month of temporary allocation.’”

The chair lifted one hand.

“Good. Keep both costs visible.”

Ollie had expected the conservative side to sound frightened.

It did not.

He had expected the permissive side to sound impatient.

It did not.

That made the choice worse.

The presenter changed to the sequence analysis.

Ollie’s name appeared in smaller type than last time.

He was grateful.

Then the chair said, “Ollie, take us through what changed between the disputed events and the successful test.”

He stood.

His knees did nothing interesting.

Also good.

He opened the first trace.

“The repeated feature in the disputed fixed lag events was not occupancy alone. It was a sequence overlap. The second partition began cycling before the first correction had fully settled. That produced a repeated timing pattern across the four events.”

He switched to Ch15.

“In the bounded test, we prevented that overlap. In cycle one, the fixed system still followed the occupancy transition by 3.2 seconds, but the response remained inside the expected settling band and closed without propagation.”

The safety engineer said, “Meaning?”

Ollie looked at the trace.

“Meaning removing the overlap did not remove all fixed response.”

“Thank you.”

He switched to cycle two.

“The second cycle was less orderly. More simultaneous movement, a slower crossing path, deliveries arriving in the wrong corridor, service traffic against the dominant flow. The prohibited overlap still did not occur. The disputed fixed lag still did not occur.”

A resident representative said, “So the condition works.”

Ollie almost said yes.

Mara said, “Which condition?”

The representative looked at her.

“The one he identified.”

Mara turned to Ollie.

“Answer precisely.”

Ollie hated her for half a second.

“The sequence rule predicts the disputed fixed lag better than any other variable we have tested so far.”

“That was not the question,” Mara said.

He knew.

He looked at the resident representative.

“Under the tested conditions, preventing that overlap prevented the disputed lag.”

“Does that make Ring Six safe?”

“No.”

The answer came easier than he expected.

The room changed slightly.

The representative leaned back.

“What does your model not prove?” Mara asked.

He had practiced this too.

That did not make it less public.

“It does not prove root cause. It does not classify the early embodied or personal-interface reports. It does not prove unrestricted habitation safety. It does not prove route or service combinations we have not tested are equivalent to the ones we did test.”

The safety engineer added, “And the 3.2-second follow?”

“It does not prove the smaller follow and the disputed lag are unrelated.”

The engineer nodded.

Not approval.

Acceptance of the sentence.

Ollie sat.

His fourth page remained invisible.

He no longer wanted it.

* * *

The resident section destroyed the clean shape of the technical argument in under four minutes.

Lea’s household wanted the restricted opening.

The family beside them did not.

The parent from the second household had two children assigned to Ring Six schools and had already moved temporary sleeping allocations once.

“I am not moving them into another place that might close in ten days,” she said. “If you need two more weeks to know more, take two weeks.”

Lea’s older household member answered before the board chair could.

“And if two weeks becomes another two?”

“Then at least I did not unpack twice.”

“We are already unpacked in three places.”

“That is your household.”

“Yes.”

The sentence stayed in the room.

No one solved it.

The school-route coordinator finally looked up from the other screen.

“If Ring Six opens at the proposed density, I can remove twelve temporary morning transfers and four late-cycle returns in the first allocation block.”

The parent who wanted delay said, “And if it closes?”

“I rebuild the routes again.”

“You say that like it is neutral.”

“It is not. I am telling you the cost on my side.”

A service planner interrupted.

“Opening at reduced density also means we need the maintenance team to cover two parallel access states for at least the first week. That is more staffing than closure.”

The housing coordinator said, “But less than another month of temporary placements across six sectors.”

“Staffing is not a philosophical quantity.”

“Neither are beds.”

The board chair closed their eyes for one second.

Ollie understood the impulse.

He had wanted a resident consensus because residents were supposed to be the people for whom the decision existed.

Instead they had different children, different shifts, different temporary rooms and different tolerance for moving twice.

Lea caught him looking.

She raised one eyebrow.

He looked away.

Iri did not.

They were standing when the apprentice supervisor called their section.

The supervisor said, “Our crews can support the restricted profile. We are split on whether we should.”

Ren whispered, “Excellent opening.”

Iri elbowed him.

The supervisor continued.

“Some of the apprentices prefer another test because the service burden of a partial opening is ugly. Restricted routes mean every exception becomes manual work. Others would rather learn the occupied state now because another test period does not reproduce real scheduling pressure.”

The chair asked, “Your position?”

“Staged opening. Stricter stop conditions than housing proposed.”

Mara did not react.

The chair looked at Iri.

“You were inside the controlled cycle?”

“Yes.”

“You filed a personal-interface adaptation event.”

“Yes.”

“Does that make you support delay?”

Iri considered the question long enough that Ollie became aware of how much everyone else wanted a fast answer.

“No.”

The housing coordinator relaxed too early.

Iri continued.

“It makes me support keeping that report class in the stop logic.”

The coordinator’s relaxation disappeared.

“Even without fixed escalation?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because you are asking people to live there, not prove a sensor wrong.”

The room went quiet.

Ollie looked at Iri.

They did not look back.

The chair said, “Define what you mean operationally.”

Iri did.

No speech about bodies and buildings.

Three same-class reports inside a short window triggered review even without fixed threshold movement.

One severe report triggered immediate local pause.

No requirement that residents produce medical data to make a report count.

The safety engineer asked two questions.

The service planner asked whether the threshold could be handled with available staff.

The answer was yes if occupancy stayed below the first density cap.

The housing coordinator said, “That threshold will create pauses.”

Iri said, “Then it creates pauses.”

“You are not the one moving households.”

“No. I am one of the people servicing the spaces they move into.”

Neither side won.

Ollie was beginning to understand that nobody was going to let him leave with a clean answer.

Then the resident representative from Ch15 asked him the question he had been hoping would not arrive.

“You gave us what the model does not prove. Fine. Do you personally support opening under the current restricted profile?”

Ollie looked at Mara.

She did not help.

Of course.

The chair said, “You may answer as a Youth Cohort participant. The board is not transferring authority to you.”

That made it better.

And worse.

Ollie looked at the proposed profile on the wall.

Partial habitation.
First residential sector only.
Density cap below half planned occupancy.
No simultaneous use of the disputed partition sequence.
Service route substitutions limited to reviewed combinations.
Enhanced fixed monitoring.
Resident and personal-interface reports visible to the same review rail as sensor events.
Staged move-in.
Lower stop threshold.

He knew every line.

He also knew how much relief he had felt when the second cycle stayed stable.

That was the dangerous part.

Relief made a model feel more complete than it was.

He could say delay.

Nobody would call him cowardly.

Mara probably would not even look disappointed.

He could say the data did not decide.

That was true.

It would also be an evasion.

The representative waited.

Ollie stood again.

“Yes,” he said.

The word sounded too simple.

He continued before anyone could make it larger than he meant.

“Under this profile. With the density cap. With the sequence restriction. With Iri’s report threshold kept in the stop conditions. And with staged occupancy, not unrestricted opening.”

The safety engineer said, “Why yes?”

Because the model worked.

Wrong answer.

“Because the disputed lag has not reproduced when the overlap is absent, including in the messier controlled cycle. That removes one reason I would have used to delay this specific profile.”

“One reason,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“What remains unresolved?”

“The early reports. Root cause. Untested combinations. Whether the 3.2-second follow belongs to the same mechanism or only the normal settling range.”

The resident representative said, “And you still say yes.”

Ollie looked at Lea’s household.

Then at the parent who wanted delay.

“Yes.”

This time he let the word stay.

He sat.

His name had not changed on the wall.

The sentence had.

* * *

The board took twenty-three minutes in a closed decision block.

Nobody was allowed to remain except voting members and required safety staff.

Ollie went to the corridor with the residents, apprentices and people whose schedules were being decided by a room they could no longer hear.

Ren immediately bought food from a service hatch.

“You are eating?” Ollie asked.

“I prepared emotionally.”

“With food?”

“Yes.”

“That is not preparation.”

“It is now.”

Lea walked past them with her older household member.

She stopped.

“You said yes.”

Ollie looked at her.

“You were there.”

“I am confirming.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

The parent from the other household was standing close enough to hear.

She said, “Not good for everyone.”

Lea turned.

“I know.”

The parent looked surprised.

Then tired.

“If it opens, I am waiting three days before we move.”

Lea nodded.

“We will not.”

“Also know.”

They did not become friends.

They went back to their own people.

Ollie sat on the floor because every seat was taken.

Iri leaned against the wall beside him.

“You wanted them to agree,” they said.

“No.”

“You made the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where reality has violated formatting.”

“That is not a face.”

“It is one of your main faces.”

Ren, eating, said, “Second most common.”

Ollie looked at both of them.

“What is first?”

Ren thought.

“Someone touched the model.”

Iri nodded.

“Correct.”

Ollie should have been more annoyed.

He was mostly tired.

His device showed three messages from the group.

AOIFE: she has asked all of us this separately

ETHAN: do not answer until the board decides

MAYA: why

ETHAN: because it is not public yet

MAYA: i asked ollie not the board

Ollie typed:

OLLIE: yes under restricted profile

MAYA: THANK YOU

ETHAN: ollie

OLLIE: she asked what i said not what board decided

SAMIR: technically correct

AOIFE: dangerous coalition

He put the device away.

Iri said, “Earth emergency?”

“Normal.”

“That is concerning.”

The door opened.

Everybody stood too quickly.

The chair came out first.

“Decision is posted. We will read the operative conditions in the room.”

Nobody cheered.

Not yet.

They went back inside.

The wall changed.

RING SIX — COMMISSIONING DECISION
LIMITED GO

Ollie felt the word before he read the conditions.

GO.

Then the conditions populated below it.

PARTIAL HABITATION ONLY.
SECTOR R6-A.
INITIAL DENSITY CAP: 42% OF PLANNED OCCUPANCY.
DISPUTED PARTITION OVERLAP PROHIBITED.
ROUTE COMBINATIONS LIMITED TO REVIEWED PROFILE.
ENHANCED FIXED-SYSTEM MONITORING.
RESIDENT / PERSONAL-INTERFACE REPORT CHANNEL ACTIVE.
STOP THRESHOLD: REDUCED.
STAGED MOVE-IN: REVERSIBLE ALLOCATION BLOCKS.
FIRST FORMAL REVIEW: 36 HOURS AFTER INITIAL OCCUPANCY.

The housing coordinator exhaled.

The safety engineer was already reading the stop language.

Mara read all of it twice.

The chair said, “This is not general habitation authorization. It is a staged occupied commissioning state.”

The parent who wanted delay asked, “Can households defer within their allocation block?”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Three days without losing position.”

She nodded.

Lea’s older household member asked, “When can the first household enter?”

The housing coordinator checked the staging schedule.

“Tonight, if already packed and inside the first block.”

The room changed again.

Not into celebration.

Into logistics.

People opened route plans.

A service planner called somebody about the additional night shift.

The school coordinator began cancelling temporary transfers.

One resident representative asked where the report interface would appear on household systems.

Ren whispered, “We have work.”

Iri said, “Yes.”

Ren looked disappointed.

Mara came to Ollie.

He waited for the sentence he had imagined when he first saw his name on a review slide.

Something about good work.

She said, “Your analysis changed the decision.”

Ollie tried not to smile too obviously.

Mara continued.

“It did not make the decision.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

She looked at the wall.

“Now you get the less pleasant part.”

“What?”

“Watching people live inside the conditions you helped define.”

That was not praise.

It was better.

* * *

Move-in staging began before Ollie finished his shift.

The first visible change was administrative.

A boundary display that had read:

COMMISSIONING — TEST OCCUPANCY ONLY

changed to:

OCCUPIED COMMISSIONING — STAGE 1

Under it, in smaller type:

R6-A / RESTRICTED PROFILE

Then the carts arrived.

Not ceremonial carts.

Badly loaded household carts.

Food modules.
Clothing.
School cases.
A folded sleeping surface.
Two plants sealed for transition.
A child’s sport bag hanging off one side.
Someone’s chair.

Ollie recognized the chair.

It was the same one from the Ch15 test.

The service worker pushing it recognized him recognizing it.

“Still legal,” they said.

“Thirty centimeters.”

“Historic thirty centimeters.”

The chair crossed the occupied boundary.

Behind it came Lea’s household.

Not all of them.

The allocation was staged, and one parent was still finishing a late shift in another sector.

The younger child carried a school case and looked at the dead ocean projection wall from the test.

“Does that turn on now?”

A service worker said, “After household setup.”

The child looked personally betrayed by commissioning procedure.

Lea saw Ollie near the diagnostics bay.

She lifted two fingers in a small greeting.

He returned it.

No speech about home.

Her older household member was already arguing about storage sockets.

A service tech redirected a cart because the restricted route profile prohibited the more obvious corridor.

The cart owner complained.

The tech showed the route.

The owner complained more quietly and followed it.

At the diagnostic station, the fixed traces remained inside expected bands.

The unresolved rail remained open.

No one had removed it because the board had said GO.

Iri came up beside Ollie carrying a service pouch.

“Stage one,” they said.

“Yes.”

“You happy?”

Ollie watched another household cross the boundary.

A child ran two steps ahead, was called back, returned, then immediately tried again at walking speed.

“Ask me in thirty-six hours.”

Iri looked at him.

“That was almost sensible.”

“Do not spread it around.”

Ren’s voice came from the corridor.

“Too late.”

Ollie ignored him.

The status line changed again as the first household authenticated inside its assigned residential volume.

OCCUPANCY: 6

Then:

OCCUPANCY: 9

A service cart passed.

A door cycled.

The first correction settled.

The second partition waited exactly as programmed.

People kept moving.

For the first time, Ring Six was not pretending to be lived in.

It was being lived in.
