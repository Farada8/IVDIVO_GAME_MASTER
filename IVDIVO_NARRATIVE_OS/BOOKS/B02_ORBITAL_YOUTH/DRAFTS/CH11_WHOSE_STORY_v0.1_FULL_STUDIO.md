# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 11 — WHOSE STORY

The snack dispenser had rejected the same payment four times and accepted three empty cups.

Aoife recorded it for six minutes.

The machine occupied a shallow wall niche between a laundry return and a public charging bench. Nothing about it deserved six minutes except that everyone using the corridor had an opinion.

A boy in a school jacket slapped the payment panel with the flat of his hand.

“That doesn’t help,” his friend said.

“It knows I’m serious.”

“It knows you’re hitting it.”

The dispenser released another cup.

The boy stared at it.

Aoife shifted the recorder slightly to catch the soft plastic knock as the cup dropped into the tray.

His friend saw the recorder.

“Oh, no.”

Aoife lowered it.

“What?”

“You’re one of them.”

“One of who?”

“The Earth people asking what it feels like to live in space.”

The boy stopped glaring at the machine long enough to look at her.

“Tell them it feels like being robbed by a wall.”

“That I can use.”

“Don’t.”

Aoife switched the recorder off.

The boy looked surprised.

His friend looked less surprised, which Aoife decided was probably worse.

The corridor was busy in the way Vela had taught her to recognize as local rather than dramatic. A laundry queue had formed because one return bank was out of service. Two young maintenance workers ate something from a folded container while standing because every seat was taken. A woman with a toddler was arguing with a route display that kept offering a connection with a six-minute walking interval through a gravity transition the toddler clearly did not intend to respect.

Beyond the opposite wall, a view cut between structures and showed Earth.

Nobody was looking at it.

Aoife had recorded Earth twice that morning and deleted both clips.

The first had looked like an advertisement.

The second had looked like somebody trying very hard not to make an advertisement.

The broken snack dispenser was better.

A girl in a dark service apron came out from behind the food counter across the corridor, crossed to the dispenser and crouched beside it.

“You’re wasting your time,” she told the two boys.

“It took my money.”

“It didn’t take your money.”

“It removed it from my account.”

“It reserved it.”

“That is a very advanced word for took.”

The girl opened a maintenance flap with a borrowed tool that looked slightly too large for the job.

Aoife turned the recorder on again, then stopped herself.

“Can I?” she asked.

The girl glanced up.

“Can you what?”

“Record this.”

“Why?”

Aoife looked at the dispenser.

“It’s been giving people cups instead of food for at least six minutes.”

The girl sat back on her heels.

“You’ve been filming that?”

“Audio.”

“Why?”

“Because everyone cares more about it than the planet.”

The girl looked through the opening toward Earth, then back at the machine.

“Obviously.”

She laughed.

Aoife turned the recorder on.

The girl pointed at it.

“Not my face.”

“It’s audio.”

“Good start.”

She reached into the open flap and pressed something.

The dispenser hummed.

A row of lights reset.

The boy in the school jacket leaned closer.

“Did you fix it?”

“No.”

“What did you do?”

“Made it someone else’s problem.”

A service notice appeared across the payment panel.

OUT OF ORDER — REFUND QUEUED

The boy checked his account.

“Oh.”

The girl closed the flap.

“Congratulations. You survived infrastructure.”

He took the empty cup from the tray.

“I’m keeping this.”

“You paid for it emotionally.”

The boys left.

Aoife stopped recording.

The girl went back toward the counter.

Aoife followed at a distance that she hoped looked casual and suspected did not.

The food point sold late-shift meals, school snacks, electrolyte packets, two kinds of coffee and one thing labelled simply HOT SOUP because apparently the people who designed orbital cities eventually ran out of imagination.

The girl retied the strap of her apron and took a tray from a warming unit.

“You’re following me.”

“I am walking in the same direction.”

“The direction ends at my work counter.”

“That weakens my defence.”

The girl looked at her again.

Aoife held up the recorder, off.

“I’m making a piece about people our age living here.”

“Why?”

“Because most of the pieces I’ve seen are about living in space.”

“That is where we live.”

“Yes.”

“So you’re fixing the preposition.”

Aoife considered that.

“Maybe.”

The girl slid the tray into a rack.

“You’re going to ask me what I miss about Earth.”

“No.”

“What the view means to me.”

“No.”

“How gravity affects my identity.”

Aoife put the recorder on the counter between them.

“Do you know why the laundry queue is twenty people long?”

The girl stopped.

Aoife nodded toward the return bank.

“One side is down.”

“That’s not why.”

“Good.”

“The allocation block moved two households out of temporary units this morning, so everyone tried to do bedding before shift change. Then Return Three died.”

Aoife picked up the recorder.

“Can I record that?”

The girl frowned at her.

“You really want the laundry?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Aoife almost said because boring was the story.

The phrase arrived polished enough that she distrusted it immediately.

“Because it changed twenty people’s evening.”

The girl looked toward the queue.

A teenager near the back had sat on a bag of clothes and was doing homework on one knee.

Two people ahead of him were arguing over whether there was time to leave and come back before a rehearsal slot.

The girl nodded once.

“Fine.”

Aoife turned the recorder on.

“What should I call you?”

The girl hesitated.

“Sela.”

Aoife marked it privately.

SELA — WORKING SOURCE / NAME CHECK BEFORE USE

“What do you want me not to ask?” Aoife said.

Sela laughed again, but more quietly.

“That is a dangerous question.”

“Because?”

“Now I have to think of everything.”

“Then don’t. Tell me if I hit something.”

Sela started handing food packets to a woman in a transit uniform.

“Are you keeping the part where I’m working?”

“If it stays useful.”

“That sounded ominous.”

“It means I don’t know what the piece is yet.”

“That is better than pretending.”

Aoife recorded the next twenty minutes in pieces.

Not an interview.

A shift.

Sela complaining that the late food packets had changed supplier and now opened from the wrong corner.

A customer asking whether the coffee machine had been cleaned and Sela saying, “Legally, yes.”

A friend arriving with a music file and trying to persuade her to listen to a new mix while Sela was counting meal tokens.

A message from Sela’s training group asking whether she could swap a morning block because one of the instructors had been rerouted to Ring Six commissioning support.

Sela replying no because she was already working before class.

A route alert moving her return transfer by nine minutes.

“That’s the third one this week,” Sela said.

Aoife glanced at the recorder.

“You want that on?”

Sela shrugged.

“Everybody knows the routes are bad.”

“Bad compared to what?”

Sela gave her a look.

“See? That’s the Earth question.”

Aoife waited.

Sela tapped the route notice away.

“Bad compared to last month.”

That was better.

Aoife left the silence after it.

Sela finished the shift at sixteen forty and vanished into the staff side long enough to change out of the apron. She returned wearing a loose jacket with one sleeve clipped shorter and carrying a case no bigger than her forearm.

Aoife pointed at it.

“Music?”

“Sound kit.”

“For what?”

“Nothing you’re recording today.”

Aoife put both hands up.

Sela smiled.

“Good. You can learn.”

They walked toward a radial transfer.

Aoife recorded the station around them instead.

A public notice about a classroom move.

Someone swearing when the laundry return announced another delay.

A child asking whether Ring Six had a better climbing room than Ring Four.

A parent saying nobody knew because Ring Six was not open yet.

A couple arguing over whose route home was worse if they stayed another hour together.

Sela heard that and said, “Put that in.”

“Why?”

“Because nobody on Earth understands that dating is a transport problem.”

Aoife smiled.

“Now who’s framing?”

Sela thought about it.

“Fine. Don’t put that sentence in.”

“Too late. I remember things.”

“That seems unethical.”

“Memory remains under-regulated.”

The transfer arrived.

Sela stepped into the higher-gravity side of the car without looking at the floor markers. Aoife followed and had to adjust half a step when the car settled.

Sela noticed.

She did not comment.

Aoife appreciated that enough to almost comment on it herself.

She didn’t.

At the next interchange, Sela stopped beside a narrow service corridor.

“This is where I leave you.”

Aoife looked past her.

The corridor carried a maintenance-side access stripe and a public-route marker farther down.

“Can I come?”

“Maybe next time.”

“What changes next time?”

Sela shifted the sound case to her other hand.

“You ask before following me to work.”

Aoife opened her mouth.

Closed it.

“That seems fair.”

Sela started away, then turned back.

“If you actually want the commissioning side, not the tour version, I can show you part of my route tomorrow.”

Aoife lifted the recorder slightly.

“On record?”

“Some of it.”

“What part isn’t?”

Sela’s expression changed—not much, but enough.

“I’ll tell you before we start.”

Then she left.

Aoife watched the service door close behind her.

Earth filled the end of the public corridor through a curved window.

Aoife recorded the laundry delay announcement instead.

* * *

The room Sela chose the next day was not private.

That mattered.

It was a shared break space beside a service junction, with three tables, a water unit, two wall lockers and a door that opened every few minutes because people actually used it.

Aoife liked it immediately.

Sela did not.

“Not that table.”

“Why?”

“Because the vent makes everything sound like you recorded inside a pipe.”

Aoife moved.

Sela chose the table farthest from the vent and set her sound case under the chair.

There was a small environmental panel in the wall beside her shoulder.

Sela touched it.

The panel woke.

A short two-tone service sound played.

The airflow changed almost imperceptibly.

Aoife heard it because she was listening for room tone.

She looked at the panel.

Sela saw her look.

“That’s the thing.”

Aoife put the recorder on the table but did not turn it on.

“What thing?”

“The boundary.”

“Okay.”

Sela sat.

“You can say the room was adjusted.”

Aoife waited.

“Not why.”

“Okay.”

“Don’t show the panel.”

“I’m doing audio.”

“You have a camera.”

“It’s in my bag.”

“Keep it there.”

Aoife nodded.

Sela continued.

“Don’t name the setting. Don’t connect it to me as a category. Don’t make it the headline. And if you want to use this part separately later, you ask again.”

Aoife did not reach for the recorder.

“Who is the audience for this version?” she asked.

Sela frowned.

“You tell me.”

“Current plan is a youth documentary sequence. Public. Earth and orbital audiences. Edited, not live. No standalone profile of you unless we separately agree. You see your excerpt before it goes in.”

“Can I kill a line?”

“Yes.”

“Even if you like it?”

“If it’s about you and inside what we agreed, yes.”

Sela leaned back.

“What if it’s about somebody else?”

“Then you can object. You don’t automatically own it.”

“That sounds less generous.”

“It is more accurate.”

Sela looked at her for a second, then nodded.

“Fine.”

Aoife pointed to the recorder.

“I want the boundary itself on record so neither of us has to remember the wording later.”

Sela considered that too.

“Do it.”

Aoife turned the recorder on.

The room tone settled around the small red indicator.

“Context agreement,” Aoife said. “Public edited youth documentary sequence. Sela reviews her excerpt before use. I can mention that the room environment was adjusted. I do not state why, do not name or show the profile or setting, and do not connect the adjustment to a body, medical or identity category. No standalone reuse of this material without asking again. Camera stays off in this room.”

She stopped.

“Anything wrong?”

Sela looked toward the wall panel.

“Say I can ask you to remove something in my excerpt before release.”

Aoife added it.

Then she played the agreement back.

Sela listened to her own silence between the terms.

“Good.”

Aoife marked the clip.

S1 CONTEXT — DO NOT CUT AWAY FROM TERMS

Then she asked, “Ready?”

Sela pointed at the recorder.

“It’s already on.”

“Yes.”

“That was sneaky.”

“The terms are the first recorded thing.”

Sela considered whether to be annoyed.

Apparently decided against it.

“Fine. Ask me something boring.”

Aoife looked at the sound case under the chair.

“Why are you carrying that to work?”

“That isn’t about Ring Six.”

“Exactly.”

Sela smiled despite herself.

The sound kit belonged to a small group that made live mixes for school performances and youth events. Sela was not the main performer. She preferred the part where everyone complained after the set that one channel had been too loud.

“That sounds terrible,” Aoife said.

“It’s excellent. Everyone is wrong in a different way.”

“What are you?”

“Also wrong. But with files.”

They talked about the food counter.

About a training block Sela had nearly missed twice because temporary transfer patterns had changed after Ring Six delays.

About a friend who had stopped staying late at rehearsals because the only reliable route home added twenty-eight minutes.

About the strange social mathematics of whether a place counted as “close” when it was physically near and operationally three transfers away.

Aoife asked what Ring Six meant to her.

Sela’s face closed slightly.

Aoife almost hated the question as soon as she heard it.

There it was.

The big meaning question.

Sela saved her from it.

“It means one less transfer between training and work if the service corridor opens the way they said.”

Aoife nodded.

“Better.”

“What?”

“My question was worse than your answer.”

“That happens a lot?”

“Often enough.”

Sela shifted in the chair.

The wall panel made the same soft two-tone sound again.

Aoife’s recorder caught it.

Neither of them reacted.

“What do people from Earth get wrong about the delay?” Aoife asked.

“That everybody wants the same thing.”

“How?”

Sela thought.

“My friend Taren wants them to open anything that cuts his commute. His mother wants another month of testing because she doesn’t trust the mixed-environment controls. My manager wants the service corridor because she is paying people to arrive late. One of my instructors wants the training rooms because our schedule is a mess. I want all of those things depending on which hour you ask.”

Aoife let that sit.

It was good.

Not because it explained policy.

Because Sela sounded irritated with herself.

“What about you right now?”

“Right now I want to get from this room to rehearsal without the route changing again.”

“That I believe.”

Sela glanced at the recorder.

“You can keep the other answer too.”

“Generous.”

“I contain multitudes.”

“Don’t make me quote that.”

“I’ll veto it.”

Aoife laughed.

Her wrist display flashed once.

ETHAN: rough thought — if you’re building around ordinary life, maybe cut between the same route at work / school / night instead of grouping by person

Aoife read it.

Then put her wrist down.

Sela raised an eyebrow.

“Problem?”

“Ethan.”

“That does not answer the question.”

“It does to several people.”

“What did he say?”

Aoife looked at the message again.

It was good.

That was becoming a recurring irritation.

“An edit idea.”

“You asked?”

“No.”

“Are you going to use it?”

Aoife locked the display.

“I am going to finish this conversation first.”

Sela nodded as if that was the correct answer to a different question.

The door opened.

Two workers came in arguing about a missing tool authorization. They filled water containers, barely looking at Aoife or the recorder.

One of them tapped the environmental panel on the way out.

Sela said, “Leave it.”

The worker stopped.

“Sorry.”

He put the setting back.

The same two-tone service sound played.

“Thanks,” Sela said.

The door closed.

Aoife had a reflexive question ready.

She did not ask it.

Sela noticed that too.

Her shoulders loosened a little.

“Okay,” she said. “You can come farther tomorrow.”

Aoife looked at her.

“Where?”

“Service side. Not commissioning floor. I’m not getting you arrested for content.”

“Professional standards.”

“Self-preservation.”

Aoife marked the moment.

Not because access had increased.

Because the reason had.

* * *

The strongest line came after Aoife turned the recorder off.

Of course it did.

They were leaving the break room. Sela had reviewed the clips they had made, removed one answer because it sounded “like I’m applying to represent the entire station,” and approved the rest for a rough excerpt.

Aoife packed the recorder.

The room reset after they stood.

The two-tone service sound played again.

Sela waited for the door to open.

Aoife asked, without thinking, “Does that route really only add forty minutes?”

Sela’s hand stopped on the strap of her sound case.

The recorder was off.

Aoife knew it was off because she had checked twice.

Sela looked down the empty corridor.

“For everyone else, maybe.”

Aoife waited.

Sela rubbed one thumb along the edge of the case.

“For me, one of the transfers means I spend the next hour pretending it didn’t cost me anything.”

There it was.

Clean.

Human.

Everything the piece had been missing.

Aoife felt the edit build itself around the sentence.

Laundry delay.

Food shift.

Three transfers.

Forty minutes on the public schedule.

Then that.

She hated how good it was.

Sela looked at her.

Aoife did not reach for the recorder.

“Okay,” she said.

Sela’s expression hardened.

“That’s all?”

“What do you want me to say?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then I’m not going to manufacture something.”

Sela looked away.

The door opened.

They stepped into the corridor.

After five metres Sela said, “That wasn’t for the piece.”

“I know.”

Aoife wished she had another answer because that one sounded too small.

She did not improve it.

They separated at the interchange.

Aoife went home with the best line she had heard all week and no recording of it.

That should have made the edit easier.

It did not.

---

At 22:41 Aoife had thirty-seven minutes of usable sound and a sequence that felt twelve seconds too short.

She knew exactly where the twelve seconds belonged.

That was the problem.

Her temporary room was narrow enough that the work surface folded over part of the bed. She had learned to edit with one knee against the wall and her feet under a chair that technically belonged to the host household.

On the screen, the sequence began with the snack dispenser releasing an empty cup.

Then:

Sela: It means one less transfer between training and work if the service corridor opens the way they said.

Laundry return warning.

A teenager complaining about a rehearsal slot.

Food packets opening from the wrong corner.

A route change.

Sela laughing about the sound group.

The brief environmental tone was there under two clips.

Aoife barely noticed it.

It belonged to the room like ventilation.

She trimmed a pause.

Restored it.

Trimmed a different one.

The sequence still wanted the line.

For me, one of the transfers means I spend the next hour pretending it didn’t cost me anything.

Aoife could hear it perfectly.

She could paraphrase.

She could build toward it without quoting.

She could ask Sela tomorrow if she would say something similar on record.

She could tell herself that the idea had been present elsewhere in the interview.

She did none of those things.

Instead she let the route chime run four seconds longer than she normally would have.

Then she cut to the broken snack dispenser.

The transition was worse.

She left it.

Ethan called at 22:58.

Aoife considered not answering.

Then she remembered the coffee in Dublin and answered anyway.

His face appeared against a background she did not recognize. Adult-track orientation room, maybe. Too clean. Too much glass.

“You’re working,” he said.

“You called me.”

“You answered.”

“Still evaluating that decision.”

He smiled.

“What do you have?”

Aoife hesitated.

Then sent him the rough sequence.

“Listen. Don’t reorganize my life.”

“I was going to say audio.”

“Same warning applies.”

He put one earbud in.

Aoife watched him listen.

That was always unpleasant.

Not because he reacted badly.

Because he reacted visibly before he decided what to say, and Aoife could usually see the argument arriving.

At the snack-dispenser opening he smiled.

At the laundry queue he stopped smiling.

At Sela’s commute answer he leaned closer to the screen.

Then the route chime ran four seconds too long.

His eyebrows moved.

There.

He finished the clip.

“The middle is strong.”

Aoife waited.

“The transition after the route alert is missing something.”

“Yes.”

“You could bridge it with—”

“There is a reason it is missing.”

Ethan stopped.

Aoife said nothing else.

He looked at the timeline she had shared.

Then at her.

“Source boundary?”

“Yes.”

“Okay.”

That was all.

Aoife had prepared herself to defend it.

The unused defence sat awkwardly in her mouth.

Ethan pointed at the earlier part of the timeline instead.

“The snack machine is good.”

“I know.”

“You sound offended.”

“I spent six minutes recording it.”

“That is commitment.”

“Professional commitment.”

His smile changed.

Book One. Virginia. Evidence cards. Too much coffee and not enough sleep.

Aoife smiled back before she could decide whether to.

Ethan said, “I still think the route structure could work.”

“Of course you do.”

“But not if it breaks the source boundary.”

“Thank you for your sacrifice.”

“It’s severe.”

“Thoughts and prayers.”

For a few seconds neither of them said anything.

It was easy.

Aoife noticed that too.

Then Ethan’s wrist flashed with another message.

His eyes dropped.

Adult-track something.

He turned the display away slightly—not secretively enough to count as hiding it, just enough that Aoife could not read.

The ease changed shape.

She did not ask.

Not tonight.

“I’m sending Sela the cleaned excerpt,” Aoife said.

“Now?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want me to—”

“No.”

He laughed.

“Right.”

This time he did not finish the sentence.

That helped.

They ended the call two minutes later.

Aoife sent Sela the excerpt with the agreed terms attached above it.

Not hidden in a link.

Not summarized.

The actual terms.

Sela responded eleven minutes later.

SELA: remove 04:12–04:18

Aoife checked.

It was a line about her manager losing staff time to route changes.

AOIFE: because it identifies the work point?

SELA: no because she’ll complain i made her sound reasonable

Aoife laughed.

AOIFE: removed

A second message arrived.

SELA: rest is fine

Then:

SELA: snack machine deserves more time

Aoife moved the machine from six seconds to eight.

Not because Sela had editorial authority over the whole piece.

Because she was right.

Aoife exported the approved excerpt.

SOURCE S1 — CLEANED / APPROVED / CONTEXT TERMS ATTACHED

She watched the file complete.

Everything they had agreed to remove was gone.

The camera had never come out.

The profile had never been named.

The setting had never been shown.

The off-recorder line was nowhere in the sequence.

The source had approved the excerpt herself.

Aoife leaned back against the wall.

For once, the clean version did not feel like the compromised version.

It felt finished.

Her device buzzed.

OLLIE: question when you’re awake

Aoife looked at the time.

AOIFE: dangerously subjective

OLLIE: in the interviews / room audio — did people use the same words before environmental changes? pressure / dry / slow / wrong air / anything like that

Aoife read the message twice.

Not because the question was difficult.

Because it made her hear the interviews differently.

Sela talking about routes.

The break-room door.

The soft environmental tone.

Other voices from the corridor.

Aoife opened her notes, not the finished edit.

AOIFE: maybe. not enough to say same thing. i can check exact wording tomorrow

Three dots appeared.

OLLIE: good. exact wording only

Aoife smiled.

Of course.

She closed the approved excerpt and left it alone.

The room around her hummed with the host household’s night setting.

Somewhere beyond the wall, a transfer passed through a lower band and changed pitch as it went.

Aoife heard it.

Did not record it.
