# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 29 — SEGMENTS

The first version of the plan opened Ring Six.

The second version did not.

The third opened four doors and started an argument about laundry.

Ollie preferred the third.

He was not in Ring Six when they built it.

That part still mattered.

His access status on the review board remained exactly what it had been the day before:

REVIEW HOLD — NO UNSUPERVISED COMMISSIONING ACCESS.

The planning room was two rings away, which meant the whole future residential sector existed for Ollie as a layered schematic, a set of operating profiles and a live consequence wall he was not allowed to walk into and verify for himself.

He disliked that.

He also understood it.

Mostly.

Mara stood at the front of the room beside a display split into six irregular blocks.

“Stop calling them blocks,” Nadia said.

“I have not called them blocks.”

“You were about to.”

Mara looked at her.

Nadia pointed at the display.

“Those shapes are going to become people’s homes. If the labels make them look interchangeable, everyone starts thinking the consequences are interchangeable.”

Mara changed the heading.

SEGMENT PROFILES.

“Better?”

“Less wrong.”

Ollie opened his own copy.

The six segments were not equal in anything except the fact that architects had once put numbers next to them.

Segment One had the cleanest service separation and the worst late-cycle school transfer.

Segment Two had more forgiving residential geometry but shared a delivery crossing with a maintenance route that became awkward under the full-recovery interlock.

Segment Three could tolerate the deeper recovery timing with almost no loss of ordinary residential movement, provided guest traffic stayed below a defined evening density.

Segment Four was the reason everyone was still in the room.

Its service return, pressure correction and common-route timing could reproduce the state window Ch27 had been built to test.

Not always.

Not under every load.

Enough.

Segment Five depended on Four for one of its efficient service paths.

Segment Six could operate around that dependency, but only by turning a normal eight-minute school route into twenty-three minutes and putting two early-shift worker groups through a transfer that did not yet have enough capacity.

Ollie stared at the network.

“Segments, not people,” he said.

Nadia looked over.

“That sounded better yesterday.”

“It is still true.”

“It is technically true.”

Mara said, “Which is the kind we need first.”

Nadia folded her arms.

“And then?”

“Then we stop pretending technical truth finishes the job.”

Nobody argued with that.

Ollie expanded Segment Three.

“Three can support the A-profile interlock without changing the common residential route.”

Mara said, “Under what occupancy?”

He gave the bounded number.

“Guests?”

“Limited after twenty-one hundred until we have the second validation set.”

“Deliveries?”

“Staged outside the resident peak.”

“School movement?”

“Unaffected inside Three. External transfer still depends on One.”

Nadia said, “So a household can be compatible with the room and incompatible with Tuesday morning.”

Ollie looked at her.

“Yes.”

“Good. Put that on the wall.”

He did.

ROOM FIT ≠ LIFE FIT.

He considered the wording.

Deleted it.

Replaced it with:

HOUSING COMPATIBILITY REQUIRES ROOM + ROUTE + SERVICE ACCESS.

Nadia nodded.

“That one sounds like you.”

“Thank you.”

“It was not praise.”

“I know.”

He stopped.

Nadia noticed.

“Did you just prevent yourself from saying something?”

“No.”

Mara said, “Continue.”

They continued.

Segment One: conditionally certifiable.

Segment Two: certifiable with service-window restrictions.

Segment Three: certifiable with evening-density and delivery limits.

Segment Four: hold.

Segment Five: hold unless the service path was rerouted through the reduced-capacity profile.

Segment Six: technically possible, socially expensive.

That last phrase did not survive Mara.

“Define expensive.”

Ollie pulled the consequences into view.

“Twenty-three-minute school transfer for one route group. Early-shift crowding at Transfer C. One maintenance window displaced. Guest access restrictions after twenty hundred. Delivery staging adds staff time.”

“Better.”

Nadia said, “And if we do not open Six?”

Ollie brought up the waitlist count.

The number sat there.

Not enormous.

Large enough.

Nadia looked at it for a few seconds.

“Keep Six in the candidate set.”

Mara said, “With restrictions visible.”

“Obviously.”

“Nothing is obvious after Chapter Twenty.”

Ollie looked up.

Mara had said it without drama.

She was already reading the next line.

He returned to the model.

The planning room spent another hour turning the idea of partial reopening into something people could actually inhabit.

A service route that looked redundant on the map turned out to be the only practical path for late food deliveries once the common corridor was under reduced-density operation.

A segment with excellent environmental margins failed housing review because the available room cluster separated an accessibility-dependent adult from the support route they used every morning.

One proposed guest restriction was technically harmless and socially absurd because it would have made a sixteen-year-old resident sign out of their own home to visit a friend two doors away after the cutoff.

Nadia killed that one in twelve seconds.

“Try again.”

Ollie did.

By the time they stopped for food, Ring Six had become smaller.

That was the first thing that made it believable.

Not smaller physically.

Smaller as a promise.

Three segments likely.

A fourth possible under restricted service routing.

Two closed.

No one had said “successful opening.”

No one had said “failure” either.

Mara sent the candidate profile into safety review.

Nadia sent the same profile into housing allocation.

Ollie watched both status lines leave the room.

His name was attached under MODEL SUPPORT.

Not DECISION.

Not AUTHORITY.

He was surprised by how little that bothered him.

* * *

Housing allocation used the same wall and changed everything on it.

The safe segments became rooms.

The rooms became households.

The households became conflicts.

Nadia stood in front of the first provisional list.

“No first-come rule.”

A resident representative said, “Why not?”

“Because the first person in the queue may not fit the first safe room.”

“Then next compatible.”

“Until accessibility disappears behind speed?”

“That is not what I said.”

“No. It is what the shortcut does.”

Another representative asked, “Need first?”

Nadia said, “Define need.”

Nobody answered quickly.

Ollie opened the compatibility layer.

Household size.

Environmental range.

Route access.

School location.

Shift location.

Accessibility.

Current temporary burden.

Wait time.

He could sort it.

Not perfectly.

That had never stopped anyone from sorting anything.

He built a temporary ordering on his private panel.

Hard constraints first.

Then burden.

Then wait time.

Then route efficiency.

It was not a score.

Technically.

He stared at the list.

The first seven households looked reasonable.

That was how trouble usually began.

Ollie closed the panel.

Nadia saw him do it.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“That looked like something.”

“I was sorting compatibility.”

“For what?”

“To see whether there was an obvious order.”

“Was there?”

“Yes.”

Nadia waited.

Ollie corrected himself.

“There was an obvious order after I decided what mattered first.”

“Good.”

“You could sound less pleased.”

“I have had a long week.”

He reopened the compatibility matrix without the rank column.

Nadia moved one household upward manually.

Ollie frowned.

“Why?”

“Current placement ends in nine days.”

“That is in the burden field.”

“Not enough.”

“Why not?”

“Because the number says nine days. It does not say the extension requires moving the household to another temporary unit before they move here.”

Ollie opened the case note.

There it was.

A second move.

School route reset.

Stored possessions shifted again.

He added a visible flag.

Another household moved down because the only compatible first-phase room placed them across a transfer closure from a daily care obligation.

A third could move immediately but would lose the work-route advantage that had made Ring Six attractive to them in the first place.

One resident wanted that trade anyway.

Another did not.

The list stopped looking like a list.

That helped.

A housing officer joined remotely and asked whether the current safe profile could absorb two more people if one family accepted split rooms.

Nadia said, “Physically, yes.”

Ollie checked the service model.

“Operationally, yes.”

The officer said, “Then why is the household still red?”

Nadia answered before Ollie could.

“Because they asked not to be split.”

The officer nodded.

“Keep red.”

Ollie looked at the matrix.

He had almost moved them.

Nobody had stopped him because nobody had seen him think it.

He deleted the hidden sort rule entirely.

That felt more important than it should have.

* * *

Lea arrived after school with her bag still over one shoulder.

She did not sit.

Her household block was already open on the wall.

Two members compatible with the first phase.

Two not.

One of the incompatible routes depended on a closed segment.

The other required an environmental profile available only in a cluster that would not open yet.

Housing had marked three possibilities.

WAIT TOGETHER.

TEMPORARY SPLIT / FIRST-PHASE PARTIAL MOVE.

REVIEW IF FOURTH SEGMENT CLEARS.

Lea read all three.

Then read them again.

“So we are eligible,” she said.

Nadia said, “Partly.”

“That is a disgusting word.”

“Yes.”

“My mother can move.”

“Yes.”

“My brother can move.”

“Yes.”

“And I stay where we are.”

“If your household chooses the partial move.”

“Which means we split.”

“Yes.”

Lea dropped her bag onto the chair.

It bounced once and fell sideways.

Ollie reached automatically to catch it, missed, and let it stay on the floor.

Lea looked at him.

“You said segments, not people.”

“Yes.”

“This is people.”

“Yes.”

She looked back at the wall.

“Can Four open?”

Mara said, “Not on current evidence.”

“When?”

“Unknown.”

Lea laughed once.

No humor in it.

“Excellent.”

Nadia said, “Your household can also wait together. No one is forcing the split.”

“That is supposed to help?”

“No. It is supposed to be true.”

Lea rubbed both hands over her face.

For a few seconds she looked younger than she usually allowed herself to look.

Then she pointed at another block.

“What about them?”

Nadia opened it.

“Full household fit. School route fit. One work-route penalty, but they accepted it.”

“They go?”

“If final review holds, yes.”

“And them?”

Another block.

“Wait.”

“Why?”

“Accessibility route crosses Five.”

Lea moved to the next.

For five minutes she went through other households.

In.

Wait.

Maybe.

Partial.

Not because she had calmed down.

Because she wanted to know who the plan was hurting if it was not her.

Ollie watched her reach the end of the first-phase list.

Lea stared at the number of households that could move.

Then at her own split block.

“This is awful.”

Nadia said nothing.

Lea pointed at the first-phase total.

“But you are still doing it.”

Nadia said, “If safety approves the profile.”

Lea looked at Ollie.

He had the stupid impulse to explain why the profile was technically defensible.

She did not need that.

So he waited.

Lea said, “Do not close it because we do not fit.”

Ollie blinked.

“I cannot close or open it.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes.”

“I hate this plan.”

“Yes.”

“And if you find a way to put us ahead because I helped you, I will hate you too.”

“That was not—”

He stopped.

It had been, a little.

Not a plan.

A want.

He had wanted the work to return something to someone he knew.

Lea picked up her bag.

The strap had twisted under the chair leg.

She pulled once, harder than necessary.

It did not come free.

Ollie bent down, lifted the chair, and freed it.

Lea took the bag.

“Thanks.”

That was all.

At the door she stopped.

“When does the final list happen?”

Nadia gave her the review window.

Lea nodded and left.

Nobody called the meeting successful.

The first-phase map stayed on the wall.

Some segments open.

Some closed.

Some households moving.

Some waiting.

Ollie looked at the distribution without trying to sort it.

Across the room, Aoife had been helping a resident find the correct temporary-route form on a public terminal.

Her recorder was clipped inside her bag.

Not in her hand.

When the resident left, Aoife walked over.

“Are they doing move prep tomorrow?”

Nadia said, “For the households likely to clear first phase. And support for people still waiting.”

Aoife nodded.

“Can I help?”

Nadia looked at the bag.

Aoife followed her eyes.

“I am not filming.”

“Then yes.”

Aoife took out her device.

For one second Ollie thought she was going to record anyway.

She opened her schedule.

“Where?”

Nadia sent the location.

Aoife put the device away.

Ollie looked back at Ring Six.

The plan had become smaller again.

Three safe segments.

One conditional.

Two closed.

No clean victory.

Enough rooms that tomorrow would be different for some people.

Not enough that everyone got to call it home.
