# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 27 — SMALL TEST

The adult safety team removed two thirds of Ollie’s protocol and improved it.

He hated that for almost nine minutes.

The test segment was not in Ring Six.

It was not residential.

Nobody was moving in.

Nobody had brought furniture, food, children, bags, arguments about shelves or anything else capable of becoming a moral reason to keep going after a stop condition.

It was an old commissioning loop beside a service-training bay, built around three connected environmental volumes that could be isolated from the rest of Vela.

The walls were ugly.

The doors were ugly.

The floor had three generations of repair patches.

Ollie trusted it immediately.

Mara stood beside the adult test lead while the final protocol scrolled on the wall.

Ollie read the parts that used to belong to him.

RETURN CORRECTION — CONTROLLED LOAD.

SERVICE TRANSITION — SIMULATED DEMAND CHANGE.

MOVEMENT-ORDER STATE — HELD / REVERSED / RESUMED.

FIXED DISPLAY — STANDARD TIMING.

STOP CONDITION — FIRST CROSS-CHANNEL DEVIATION BEYOND PRE-REGISTERED BAND.

Then the parts the adult team had changed.

No attempt to recreate Ring Six occupancy density.

No uncontrolled participant improvisation.

No resident recruitment.

No medical or support-system ingestion.

No attempt to produce a sustained oscillation.

The goal was smaller.

Can the proposed ordering create one measurable coupled deviation before the system stabilizes?

Ollie looked at the line twice.

One measurable deviation.

Not the whole failure.

Not proof that every earlier report meant the same thing.

Not a victory.

Mara noticed where he had stopped reading.

“You object?”

“No.”

“That sounded expensive.”

“I had more stages.”

“We removed them.”

“I noticed.”

“Why?”

“Because you can answer the first question without running the second.”

Mara nodded.

Ollie disliked the answer because it was his kind of answer.

The test lead, Dr. Saye’s counterpart from environmental safety, pointed toward a side console.

“Ollie.”

“Yes.”

“That station is yours.”

It had one screen.

He looked at it.

One.

“Timing comparison only,” the test lead said. “You see pre-registered event markers, the fixed display and the derived timing channel. You do not see full service command. You do not see participant identities. You do not change the test.”

“I know.”

“Say what you do if your model is wrong.”

“Mark the result.”

“And?”

“Do not improve the test while it is running.”

“And?”

“Do not ask for another channel.”

The test lead looked at Mara.

Mara said, “He has been trained by disappointment.”

Ollie sat down.

His screen opened.

Four lines.

RETURN CORRECTION.

SERVICE TRANSITION.

MOVEMENT ORDER.

FIXED RESPONSE.

Nothing else.

He had never had less data for something he cared about this much.

* * *

Iri’s job was to move a service cart.

That was all.

They were offended.

“This is not even a real cart.”

“It is a real cart,” Ollie said.

“It has ballast.”

“Real carts also have ballast.”

“This has fake ballast.”

“Mass does not know.”

Iri looked at him.

“That sentence explains too much about you.”

The cart contained sealed water blocks and three inert equipment cases.

Its path crossed the test transition exactly once.

Iri would push it to the marked hold point, stop when the adult controller signaled, reverse into a side pocket, wait, then resume after the participant group crossed.

The participant group consisted of four trained safety staff.

Not residents.

Not teenagers.

Not anyone whose housing depended on the result.

Two had ordinary environmental comfort patches approved for test use. The patches did not expose body data. They returned only a coarse event flag if the immediate air boundary changed outside the pre-registered local band.

One participant had no patch.

One carried a handheld environmental strip.

Iri’s own regulator remained private.

The test did not need it.

Ollie had asked whether that was intentional.

The test lead had said yes.

He had liked that answer more than he expected.

Now Iri stood beside the fake-real cart wearing a service vest and looking bored.

“You wanted operational reality,” Mara said.

Iri tapped the cart handle.

“This is operational theater.”

“The water blocks weigh the same as loaded service cases.”

“They do not complain.”

“Neither do service cases.”

“You have met different service cases.”

The test lead looked up.

“Are we ready?”

Iri stopped talking.

That answered the question.

Ollie checked his screen.

All four lines rested inside the baseline band.

The first run was designed to fail to reproduce anything.

Matched environmental load.

Return correction.

Service transition.

Movement forward only.

No hold.

No reversal.

No crossed-before-display state.

If Ollie’s hypothesis was useful, nothing interesting should happen.

The test started.

A controlled thermal/humidity load changed in Volume A.

The local environmental controller corrected.

Return correction became active.

The service simulator changed demand.

Iri moved the cart through.

Four adults crossed behind it in the planned direction.

The fixed display followed.

No early flag.

No delayed deviation.

No coupling.

The system settled.

The test lead said, “Control one complete.”

Ollie wrote:

NO EFFECT.

He wanted the result to feel reassuring.

Instead it felt like a door that had not opened yet.

Second control.

Same movement order.

Different module-emulation profile.

No effect.

Third control.

Movement held, but no service-transition change.

One comfort patch flagged a brief local dry shift.

Ollie saw the event marker appear only as:

EARLY LOCAL EVENT — WITHIN PRE-REGISTERED PARTICIPANT CLASS

The fixed response remained ordinary.

No delayed deviation.

No coupling.

Ollie stared at the flag.

The old version of him would have wanted to move it to the side.

The newer version was apparently more annoying.

He marked:

EARLY EVENT / NO COUPLED RESPONSE.

The test lead said, “Correct.”

Ollie had not asked.

He wrote it anyway.

Iri rolled the cart back to start.

On the return they glanced at Ollie’s screen.

“You got one?”

“One early event.”

“And?”

“Nothing.”

Iri nodded.

No triumph.

Good.

* * *

The reproduction run began after a twenty-six-minute reset.

Most of that time was boring.

The adult team checked isolation.

Verified environmental reserve.

Confirmed stop authority.

Re-zeroed the local sensors.

Changed one airflow insert.

Ran the cart path empty.

Repeated the emergency exit.

Confirmed the four participants had not changed their consent.

Ollie waited.

Serious engineering, he discovered, was mostly people preventing the interesting thing from becoming interesting in the wrong way.

Mara gave him a food bar.

He looked at it.

“What?”

“You missed lunch.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“That is not relevant.”

He opened it.

Iri, across the test lane, pointed.

“Authority abuse.”

Mara said, “Move the cart.”

Iri moved the cart.

The test lead raised one hand.

“Reproduction condition. Run A.”

Ollie put the food bar down.

His screen cleared.

BASELINE.

Return correction entered active state.

The service-demand simulator shifted.

Iri moved the cart.

The four participants approached the transition.

The controller signaled HOLD.

Iri stopped at the side of the opening.

Two participants crossed.

The third paused because the cart occupied part of the preferred line.

The fourth held behind.

The controller signaled REVERSE.

Iri backed the cart into the service pocket.

The third participant changed line.

The fourth followed.

The local environment controller increased correction.

Ollie watched the timing channel.

Nothing.

Five seconds.

Nothing.

He felt stupid for counting when the system was doing it for him.

The movement-order state changed:

HELD.

REVERSED.

RESUMED.

One participant crossed before the wall display finished updating the service state.

A coarse event flag appeared.

EARLY LOCAL EVENT.

Ollie stopped breathing.

The flag came from a test patch on a trained adult participant, independent of Iri, residents, and the earlier source cases.

The test lead said, “Continue.”

Return correction remained active.

Service demand was still changing.

The fixed display stayed inside expected band.

Ollie watched the pre-registered window open.

The handheld environmental strip changed first.

Tiny.

Local.

Still inside safety limit.

Then the fixed response moved.

The movement was small but crossed the pre-registered timing band.

The line crossed the pre-registered timing band exactly where the model said a coupled deviation could appear.

Ollie did not say anything.

The test lead did.

“Deviation confirmed. Stop.”

The stop condition made the next thirty seconds deliberately boring.

The service simulator froze.

No second movement reversal.

No additional occupancy equivalent.

Adaptive optimization in the test loop dropped to conservative mode.

The partition remained fixed.

Local control returned to certified baseline.

Iri stopped the cart.

Participants stayed where instructed.

The fixed line moved once more.

Then settled.

No oscillation.

No escalation.

No alarm outside the test loop.

Ollie stared at the trace.

One early local flag.

One local environmental change.

One later fixed deviation.

Same order.

Inside the forecast window.

Then the adult team had killed the condition before it could become anything else.

The test lead said, “Ollie.”

He looked up.

“Result?”

He knew the sentence he wanted.

He did not use it.

“Run A reproduced the pre-registered ordered relationship: early local event, local environmental movement, then delayed fixed deviation under active return correction plus service transition plus held-reversed-resumed movement order.”

“Did we reproduce the Ring Six incident?”

“No.”

“Did we establish the full cause?”

“No.”

“Did the control runs show the same relationship?”

“No.”

“Did an early participant event occur once without coupled response?”

“Yes.”

The test lead nodded.

“Good. Save your channel.”

Ollie saved it.

His hands were shaking slightly.

He put them under the console.

Mara saw.

She said nothing.

* * *

They ran one more condition.

Ollie had not expected that.

The adult protocol had reserved a matched variant in case Run A produced a result inside the forecast band.

Same environmental load.

Same control generation emulation.

Same service-demand transition.

Same number of participants.

Different movement order.

No reversal.

The cart held.

Participants waited.

Then everyone resumed in the same direction after the return correction had closed.

One patch flagged nothing.

The handheld strip moved slightly and recovered.

Fixed system ordinary.

No delayed deviation.

No coupling.

The model survived a comparison it could have lost.

Ollie marked the result and felt less relief than he had expected.

The test had become harder to dismiss.

That was not the same as complete.

The test lead ended the session.

Adults started dismantling the setup before anyone could become emotionally attached to it.

The water blocks came out of the cart.

The airflow insert went back into storage.

Participant patches were cleared and sealed under the test protocol.

Ollie’s one timing channel closed.

His screen returned to:

REVIEW HOLD.

Iri came over.

“Well?”

“You were there.”

“I had a cart.”

“You had an excellent cart.”

“It was mediocre.”

Ollie looked through the glass at the adults reviewing the combined trace.

“We reproduced the relationship.”

Iri leaned against the rail.

“The whole thing?”

“No.”

“The thing you wanted?”

Ollie thought.

“Enough of it to reject the single-component explanation.”

“That sounds less satisfying.”

“Yes.”

Iri looked at him.

Ollie continued before they could ask.

“An early local event can happen without the coupled response. We saw that in control three.”

“So not a magic warning.”

“No.”

“And the ordered overlap can produce the delayed fixed deviation.”

“Under this test.”

“Not every module.”

“No.”

“Not every body.”

“No.”

“Not Ring Six.”

“No.”

Iri nodded.

“That is annoyingly reasonable.”

“I have been working on it.”

Mara opened the test-room door.

“Review.”

Ollie started to follow.

She held up a hand.

“Not you.”

He stopped.

The disappointment arrived cleanly.

No surprise.

Still disappointment.

“I thought—”

“You thought the test would change your review status while the test is still being reviewed?”

He closed his mouth.

Mara pointed toward the public corridor.

“Eat the rest of the bar.”

Ollie looked at the half-finished food bar on the console.

“This feels punitive.”

“It is nutrition.”

Iri said, “Authority abuse.”

Mara shut the door.

* * *

The technical review lasted eighty-three minutes.

Ollie spent forty of them doing inventory.

He knew because the inventory station had a clock and because he checked it seventeen times.

Tomas was on the next bench sorting seal kits.

His brace was still on.

“You are terrible company,” Tomas said.

“I am counting.”

“You have counted the same kit three times.”

“I am verifying.”

“You are waiting.”

“Yes.”

“Better.”

Ollie looked at him.

“Do not start.”

Tomas smiled.

Mara had routed him a summary rather than restoring access.

TEST RESULT — CONTROLLED REPRODUCTION

FINDING:
NO SINGLE COMPONENT FAULT IDENTIFIED.

BOUNDED ORDERED COUPLING REPRODUCED UNDER TEST CONDITIONS.

EARLY LOCAL/PARTICIPANT EVENT MAY PRECEDE FIXED DISPLAY IN SOME CONDITIONS BUT IS NEITHER NECESSARY NOR SUFFICIENT.

COUPLED RESPONSE REQUIRES MULTI-STATE INTERACTION; CONTROL-GENERATION / MODULE EFFECTS REMAIN MATERIAL.

SUSTAINED OSCILLATION NOT PRODUCED BY DESIGN.

CONDITION INTERRUPTED AT FIRST PRE-REGISTERED DEVIATION.

Ollie read the paragraph twice.

Tomas looked over.

“That good?”

“It closes the component question.”

“Meaning?”

“Nothing is secretly broken.”

“That sounds good.”

“It means the fix is worse.”

Tomas waited.

Ollie opened the engineering consequence line.

FULL-RING CONTROL / TRANSITION REDESIGN:
NOT CERTIFIABLE WITHIN CURRENT RELOCATION WINDOW.

LOCAL / SEGMENT-SPECIFIC MITIGATION:
REVIEW REQUIRED.

The review had produced a deadline problem instead of a culprit.

They could now reproduce enough of the failure to know what kind of problem it was.

They could not rebuild the whole ring fast enough to make the problem disappear.

Tomas read over his shoulder.

“So I still don’t get the easy commute.”

“Not from a full opening on schedule.”

“Could be worse.”

“You got injured.”

“Yes.”

“That is worse.”

Tomas considered.

“Fair.”

He went back to the seals.

Ollie kept reading.

The last line was not technical.

DECISION STAGE:
SAFETY / HOUSING / RESIDENT REVIEW REQUIRED.

The next problem was no longer diagnostic: safety and housing now had to decide what to do with a mechanism they could reproduce but not redesign away before the deadline.

He did not like choices with other people’s housing inside them.

* * *

Mara found him after shift in the maintenance corridor outside the inventory bay, without a commissioning-room debrief or celebration.

“You understand the result?”

“Yes.”

“Say the part you like least.”

“Full-ring redesign cannot be certified before the relocation deadline.”

“Other part.”

“Which?”

“The test did not prove every early report is part of the causal chain.”

“I know.”

“Say it.”

“Early local or embodied events may lead fixed sensing in some conditions. They are not necessary or sufficient for the coupled response.”

“Good.”

“And the ordering is now a reproduced part of the coupling class.”

“Under tested conditions.”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“Module/control generation still matters.”

“And?”

Ollie exhaled.

“We know enough to stop asking for a broken component.”

Mara nodded.

That was the closest thing to closure she was going to give him.

He asked, “What happens now?”

“Housing wants the full ring.”

“Safety won’t certify it.”

“Correct.”

“Broad restrictions?”

“Being proposed.”

“By body class?”

“By compatibility and environment profile among other things.”

Ollie thought of Iri’s regulator.

Of the source Aoife had hurt by making private accommodation inferable.

Of households already waiting.

“Then some people carry all the uncertainty.”

Mara looked at him.

“Yes.”

“That is bad.”

“It is one bad option.”

“What is the other?”

“Keep everyone out until redesign.”

Ollie pictured Lea’s small room that still was not hers.

Tomas’s commute.

Temporary housing split across routes.

School schedules.

Care schedules.

The chair moved thirty centimeters so it could stay.

“Also bad.”

“Yes.”

Ollie looked back toward the sealed test corridor.

“What if the risk is not attached to people?”

Mara waited.

He was not ready to answer his own question.

Not yet.

The test had closed the component mystery and handed the remaining problem to a decision process.

Mara said, “Go home.”

“I have study work.”

“Then go somewhere else and do that.”

Ollie picked up his bag.

At the next junction, Iri was waiting for a ringline with the repaired percussion clamp clipped to their pack.

“You’re done?”

“Yes.”

“Fixed?”

“No.”

Iri smiled.

“Good.”

Ollie almost argued.

Then understood what they meant.

There was no broken part left for Ollie to find.

The ringline arrived.

Ollie got on.

For once, he left the test behind.
