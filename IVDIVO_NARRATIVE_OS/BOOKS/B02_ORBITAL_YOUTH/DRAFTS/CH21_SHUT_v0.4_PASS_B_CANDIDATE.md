ORBITAL YOUTH
Chapter 21 — SHUT

Ollie spent twenty-three minutes trying to open a file he already knew he was not allowed to open.

The first attempt produced:

RING SIX INCIDENT DATA — REVIEW PARTITION
ACCESS: HOLD

The second produced the same thing.

So did the third.

On the fourth, the system stopped being polite.

YOUR ACCESS STATE HAS NOT CHANGED.

Ollie stared at it.

Ren, at the other end of the service table, said, “I think it knows you now.”

“I am checking whether the review partition has propagated.”

“You checked that six minutes ago.”

“It could have changed.”

“Did it?”

“No.”

“Good investigation.”

Ollie closed the request and opened the incident cross-reference instead.

The first page contained no line that said OLLIE CAUSED RING SIX TO OPEN.

He had been looking for one anyway.

Decision inputs appeared as a sequence of attached records.

HOUSING / TEMPORARY ALLOCATION PRESSURE.

RESIDENT ADVISORY — SPLIT.

SCHOOL / ROUTE CONSEQUENCE.

SERVICE STAFFING IMPACT.

ENVIRONMENTAL SAFETY CONDITIONS.

BOUNDED OCCUPANCY TEST — LIMITED OPEN CYCLE.

TIMING / TRANSITION ANALYSIS.

PUBLIC TECHNICAL POSITION — RESTRICTED PROFILE SUPPORT.

COMMISSIONING BOARD AUTHORIZATION.

Then the occupied incident.

A separate continuity block showed what the decision now meant outside the review room.

MAINTENANCE INJURY — MEDICAL / WORK RESTRICTION ACTIVE.

R6-A HOUSEHOLDS — TEMPORARY ALLOCATIONS REACTIVATED.

SCHOOL / ROUTE REVERSIONS — IN PROGRESS.

SERVICE DAMAGE — REVIEW / REPAIR.

No arrow connected Ollie’s name to the injured worker.

No arrow removed his analysis from the chain either.

He opened the board record.

His answer from the opening review sat exactly where he remembered it.

SUPPORT LIMITED HABITATION UNDER DEFINED RESTRICTIONS: YES.

The next field contained the safety engineer’s lower stop threshold.

The next contained housing’s allocation schedule.

The next contained resident deferral rights.

The next contained the board vote.

Ollie scrolled back to his own entry.

His analysis had mattered. So had six other inputs and the adults who owned the authorization.

He switched to the technical appendix he was still allowed to see.

The event reconstruction had been updated since the evacuation.

09:18 — RESIDENT REPORT.

09:22 — PERSONAL-INTERFACE REPORT.

09:25 — THIRD SAME-CLASS REPORT / REVIEW THRESHOLD.

INBOUND STAGING PAUSED.

RESIDENT MOVEMENT REDUCED.

EAST SERVICE — HELD / OCCUPIED.

COMMON RETURN CORRECTION — ACTIVE.

SERVICE C — OPEN.

PARTITION B — LOCKED.

Then the first fixed rise.

Five human or interface reports existed before the fixed threshold crossing.

Ollie had watched them arrive.

What he had not seen was the next diagram.

The review team had overlaid the environmental control dependencies rather than the named route states.

He leaned closer.

East service and service C belonged to separate route categories in his model.

The common return correction belonged to environmental control.

Partition B belonged to residential sequencing.

Separate labels.

Separate rule checks.

Underneath them, however, two airflow controls and the common-volume return path shared the same adaptive environmental loop.

Ollie expanded the timeline.

The east door stayed held around the waste cart.

The common return correction extended because residents reversed direction.

Service C opened to receive the same cart.

No prohibited partition moved.

No single state violated the profile.

The shared loop still received simultaneous demand from three allowed actions.

He moved the timestamps together.

The shape appeared.

Not the old direct overlap.

The same demand relationship produced without the named transition that his model had been watching for.

Ollie sat very still.

Ren looked up.

“What?”

Ollie did not answer.

Ren waited.

That was unusual enough to make Ollie notice.

He rotated the diagram once more.

The result remained preliminary.

The review note said so in three places.

POSSIBLE DISTRIBUTED EQUIVALENCE.

NOT ROOT-CAUSE FINDING.

EMBODIED / PERSONAL-INTERFACE ROLE UNKNOWN.

He read the second line twice.

Ren finally came over.

“Can I look?”

“It is open review material.”

Ren leaned over the display.

Ollie pointed at the three states.

“I treated these as separate.”

“They are separate.”

“Operationally.”

Ren traced the dependency layer with one finger without touching the screen.

“Oh.”

The word came out quieter than his jokes usually did.

Ollie closed the diagram.

Ren straightened.

“That is bad. I mean the model, not you.”

Ollie opened the decision record again.

Ren watched him do it.

“Are you trying to find your percentage?”

Ollie looked up.

“My what?”

“Of responsibility.”

“That is not a real quantity.”

“You keep reopening the only page where you might calculate it.”

Ollie did not answer.

Ren went back to his own work.

Ollie read the record again.

His analysis had not created the defect.

It had removed one legitimate reason to delay.

The distinction was exact and left his name exactly where it was.

* * *

Mara refused him at 11:06.

He had waited until 11:05 to ask because asking earlier would have looked desperate.

He had spent the previous hour being desperate privately.

“I want supervised access to the raw incident logs.”

“No.”

“Read-only. No control, no write authority.”

“The hold still applies.”

“What about an export of the environmental dependency layer?”

Mara kept tagging a damaged service component.

“You are changing the size of the request, not the reason for the hold.”

“You said the hold was procedural.”

“It is.”

“Then procedure can define supervised access.”

“It can. Not for you while your work is under review.”

Ollie stopped.

Mara turned the component over, checked the serial, and entered it into the service inventory.

“People whose work is under review do not alter the evidence environment and do not receive privileged access to raw review material. That is the boundary.”

“I would not alter anything.”

“I did not accuse you of altering anything.”

He started to answer.

Mara slid the tagged component aside and picked up a pressure-door track sensor from the next tray.

Ordinary work.

Ollie disliked it on sight.

She pushed it across the bench.

“Track module E-seventeen. Mature ring. Service corridor. Iri is already there.”

“That is not Ring Six.”

“Correct.”

“I can still be useful to the review.”

“If review asks for you, review gets you. This needs calibration now.”

Ollie did not touch the sensor.

“You could give it to Ren.”

“I could.”

“Why me?”

“Because you came here asking to be useful.”

“I meant useful to Ring Six.”

Mara looked at him then.

For a second he wished she had kept working.

“What do you actually want?” she asked.

“Access.”

“You do not have it.”

The answer landed harder without the argument around it.

Ollie looked at the sensor.

Mara waited.

He took it.

“E-seventeen?”

“Yes.”

“Any relation to Ring Six?”

“None that I know of.”

He picked up the calibration case.

“Will doing this change my access state?”

Mara had already turned back to the inventory.

“No.”

Ollie reached the door.

“Ollie.”

He stopped.

“Useful and permitted are different questions.”

Mara picked up the next tag.

That was all.

He left.

* * *

Iri was halfway inside a wall panel when Ollie arrived.

Only their legs were visible.

Ollie put the calibration case down.

“Apparently I am your punishment.”

“Unlikely. I already have work.”

Ollie waited.

“That was a joke.”

“I heard it.”

“You usually react.”

A hand appeared from inside the panel.

“Calibration lead.”

Ollie opened the case and put the lead into it.

Iri backed out of the panel.

Their hair had caught dust at one side. They did not fix it.

“E-seventeen?” they asked.

“Yes.”

“Review hold?”

“Yes.”

Iri nodded once and connected the block.

Ollie watched them.

“You approve.”

“Of the hold applying to you? Yes.”

“I did not cause the defect.”

Iri looked up from the connector.

“I did not say you did.”

Ollie handed over the rest of the lead harder than necessary.

Iri caught it, gave him a brief look, and ran the first calibration.

Five-percent drift.

“Again.”

Ollie reset the test.

The second reading was 4.8.

“Replace?” he asked.

“Clean first.”

“It is outside preferred calibration.”

“Still clean first.”

“If the drift is internal, cleaning does nothing.”

“Then three minutes from now you get to be right.”

Ollie took the cleaning strip.

They worked in silence.

He cleaned the contact surface while Iri checked the seating.

The next calibration read 1.2 percent.

Ollie stared at it.

“You suspected contamination.”

“Cheapest check first.”

“You could have said that.”

“You could have cleaned it.”

Ollie put the strip away.

Iri started reseating the panel.

He said, “Are you angry with me?”

Iri stopped with one hand on the fastener.

“Yes.”

Ollie had prepared for a less efficient answer.

“For the opening?”

“For how you listened.”

He frowned.

Iri tightened the fastener once, then set the driver down.

“You heard the reports.”

“I logged them.”

“I said heard.”

“I did not ignore them.”

Ollie felt himself moving into the argument before Iri had finished.

“The reports contradicted each other. Different language. Different onset precision. Some people said pressure, some airflow, some just wrong. They did not all happen before fixed changes. The ones during the bounded occupancy test did not escalate.”

Iri waited until he ran out of sentence.

“There. That.”

“What?”

“I had not finished.”

Ollie closed his mouth.

The corridor carried on around them. A maintenance cart passed. Someone farther down asked for a filter size. A child in the occupied residential section complained that a door was too slow.

Iri picked up the driver again.

“The words were messy. I am not arguing that.”

Ollie stayed quiet.

“The timing was less messy. Still bad. Less bad. People kept reporting a change before the fixed system admitted there was one.”

“Not every time.”

Iri looked at him.

Ollie exhaled.

“Sorry. Finish.”

“Trying something new?”

“Temporarily.”

Iri tightened the next fastener.

“You treated the reports like annotations until the building gave you something clean enough to call evidence.”

Ollie shifted the calibration case against his leg.

“That is not entirely fair.”

“No. It isn’t.” Iri’s attention stayed on the panel. “Some reports were adaptation. Some could have been unrelated. You needed something repeatable.”

The agreement removed the place Ollie had been preparing to defend.

Iri checked the fastener torque.

“But repeatable does not have to mean the same words. Maybe it is the same order.”

Ollie looked at the maintenance display.

Resident report.

Fixed response.

He saw the incident rail in his head.

Five entries.

Then threshold.

“That still does not make the reports causal. Or prove they are sensing the same variable. Or tell us whether personal adaptation is part of the loop.”

Iri picked up the calibration case and put it into his hands.

“I did not say any of those things. I said order is something you can test.”

Ollie frowned at the case.

“You are becoming difficult to argue with.”

“Good.”

“That sounded like Mara.”

“Take it back.”

“Immediately.”

Iri started down the corridor.

“Next panel.”

Ollie followed.

* * *

The next panel was not a track sensor.

It was an environmental service interface in an older occupied module, two generations behind Ring Six and apparently designed by people who hated knees.

Ollie knelt on the floor.

“I withdraw all criticism of Ring Six access panels.”

Iri handed him a tool.

“Historic moment.”

The service request was ordinary.

INTERMITTENT LOCAL COMFORT COMPLAINTS.
NO CURRENT FAULT.
CHECK INTERFACE / FILTER / RETURN TIMING.

Ollie opened the complaint history because it was part of the maintenance task.

Four entries.

The first said:

DRY EDGE AFTER SERVICE DOOR. CLEARS QUICKLY.

The second:

PRESSURE FEELS LATE ON RETURN. NO FIXED ALERT.

The third:

AIR CHANGED AFTER DOOR / PERSONAL SUPPORT CORRECTED BEFORE WALL UPDATE.

The fourth was six months later:

SAME THING. SHORT. NO FAULT FOUND LAST TIME.

Ollie stopped scrolling.

Iri was checking the filter housing.

He turned the display toward them.

Iri read all four entries, then crouched beside him.

“Different module generation. Different return control. No Ring Six partition logic.”

Ollie pointed to the third complaint.

“Personal support corrected before wall update.”

Iri read it again.

“Maybe useful.”

“Same ordering.”

“Maybe.”

“You are enjoying that word.”

“Very much.”

Ollie opened the fixed service history.

No incident.

No threshold event.

No damage.

The complaints had been closed as intermittent comfort issues after checks found no component fault.

Reasonable at the time.

Possibly still reasonable.

He searched the local task scope for access to other modules.

Denied.

Maintenance ticket only.

“Could we search broader complaint history?” he asked.

Iri pointed at the task header.

“Not from this ticket.”

Ollie looked at the four entries.

“What can we do?”

“Finish what we came for.”

They checked the filter.

Clean.

The interface was normal.

Return timing sat inside mature-module tolerance.

No fault.

Ollie entered:

NO CURRENT COMPONENT FAULT.

Before closing the ticket, he found the field for a related observation.

He angled the display toward Iri.

“This one?”

Iri read the field.

“Within scope.”

Ollie typed:

HISTORICAL COMPLAINTS INCLUDE REPEATED USER/PERSONAL-INTERFACE CHANGE PRECEDING FIXED-SYSTEM UPDATE. NO CLAIM OF COMMON CAUSE. FLAG FOR REVIEW IF INCIDENT TEAM REQUESTS CROSS-MODULE COMPARISON.

He showed Iri before submitting.

They read it once.

“Keep the no-common-cause line.”

Ollie submitted the ticket.

The system accepted the note.

No access expanded.

No one called him back to Ring Six.

They closed the panel and moved to the next service request.

Behind them, the older module stayed occupied.

A resident opened the service door.

The local return correction adjusted.

Nobody stopped walking.
