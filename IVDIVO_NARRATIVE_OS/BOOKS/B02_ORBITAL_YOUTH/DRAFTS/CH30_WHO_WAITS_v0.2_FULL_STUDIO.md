# ORBITAL YOUTH

## Chapter 30 — WHO WAITS

Aoife’s first job was to carry a box six meters and then carry it back.

The box contained school things.

Mostly.

A pair of shoes.

Two tablets in protective sleeves.

A paper notebook with three corners folded over.

A uniform layer still sealed from laundry.

Three pens.

A charger.

A second charger that did not belong with any of the devices in the box.

And one cooking spoon.

Aoife held up the spoon.

Lea’s mother looked inside the box.

“I wondered where that went.”

“This is school?”

“It was beside the school bag.”

“That is not the same category.”

“You have been here four minutes.”

“Long enough to establish standards.”

Lea, sitting on the floor beside two open storage cases, said, “Do not establish anything.”

Aoife put the spoon on the counter.

“Fine.”

Lea’s mother pointed at the box.

“Back to Temporary Three.”

“I just brought it from Temporary Three.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I thought his school gear was moving with me.”

Lea said, “It is not.”

“I know that now.”

Aoife looked between them.

Lea’s mother was one of the household members likely to move into the first Ring Six phase.

Lea was not.

Neither was Lea’s younger brother, whose environmental fit depended on a cluster that remained closed.

The household could split now or wait together.

They had not decided.

This meant the school box had nowhere permanent to go.

Aoife lifted it again.

“Temporary Three.”

Lea’s mother nodded.

“Sorry.”

“Do not be. I volunteered.”

Lea said, “That was your first mistake.”

Aoife carried the box back.

Temporary Three was not a building.

It was what everyone called the third sleeping module assigned to the household during the last eight weeks.

The family currently used two rooms on one ring and one small overflow room in another host sector because no contiguous temporary unit had matched all four people when their previous accommodation closed.

Lea slept in the overflow room three nights a week because it made her school route less bad.

Her mother kept most work clothes in the other room because her shift began before the first convenient transfer.

Her brother’s school things had migrated between both.

Laundry lived wherever somebody remembered to collect it.

Aoife had thought temporary housing meant sleeping somewhere you did not want to stay.

After four trips between the three rooms, even the household's objects had stopped having reliable addresses.

The box went back beside a folded sleeping layer and a bag of clean laundry that nobody wanted to unpack because they might move in two days.

Or six.

Or not at all.

A label on the laundry bag read:

RING SIX — IF MUM MOVES.

Someone had crossed out `IF`.

Then written it again.

Aoife put the school box down.

Lea appeared in the doorway.

“You can still leave.”

“I am beginning to enjoy this.”

“That is disturbing.”

“I have a strong relationship with badly labeled objects.”

“You literally make documentaries.”

“That is different.”

Lea looked at the recorder case inside Aoife’s shoulder bag.

It had not moved all morning.

“Are you actually not recording?”

“No.”

“Not secretly?”

Aoife looked at her.

Lea shrugged.

“Reasonable question.”

“Yes.”

Lea came in and picked up the spoon Aoife had left on the counter.

“You forgot this.”

“That spoon has more transit history than I do.”

“It is coming with Mum.”

“Why?”

“She cooks.”

“So do you.”

“Badly.”

“Still cooking.”

Lea put the spoon into a different box.

Aoife watched.

“Where does that one go?”

“Ring Six.”

“Definitely?”

“Probably.”

Aoife did not ask what probably meant.

That was becoming a useful skill.

* * *

By midday, the move-preparation room had acquired six different kinds of tape and no scissors.

Aoife found the scissors in a box labeled BATHROOM.

Nadia Park did not appear surprised.

“Keep them.”

“They belong to someone.”

“They belong to civilization now.”

Aoife put them in her pocket.

The room had once been a community classroom.

Today it was half relocation desk, half storage failure.

Households likely to clear first phase used one side.

Households still waiting used the other because they also had paperwork, route changes, storage questions and temporary-placement renewals to deal with.

The line between the two sides was imaginary.

People crossed it constantly.

A man from a first-phase household borrowed tape from a woman whose allocation remained on hold.

She gave it to him and then complained for five minutes that his segment should never have opened before hers.

He listened while taping a box.

“You still want me not to move?”

“I want Four open.”

“That is not the same question.”

“No.”

“So?”

She thought.

“Move.”

He tore the tape.

“Thank you.”

“I remain angry.”

“I noticed.”

Aoife carried a bundle of folded wall fabric toward the storage counter.

The woman looked at her.

“You’re the Earth documentary girl.”

Aoife nearly corrected `girl`.

Then did not.

“Yes.”

“You recording this?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Aoife shifted the fabric higher against her chest.

“I’m carrying your wall.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is today.”

The woman looked at the recorder bag.

“Good.”

Then, after a second:

“Also annoying.”

Aoife smiled.

“Why?”

“Because I had a very good complaint ready.”

“You can still complain.”

“What is the point if nobody is preserving it?”

The man with the tape said, “I will remember.”

“You forget your own shift times.”

“That is unrelated.”

Aoife left them arguing.

At the storage counter, a housing worker checked the fabric tag.

“Wrong destination.”

Aoife closed her eyes.

“Where?”

“Temporary Five.”

“There is a Temporary Five?”

“For them.”

“Of course.”

The worker looked at the fabric.

“You want a cart?”

“No.”

“You are carrying enough for a wall.”

“I am aware.”

She carried it back.

On the route, a child passed her with a plastic crate full of toys and one plant.

The child was crying because the plant had to stay behind for two days while the destination environmental profile was verified.

The adult beside them said, “It is two days.”

The child said, “That is a long time for basil.”

Aoife almost laughed.

Did not.

The child was serious.

So was the basil, presumably.

She kept walking.

* * *

The first argument that made no documentary sense happened over laundry.

A resident named Sela had opposed the segmented opening from the beginning.

Aoife knew this because Sela had said so twice in public housing meetings and once directly to Aoife, without being asked.

Sela’s household was still waiting.

Their room cluster depended on Segment Four.

At thirteen twenty, Aoife found Sela using the newly opened service route through Segment Two.

The route shortened the walk between Sela’s temporary sleeping module and the only laundry bank with the equipment their household needed.

Aoife was carrying a bag of someone else’s clean clothes.

Sela was carrying twice as much dirty laundry.

They recognized each other at the same time.

Sela looked at the route marker.

Then at Aoife.

“Do not.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were going to.”

“I genuinely wasn’t.”

“You have a face.”

“Everyone keeps telling me this.”

Sela adjusted the laundry bag.

“Yes, I am using the route.”

“Okay.”

“I still think the opening plan is wrong.”

“Okay.”

“Those statements are not contradictory.”

Aoife thought about the morning.

The man moving.

The woman angry.

The borrowed tape.

“No.”

Sela looked suspicious.

“You agree?”

“I agree that you can use a route and still dislike the allocation policy.”

“That was too fast.”

“I have had practice today.”

They walked together for one junction.

Sela said, “My commute is twelve minutes shorter now.”

“That sounds useful.”

“It is.”

“And you still want Four opened first.”

“I want my household out of temporary.”

Aoife nodded.

Sela stopped.

“See?”

“What?”

“You want to make that mean something.”

Aoife felt the reflex arrive.

The sentence practically assembled itself.

Infrastructure can improve a life without resolving the injustice a person sees in the system.

It was clean.

Accurate enough.

Also exactly the sort of thing that would make Sela into evidence for Aoife’s understanding.

Aoife let the sentence go.

Sela watched her.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That is suspicious.”

“I was thinking.”

“Dangerous.”

“Apparently.”

They separated at the next route.

Sela took the shorter service connection.

Aoife took the laundry bag toward Temporary Two.

Neither changed their mind.

* * *

Tomas was fifteen minutes late to his own lunch.

He blamed light-duty work.

Nadia blamed him.

Aoife blamed a transfer queue because she had been standing in it with him.

Tomas accepted none of these.

“I am not late. Lunch is flexible.”

“You said twelve thirty,” Aoife said.

“That was aspirational.”

“You sound like housing.”

“That is insulting.”

They sat at a narrow food counter near a service transfer opened under the first-phase plan.

Tomas still wore the forearm brace.

He had learned to do almost everything one-handed except open one particular food packet, which he handed to Aoife without comment.

She opened it.

He took it back.

“You could say please.”

“I could.”

“Or thank you.”

“I could also eat.”

Aoife took her own food.

The opened service route behind them had changed Tomas’s commute to light-duty coordination.

Not solved it.

Changed it.

Before the partial opening, his reassigned work position required two transfers from temporary housing plus a long service loop because the most direct route crossed the closed ring boundary.

Now one service connection was available during defined hours.

“What did it save?” Aoife asked.

Tomas looked at her.

“You are asking like a documentary.”

“Sorry.”

He checked the time anyway.

“Seventeen minutes in the morning if I catch the good transfer.”

“That is a lot.”

“Yes.”

“Does it help?”

“Yes.”

He ate.

Aoife waited.

He looked at her.

“You want the other half.”

“There is always another half.”

“That sounds like a thesis.”

“Do not insult me.”

Tomas smiled.

“My light-duty assignment moved twice this week because the work queues changed around the opening. Yesterday I saved seventeen minutes getting there and lost twenty-six because they sent me to a different service desk.”

Aoife nodded.

“My sister likes the new route,” he continued. “Her child hates the transfer because the waiting area has no wall game.”

“That seems fixable.”

“It is. The child has already submitted a complaint.”

“Good.”

“Very serious governance.”

Aoife laughed.

Tomas finished half the food.

“Do not make me a story about mixed outcomes.”

“I am not recording.”

“You can think without recording.”

“Unfortunately.”

“Then stop arranging me.”

That landed harder than Sela’s version.

Aoife looked at the people moving through the new route.

A worker with tools.

Two teenagers carrying food.

An older resident with a support frame.

Someone pulling a child by one hand while carrying laundry in the other.

The route was useful.

Tomas was still in temporary housing.

His arm still hurt enough that he changed how he held the packet.

His workday was better in one direction and worse in another.

Aoife ate.

No sentence arrived that she trusted.

She finished eating without opening her notes.

* * *

Aoife saw the person she had interviewed standing beside a box labeled KEEP HORIZONTAL.

Aoife almost walked past.

Not because she wanted to avoid them.

Because the source had not asked for anything.

Then the box tilted.

Aoife caught one end.

The source caught the other.

“Horizontal,” the source said.

“I saw.”

“You were walking away.”

“I was giving you space.”

“With gravity?”

“That sounded better in my head.”

They lowered the box onto a cart.

For several seconds neither said anything.

Aoife’s recorder remained inside her bag.

They looked at it.

Then at the cart.

“You working?”

“Badly.”

“I heard.”

“From who?”

“Everyone.”

“That seems fair.”

They checked the destination tag.

It listed a first-phase storage bay, not a residence.

Aoife did not ask why.

They noticed that too.

“You can ask where it goes.”

“I can also read.”

“That has not stopped you historically.”

Aoife smiled despite herself.

“Storage first?”

“Yes.”

“Then residence?”

“Maybe.”

Aoife nodded.

They took one handle of the cart.

Aoife took the other.

They pushed.

The route was short.

That helped.

At the storage bay, the tag reader rejected the box.

Wrong entry orientation.

Aoife rotated it.

Rejected again.

They said, “Other side.”

“It says horizontal.”

“Horizontal is not directional.”

“That is hostile labeling.”

“Welcome to civilization.”

They turned the box.

Accepted.

They looked at the green confirmation.

“Excellent.”

“Do not use that voice.”

“What voice?”

“The one that makes everything sound recordable.”

Aoife stopped.

“Sorry.”

They looked at her.

Then at the recorder bag.

“You actually left it off.”

“Yes.”

“All day?”

“Yes.”

“That sounds painful.”

“More physically than expected.”

They leaned against the cart.

Aoife waited.

No apology speech.

No question about whether they were okay.

They pointed back toward the staging room.

“You know the school-box rule?”

“No.”

“Never put socks in the same box as school cables.”

Aoife stared.

“What?”

“You will lose both, but only notice one.”

“That is absurd.”

“It is true.”

“Why?”

“Because socks migrate and cables get borrowed.”

“That is not a rule. That is a household failure.”

“Still a rule.”

Aoife laughed.

They did too.

Small.

Then said, “You can use that one.”

Aoife’s hand moved toward her bag before she stopped it.

Not far.

Enough.

They saw.

“You did that very visibly.”

“I know.”

“You can record it later.”

“Later?”

“If you still want it.”

Aoife let her hand fall.

“Okay.”

They looked mildly surprised.

“That’s all?”

“You said later.”

“Yes.”

“So later.”

They stood beside the cart.

Nothing repaired itself.

They did not offer another line.

Aoife did not ask.

A housing worker came through and pointed at the empty cart.

“You two finished?”

“Yes,” the source said.

Aoife moved aside.

They went one direction.

Aoife went another.

After six steps, they called back.

“And not the box location.”

Aoife turned.

“I know.”

“Good.”

Aoife watched them disappear into the next route, then checked that her recorder was still zipped inside the bag before she went back to work.

* * *

At sixteen forty, the first-phase board changed.

Three households cleared.

Two remained pending.

One withdrew from partial move because splitting the household would create more childcare travel than the new room would save.

A resident who had spent a week arguing for “open whatever is safe” did not clear.

His household depended on the conditional segment.

He read the update twice.

Then asked Nadia when the next review would happen.

Nadia gave him the window.

He said, “That is after the migration start.”

“Yes.”

“So everyone else moves first.”

“Some people.”

“I supported this.”

Nadia did not answer immediately.

Aoife was nearby with a stack of route cards.

The resident looked at her.

“Do not record me.”

“I’m not.”

“I said open what was safe.”

“Yes.”

“And now I wait.”

“Yes.”

“That sounds stupid when you say it.”

Aoife adjusted the cards.

“It sounds like what happened.”

He looked back at Nadia.

“I still think opening the safe segments is better than keeping them empty.”

Nadia nodded.

“And I still think this is a terrible result.”

“Yes.”

“Can both go in the record?”

“The housing record?”

“Yes.”

Nadia opened the case note.

“They can.”

The resident dictated both.

No one improved the wording.

Aoife carried the route cards to another table.

A family that had opposed the first-phase plan because they feared it would split the community had just received a full compatible allocation.

They were moving.

One of them was crying.

Another was arguing about whether to bring a table that would not fit the new room.

A teenager in the household said, “Leave it.”

The adult said, “It was my mother’s.”

“It still won’t fit.”

“That is not the point.”

The teenager looked at Aoife.

“Do not help.”

Aoife raised both hands.

“I have learned.”

The adult took the table.

The teenager took the legs after someone removed them.

They would make it fit or store it or fight again later.

Across the room, Lea was still waiting.

Her mother had chosen not to split the household that day.

The school box remained in Temporary Three.

The spoon had moved.

Aoife knew this because Lea sent her a message.

LEA: mum moved the spoon back

AOIFE: where

LEA: nobody knows

AOIFE: civilization ends

LEA: finally

Aoife smiled.

Then a second notification appeared.

Not from Lea.

Ethan.

ETHAN: I got the deadline.

Aoife stopped in the middle of the room.

Around her, people kept labeling boxes.

A child complained about basil.

Someone called for tape.

Nadia asked who had taken the scissors.

Aoife put one hand into her pocket.

She still had them.

ETHAN: answer due tomorrow 18:00

A second message followed.

ETHAN: first adult-track placement window overlaps migration week

Aoife looked at the screen.

The room did not become quieter.

No one around her knew the message existed.

She typed:

AOIFE: what are you going to do

Three dots appeared.

Stopped.

Appeared again.

ETHAN: don’t know yet

Aoife read that twice.

Aoife left the answer on the screen for a moment.

Nadia called again.

“Scissors?”

Aoife put the band away.

“Sorry.”

She carried them across the room.

On the board, some households were moving.

Some were not.

Tomorrow had a migration schedule now.

Ethan had a deadline.

Aoife handed Nadia the scissors and went back to the boxes.
