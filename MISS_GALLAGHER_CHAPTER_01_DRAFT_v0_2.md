# MISS GALLAGHER
## Chapter One — Working Draft v0.2

**STATUS:** WORKING SYNTHESIS / NOT CANON UNTIL APPROVED  
**Purpose:** Primary Writer draft after Writers’ Room Pass 01 and conditional Greenlight.  
**Provisional addition:** Enya has an independent time-limited conference delivery objective. This may be replaced without changing the chapter’s structural function.

It was raining in Dublin, and Smith entered the city through a man.

The man was a hotel clerk with greying temples, a wedding ring polished thin on the underside, and an umbrella he had bought that morning because the first one had turned inside out on College Green. Smith knew none of this because he wanted to know it. Ordinary lives leaked. You stepped into them and there they were: the wet cuff against the wrist, the half-remembered argument with a wife, the pressure behind the eyes from too much coffee, the small resentment toward a manager who had changed next week’s rota.

Smith ignored all of it.

He moved the clerk beneath the awning, let him shake the umbrella, let his body remember the sequence of the front desk, the key cabinet, the terminal, the private staff extension. Two calls in the clerk’s voice. One question asked as though it were routine. Less than a minute later Smith had a room number, a conference registration, and the time at which the closed evening session would begin.

Enya Gallagher.

Irish passport. American address. Philosophy of Synthesis Conference. No special security request. No companion registered. No diplomatic note. No visible reason for the assignment to have reached Smith at all.

He withdrew before the clerk noticed the cold at the base of his skull.

The man blinked under the awning and looked down at the umbrella as if he had forgotten why he was holding it.

Smith was already elsewhere.

The order had been seven words long.

**Gallagher. Dublin. Confirm. Take her before evening.**

There were operations that required months of preparation: border permissions, intermediary bodies, false deaths, quiet negotiations between offices whose existence ordinary governments would have denied even if shown the buildings. There were operations in which a wrong movement could open a chain of consequences across families, institutions, sometimes countries.

This was not supposed to be one of those.

A woman in a hotel.

Locate. Confirm. Take.

The simplicity was almost insulting.

Smith checked her from the lobby first.

He did not reach for her mind. That was not how competent work began. Thought was noisy, personal and often useless. He looked for the thing beneath thought: the relation that told him where a person ended and what, if anything, already had purchase there.

Every ordinary subject he had ever examined answered in some form.

Not visibly. Not with light, or a chain, or any of the childish images beginners invented before training stripped metaphor from them. But there was always a relation. A point of tension. A direction. Sometimes clean, sometimes tangled, sometimes buried beneath contracts, loyalties, inherited claims and the residue of other hands. Even the nearly destroyed had one.

Gallagher did not.

Smith held the read for three seconds longer than protocol required.

Nothing.

He released it.

The first explanation was contamination.

The clerk was an ordinary carrier. Useful for speech, motion, short access. Bad for precision. His headaches, stale coffee, old grief and twenty-seven years of unexamined habit made him a poor instrument for anything delicate. Smith had known that before he entered him.

So the result meant nothing yet.

He left the hotel, walked half a block beneath the rain, returned in his own body and booked the room beside hers.

The receptionist smiled without recognizing him.

That was as it should be.

In the lift, Smith watched the numbers rise and let the borrowed traces of the clerk fall away from him one by one. The man’s wife. The rota. The coffee. A school photograph of a daughter in a red jumper. He removed each impression with the same care a surgeon gave to washing blood from his hands.

By the time the doors opened, he was himself again.

Or what he had called himself for so long that the distinction had stopped mattering.

His room was narrow, expensive, and anonymous. The carpet had a geometric pattern chosen by someone who wanted guests to remember nothing. One wall separated him from Gallagher.

He put his coat over the chair, stood with both feet flat on the floor, and checked her again.

This time there was no clerk between them.

No borrowed nervous system. No ordinary human clutter. No excuse.

He found the room first: plumbing, electric lines, the building’s old stone beneath newer material, the small disturbances made by two hundred people stacked vertically above and below one another. Then the living presence on the far side of the wall.

A woman.

Breathing. Moving.

And where the expected relation should have been—nothing.

Smith did not move.

He changed the angle of the read. Not stronger. Strength was what amateurs used when information failed. He changed the question.

A concealed relation could still leave secondary evidence. A defended subject could still disturb the structure around the defense. A severed one left damage. A transferred one left discontinuity. Even the dead had consequences if one knew where to look.

Gallagher produced none of the expected answers.

That was the second check.

Smith opened his eyes.

Rain moved down the window in slow crooked lines.

For the first time since receiving the assignment he looked at the clock.

18:11.

The closed session began at 19:30.

His report window was 19:40.

He had eighty-nine minutes before an easy assignment became an official anomaly.

That mattered.

People imagined institutions punished failure because institutions were cruel. Sometimes they were. Cruelty was common and intellectually uninteresting.

The department punished certain failures because a failed hand could become evidence.

A hand that could not take a target might have encountered a stronger defense. It might have been compromised. It might have been recognized. It might have learned something it did not have the authority to know. In any of those cases the hand itself became part of the incident.

Smith had processed such incidents.

He knew exactly how cleanly they could be closed.

He sat on the edge of the bed.

The thought came to him in the professional grammar he trusted.

**Confirm category. Correct method. Complete assignment.**

There was no fear in it.

There did not need to be.

He had worked under worse conditions.

Once, years ago, a man had known Smith was coming three days before Smith arrived. The man had built defenses into a farmhouse, into the road, into his own family photographs. He had read futures badly but often enough to know the shape of the end.

When Smith entered, the man did not attack.

He bargained.

Money first. Then names. Then access. Then things Smith had not known existed and had not believed after hearing them. At the end the man offered everything he claimed to own for the continuation of his own life.

Smith listened until the offers stopped.

Then he completed the order.

No anger. No pleasure. No bargain.

People in the department disliked many things about Smith, but no one had ever accused him of appetite.

That was his pride, if he had one.

He could not be bought because there was nothing in him asking to be fed.

On the other side of the wall, Enya Gallagher opened a wardrobe.

Smith listened at the smallest level that still gave him useful information.

Not thought, exactly. The nervous system was physical; physical systems carried current; attention left patterns. With most people, once you learned how to distinguish signal from the endless interior weather, the first layer came easily.

A dress.

No. Too formal.

Another.

Shoes that would hurt after an hour.

A brief irritation about rain.

A calculation about whether there was enough time to go downstairs before the session.

Then nothing again, as though the ordinary surface ended above a depth Smith’s instrument could not enter.

He waited.

A drawer opened.

Paper moved.

Gallagher crossed the room twice. On the second pass the television came on for less than thirty seconds and went off again. She checked the time.

18:19.

Her phone rang.

Smith did not take the call. He did not need to. He heard only her side through the wall, the acoustics reduced to shape and cadence rather than words until she moved closer.

“Yes,” she said.

A pause.

“No, I have it.”

Another pause.

“I was told it had to be handed in personally.”

She listened.

“I know the session is closed.”

Longer pause.

“No. I’ll be there before half seven.”

She ended the call.

Smith looked at the wall.

So she had an objective.

That was useful.

People with objectives exposed routes.

He stood and put his coat back on.

Before leaving, he made one more attempt—not deeper, but narrower. He followed only the active physical line associated with the call: hand, breath, attention, movement toward the object she had said she possessed.

For a moment he caught the pressure of paper under her fingers.

A narrow envelope or folded document.

Then the line vanished.

Not blurred.

Not defended.

Gone.

Smith stopped so quickly the bed frame touched the back of his knee.

On the other side of the wall, Gallagher stopped too.

Neither moved.

The stillness lasted perhaps two seconds.

Then she resumed packing.

Smith left the room.

---

The conference occupied three rooms on the hotel’s first floor and behaved, in every visible respect, like a conference.

There were badges. Coffee. A table with printed programs. Men and women standing in circles and pretending not to look at one another’s name cards before deciding whether a conversation was worth entering. A graduate student argued softly with someone old enough to have forgotten the argument twice before. Two people near the windows discussed funding. Someone laughed too loudly.

Smith disliked conferences.

Not because they were crowded. Crowds were useful.

Because educated people generated extraordinary amounts of structured noise.

He entered the first available trained carrier near the stairwell.

The man was old.

Not old enough to be weak, but old enough that the body had stopped hiding its maintenance from itself. One knee hurt on stairs. The lower back tightened after sitting. There was medication in the bloodstream and the beginning of a headache behind the left eye.

And sweat.

The man had walked too quickly from the Luas stop under a coat made for colder weather, and the smell of his own body rose into Smith’s borrowed nose with offensive persistence.

Smith had been shot through carriers. Burned through them. Once remained functional for nine minutes after a host’s heart stopped.

The smell of this man’s shirt distracted him more than any of those things.

He accepted the humiliation because the carrier was trained.

That was the difference.

Ordinary people were not empty rooms. They were houses full of sleeping children. You could move through them, but every careless step broke something. A trained carrier had been prepared for passage. Channels existed. Damage could be contained.

There were no others close enough.

Smith approached the door to the closed session.

A young woman with a badge held up one hand.

“Sorry. Speakers and registered delegates only.”

The carrier smiled automatically.

“I’m with—”

Smith stopped the sentence.

The badge around the young woman’s neck had a small blue stripe. Staff. Temporary. No authority beyond the door.

Behind her, another person sat at a table checking names against a printed list.

No electronic gate to bypass. No useful official to inhabit without creating an obvious absence. No trained carrier inside his immediate reach.

A wooden door, a paper list and a twenty-two-year-old volunteer had stopped him.

Somewhere deep in the borrowed body, the old man found this funny.

Smith felt the beginning of the reaction and shut it down before it became laughter.

“I must have the wrong room,” he said.

The volunteer relaxed.

“Public panel is in the Liffey Room at eight.”

“Thank you.”

He turned away.

18:47.

Gallagher was still upstairs.

He knew because he had left a minimal line of observation on the corridor, not on her. Door movement. Lift use. Foot traffic. Nothing more.

He moved through the conference perimeter once.

There were six obvious routes into the closed room and only two that would not force him to damage or expose someone. One would require a carrier he did not have. The other would require Gallagher to enter first.

So he would follow her.

Simple.

He returned the old man to himself in an empty section of corridor.

The man leaned one hand against the wall and breathed through his nose.

“Christ,” he whispered, though no one had spoken to him.

Smith left before the man could wonder why his mouth tasted of someone else’s concentration.

In his own body again, Smith stood near the lift and watched rain silver the glass doors at the far end of the lobby.

18:56.

His phone vibrated once.

No caller ID.

A department channel.

**STATUS.**

Nothing else.

Smith looked at the word.

The correct answer was concise.

**TARGET CONFIRMED. CAPTURE PENDING.**

He typed it.

His thumb rested over send.

Then he deleted **TARGET CONFIRMED**.

He had confirmed a woman.

He had not confirmed the category under which the order had been issued.

He typed:

**SUBJECT LOCATED. CLASSIFICATION PENDING.**

He looked at the sentence for three seconds.

It was accurate.

Accuracy had always protected him.

He sent it.

The response came almost immediately.

**ORDER UNCHANGED.**

Smith put the phone away.

For no reason he could name, the words irritated him.

---

At 19:04 the lift opened.

Enya Gallagher stepped out carrying a dark coat over one arm and a narrow folder in the other hand.

Smith saw her directly for the first time.

Nothing about her resolved the problem.

She was not dressed like someone expecting violence. She had changed twice and chosen something simple in the end: dark trousers, a pale blouse, shoes sensible enough for rain. Her hair was pinned loosely and would not survive ten minutes outside. She held the folder too carefully to be casual about it and not carefully enough for it to be valuable in any ordinary financial sense.

She crossed the lobby without looking around.

Smith followed at a distance.

Not because he feared being recognized.

Because professionals did not create unnecessary questions.

She stopped at the conference table.

The volunteer with the blue stripe looked up.

Gallagher said something Smith could not hear from where he stood. The volunteer shook her head. Gallagher opened the folder, showed a page, then pointed to the printed program.

The volunteer looked uncertain.

A second staff member joined them.

There was discussion.

Enya did not raise her voice.

She did not invoke a name or threaten a complaint. She waited, answered two questions, and finally took the folder back when the second staff member gestured toward the closed door.

Permission.

Earned through some ordinary rule Smith did not yet know.

She walked toward the session.

Smith moved.

He had perhaps ten seconds before the door closed behind her.

At four metres he opened the narrowest contact he could manage without commitment.

Not entry.

Not seizure.

A touch.

Enough to establish direction.

The effect came from below his own spine.

Smith stopped.

It rose through him slowly, impossibly, from the base of his back toward the skull.

Not pain.

Not force.

Recognition without memory.

His first thought was attack.

His second was contamination.

His third did not arrive in professional language at all.

A room full of light.

Someone standing where he could not see the face.

A voice saying something he had once understood completely.

Then the memory—or whatever it was—collapsed before the words returned.

Smith’s hand had gone to the wall.

He did not remember putting it there.

Across the corridor, Enya Gallagher stopped with her hand on the conference-room door.

She did not turn immediately.

The wave faded.

Smith could hear the hotel again: glasses touching somewhere in the bar, a suitcase wheel catching on a seam in the floor, rain against the entrance doors, the faint bass of a song from a kitchen radio.

Gallagher looked over her shoulder.

Not toward the lift.

Not toward the lobby.

At him.

There were people between them. A man carrying coffee crossed her line of sight. The volunteer spoke. Gallagher did not answer.

For one second Smith had the exact, absurd sensation that she was not looking at the body he stood in.

She was looking at the place from which he looked.

Then she smiled.

It was not triumph.

That would have been easier.

It was the small, almost relieved smile of someone who had been waiting for a late guest and had just seen him come through the door.

Gallagher entered the closed session.

The door shut.

Smith remained where he was.

His phone vibrated again.

**19:10. PREPARE TRANSFER.**

He read the instruction once.

Then again.

For the first time in years, perhaps for the first time in his professional life, Smith did not immediately begin the next required action.

He put two fingers against the place on his spine where the wave had begun.

There was nothing there.

No mark.

No pain.

No evidence his department would accept.

Only the afterimage of a room full of light and the certainty—irrational, unwelcome, entirely his own—that if he obeyed the order exactly as given, he would destroy the only evidence he had ever encountered that the order might not be the same thing as the truth.

His phone vibrated a third time.

Smith turned it face down in his palm.
