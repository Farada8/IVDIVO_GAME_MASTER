## Preliminary structural map — NOT YET LIFECYCLE-COMPLETE
The book is organized around three broad jobs:

1. **INVENTION** — discover a character as a human causal system: action, motive, history, reputation, habits, abilities, body, relationships.
2. **CONSTRUCTION** — decide how much character the story actually needs: story factor dominance, hierarchy, emotional stakes, audience sympathy, comedy/seriousness, transformation.
3. **PERFORMANCE** — decide how the reader receives the character: voice, person, narrator/viewpoint choice, distance/penetration.

This is already operationally important: a prose/POV fix cannot substitute for a defective invented motivation, and additional backstory cannot repair a scene whose character has no causal job.

## Provisional transferable mechanisms

### C15-M01 — INVENTION / CONSTRUCTION / PERFORMANCE DIAGNOSIS
Before editing a character problem, classify it:
`WHO IS THIS PERSON? → WHAT JOB/DEPTH DOES THIS STORY REQUIRE? → HOW DOES THE READER EXPERIENCE THEM?`

Failure mode: line-editing a motivation problem, or adding biography to a viewpoint problem.

### C15-M02 — ACTION IS PRIMARY CHARACTER EVIDENCE
What a character does under conditions is stronger evidence than author labels about personality.

Failure mode: narrator says `Ethan was learning restraint` after the scene has already shown his hands staying off a control.

### C15-M03 — MOTIVE DEEPENS ACTION
Action establishes the observable person; motive changes the meaning of the same action.

IVDIVO use: distinguish two cadets who both volunteer for the same dangerous training but for different reasons—belonging, prestige, curiosity, loyalty, escape from family expectation, or genuine service.

### C15-M04 — INTERROGATE FIRST ANSWERS
For a character fact, repeatedly ask `why? / for what purpose? / what caused that? / what results?` because the first available explanation is often generic.

Failure mode: a stock wound, generic rebellious teen, generic ambitious cadet, generic wise mentor.

### C15-M05 — STORY-FACTOR DOMINANCE CONTROLS CHARACTER DEPTH
Card's MICE model distinguishes milieu / idea / character / event emphasis. IVDIVO uses this only diagnostically, not as a mandatory taxonomy.

Production consequence for Orbital Youth: **Book Two should not accidentally become a pure milieu or idea story.** The orbital civilization can be a major attraction, but one complete external problem plus a character change must control the book's causal spine.

### C15-M06 — CHARACTER HIERARCHY CONTROLS ATTENTION BUDGET
Walk-on / minor / major is a continuum of promised reader attention. A minor can flash memorably without acquiring a complete independent arc; a major recurs and changes causal flow.

Failure mode: every named cadet gets equal biography, POV, jokes and trauma, producing ensemble fog.

### C15-M07 — MEMORABILITY IS NOT THE SAME AS IMPORTANCE
Eccentricity, exaggeration or obsession can make a minor character memorable, but memorability must not falsely promise major causal significance.

### C15-M08 — SYMPATHY AND MORAL AGREEMENT CAN BE SEPARATED
The strongest moral pressure can come from two sympathetic characters whose desired outcomes conflict.

IVDIVO use: mentor/cadet, human/robotic person, planetary/orbital authority, or two friends can both be reasonable; the scene should force choice without converting one into a secret villain.

### C15-M09 — REALISTIC + EXTRAORDINARY HERO BALANCE
A protagonist needs enough ordinariness for reader recognition and enough unusual capacity/choice/problem to justify sustained attention.

Failure mode A: superhero competence erases adolescence.
Failure mode B: aggressively ordinary life produces no story-worthy decision.

### C15-M10 — TRANSFORMATION REQUIRES CAUSAL JUSTIFICATION
The larger the behavioral change and the more central the character, the more causal preparation/payoff the story owes. If the cause is withheld, the text must signal that the inconsistency is an intentional question.

Failure mode: a normally cautious character suddenly risks everyone because the climax needs it.

### C15-M11 — VOICE IS RELATIONSHIP-SPECIFIC
People possess different vocabularies, syntax, formality and attitude in different relationships/roles. Character voice is not a fixed slang pack.

IVDIVO use: the same teenager should not speak identically to a close friend, parent, robot peer, cadet instructor, romantic interest and formal review board.

### C15-M12 — POV DEPTH IS A TEMPERATURE CONTROL
Cinematic / light / deep penetration are usable as a continuum. Deep access raises intensity and subjectivity; distance can restore clarity, pace and observable action.

Failure mode A: permanent deep POV becomes exhausting/claustrophobic.
Failure mode B: permanent cinematic narration withholds the very interiority prose handles best.

### C15-M13 — DIALOGUE SHOULD NOT CARRY ALL CHARACTER EXPOSITION
If characters must openly state their history, feelings and relationship facts so the reader can understand them, the scene often lacks viewpoint filtering, behavior and shared-history implication.

### C15-M14 — CHARACTER CHANGE MUST ALTER PATTERN, NOT LABEL
A transformation is meaningful when a repeated behavioral pattern changes under a different cause/choice—not when the narrator announces a new trait.

## Provisional failure modes / historical limits
- Some examples and gender/social assumptions reflect the book's original era; do not transfer period stereotypes as current youth psychology.
- Card's MICE framework is useful as a dominance diagnostic but should not become a rigid four-box genre ontology.
- His sympathy claims are craft heuristics, not universal psychological laws; current reader response must be tested with real readers.
- `hero = teacher by example` can become moral simplification if used prescriptively. IVDIVO keeps ambiguity and reader inference.
- `signs and portents` can become manipulative pathetic fallacy if every emotional beat gets matching weather/environment.
- Stereotypes as background compression require modern ethical scrutiny; do not use identity stereotypes as cheap invisibility mechanisms.
