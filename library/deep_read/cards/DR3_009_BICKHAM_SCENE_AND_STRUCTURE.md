## Unique strength

Bickham is strongest at testing whether individual scenes have the right-sized goal, conflict and result to change the novel without prematurely ending it, and at diagnosing common scene failures. Compared with Swain, Bickham pushes the same causal family toward revision, pacing and long-form plotting.

## Transferable mechanisms

### BI01 — Fiction needs stronger causal legibility than random life
Plot developments should have prepared causes, especially when a consequence materially changes the story.
Use: continuity/credibility audit.

### BI02 — Stimulus/response is structural microgrammar
An external or interpersonal cause should plausibly produce the next behavior/thought/action.
Failure: unmotivated behavior inserted to move plot.

### BI03 — Scene goal must have meaningful scope
Too-small goal cannot affect story; too-large goal may resolve or destroy the story prematurely.
Use: calibrate scene stakes.

### BI04 — Result changes the course
A scene should produce consequence with enough scope/immediacy/direction that the story situation is not identical afterward.

### BI05 — Conflict must be fully developed when promised
Do not cut around the confrontation the causal chain has prepared because it is difficult to write.
Use: Red Team flags skipped obligatory scenes.

### BI06 — Disaster/result must be proportionate and generative
The result should worsen/complicate/narrow or redirect possibilities while leaving credible story movement.
Failure: either trivial outcome or terminal overkill too early.

### BI07 — Sequel gives emotional/thought/decision bridge
After a result, character processing can re-establish emotional state, examine dilemma and generate new intention.

### BI08 — Physical stimulus can motivate memory/backstory
Memory is stronger when triggered by current environment/action rather than appearing because author wants exposition.

### BI09 — Scene conflict should avoid circularity
If both sides repeat the same positions, introduce new evidence, tactic, status move, cost or choice.

### BI10 — Minimize unnecessary participant diffusion
When the dramatic core is two opposed wills, extra speakers can dilute focus; ensemble scenes need explicit status/agenda design.
Use: prevents symmetrical committee dialogue.

### BI11 — Scene errors are often structural, not stylistic
Common faults include forgotten goal, unmotivated opposition, unfair odds, low stakes, circular argument, loss of POV and contrived result.

### BI12 — More unresolved 'plates' can increase suspense—up to cognitive overload
Main plot and subplots can create multiple concerns, but each must remain trackable and eventually come down safely.
Use: ensemble saga without random subplot accumulation.

### BI13 — Variation conceals underlying structure
Vary intensity, scene type, goal scale and result style while preserving causal function.

### BI14 — Master plot is writer/story-specific, not fixed template
Clarify the broad pattern appropriate to this novel/genre, then manipulate scene units inside it.

## Character mechanisms

- A character earns forward motion by deciding and acting after consequences.
- POV and stimulus-response reveal what matters to the focal character.
- Opposition must be motivated and credible enough to force strategy.

## Scene/dialogue implications

Bickham revision checklist:
- Is the scene goal clear enough to pursue?
- Is opposition motivated?
- Does conflict develop rather than circle?
- Are stakes sufficient?
- Does the result alter the course?
- Is POV stable enough for the intended effect?
- Did the draft skip the difficult confrontation it promised?

## Escalation/payoff

Escalation comes from accumulating logically related problems/subplots and from scene results that add new dimensions to the central problem. Payoff requires resolving the story questions the novel has kept 'in the air'.
