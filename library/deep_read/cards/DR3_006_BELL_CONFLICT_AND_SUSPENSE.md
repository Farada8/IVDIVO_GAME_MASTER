## Unique strength

Bell is strongest as a reader-propulsion diagnostic. His useful abstraction is that readers remain engaged when a character they care about faces meaningful trouble, conscious opposition and local uncertainty. This applies to quiet relationship scenes as well as physical danger.

## Do NOT take

- Do not equate conflict with fighting, shouting or violence.
- Do not force every scene into thriller intensity.
- Do not manufacture cliffhangers with no consequence.
- Do not treat reader manipulation as permission to cheat causality.

## Transferable mechanisms

### B01 — Meaningful trouble is the entry price
A scene that merely informs, decorates or demonstrates quirk needs pressure that matters to someone.

### B02 — Trouble may be external or internal
Threat to dignity, love, belonging, identity, status or hope can propel as strongly as physical danger when story stakes make it real.

### B03 — Conflict is incompatible forces, at least one with conscious will
For interpersonal drama, both sides should be capable of strategy.
Use: jurisdiction, family, romance, work, peer groups.

### B04 — Opposing agendas create dynamic scenes
Two people cannot both get the same outcome unchanged.
Effect: tactics/countertactics emerge naturally.

### B05 — Suspense is a local dramatic question inside conflict
Examples of abstract questions: Will she be believed? exposed? selected? forgiven? late? trapped? forced to reveal? lose authority?
Failure: same 'will they survive?' question repeated.

### B06 — Suspense tightens emotional experience
Narrow options, delay an answer for causal reasons, increase cost of waiting/acting, then release or complicate.

### B07 — Buildup and release create rhythm
Anticipation → pressure → payoff/partial release → new complication.
Failure: permanent maximum intensity causes numbness.

### B08 — Conflict must evolve in the middle
Change tactic, arena, leverage, relationship, information or stake; do not re-argue identical ground.

### B09 — Inner conflict becomes dramatic when choices exclude each other
Character must act while important values/desires pull in incompatible directions.

### B10 — Dialogue conflict is tactical
Words should seek outcomes; contradiction alone is not dramatic conflict.

### B11 — Setting can create suspense
Space, visibility, distance, crowding, access, time windows and environmental constraints can narrow choices.
Use: orbital architecture and Old Earth containment sites.

### B12 — Revision asks where pressure disappears
Find pages where reader can safely stop because no question, cost or emotional expectation remains; redesign upstream.

## Character mechanisms

- Trouble exposes what the character values.
- A strong character needs something sufficiently important to fight, risk or change tactics for.
- Interior threats should produce external choices/consequences.

## Scene/dialogue implications

Conflict ladder:
`GOAL → RESISTANCE → TACTIC → COUNTERTACTIC → NARROWER OPTIONS → LOCAL QUESTION → RESULT → NEW PRESSURE`.

## Escalation/payoff

Escalation should expand consequence or reduce viable options, not merely increase volume. Suspense questions must receive real answers, even when the answer opens a harder question.

## Cross-source check

AGREES WITH McKee: antagonism and progressive complication generate character/story power.
AGREES WITH Swain/Bickham: scene conflict must produce a changed result/disaster that motivates later action.
AGREES WITH Rosenfeld: stakes create tension; scenes require intention.

## IVDIVO transformation

Red Team question for every scene: `If I remove the trouble/resistance, does the scene still play almost identically?` If yes, it is probably exposition or atmosphere pretending to be story.
Use ordinary-life suspense liberally: money, work shifts, housing, attraction, embarrassment, status, family permission, repair deadlines, transport, jurisdiction.
