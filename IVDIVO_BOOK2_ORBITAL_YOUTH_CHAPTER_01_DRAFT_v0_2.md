# ORBITAL YOUTH
## BOOK TWO
### CHAPTER ONE — THE LAST GROUND

**Status:** WORKING DRAFT v0.2 / RED-TEAM PATCHED / NOT CANON

Ollie had been told three times not to touch the rover.

The first time had been polite.

The second time had included his name.

The third time Mina stood between him and the open service panel with both hands on her hips and said, “If you reach past me, I am calling your mother.”

Ollie looked at the rover.

The rover looked unfinished.

This was not because it was unfinished. It had passed drive, obstacle, comms and recovery tests yesterday. The left manipulator no longer twitched under load. The rescue cradle had survived Ethan standing in it while Maya drove the prototype over the practice ramp, which had not been part of the official test plan.

Technically, the rover was ready.

This was worse.

“There’s still a vibration at forty-two percent front-left load,” Ollie said.

Mina held up her tablet. “Inside tolerance.”

“With the new compensator values?”

“Yes.”

“And lateral bias?”

“Yes.”

Ollie looked at the service panel.

Mina moved two centimeters to the left.

He had not moved.

“That feels accusatory.”

“You leave in fifty-three minutes.”

“Fifty-eight.”

“Your mother messaged me.”

Ollie stared at her.

“That is a hostile escalation.”

“You created the conditions.”

On the other side of Bay Four, Jonah laughed into a parts crate.

The maker bay at Jefferson Regional Technical Center had the Sunday-evening emptiness of a place that belonged to teenagers only because adults trusted them enough to leave the lights on. Half the workbenches were covered in sleeping projects. A climbing drone hung upside down from a gantry. Three lengths of printed structural foam lay under a sign marked NOT TRASH, which meant they would remain there until graduation.

The rover sat on maintenance blocks in the center aisle.

Four independently articulated wheel units. Flat chassis. Fold-out rescue cradle. Two manipulators. Sensor mast. Emergency beacon. No white shell pretending rescue work was clean.

Ollie liked it more because of that.

Mina set the tablet down.

“We ran the full load series.”

Ollie said nothing.

“We ran the asymmetric casualty load.”

“That was Ethan.”

“He was available.”

“He complained.”

“He improved realism.”

Jonah laughed again.

Mina picked up a torque driver, checked the bit and put it back in the tray.

“The vibration is logged. If it changes, we inspect. If it doesn’t, we do not take apart a working wheel assembly six days before competition because you enjoy discovering new ways to make things temporarily worse.”

Ollie considered this.

“That is an unfair description of diagnostics.”

“It is an accurate description of you.”

His wrist chimed.

DEPARTURE WINDOW — 00:47:31

He dismissed it.

Mina watched him do that too.

Then she turned her tablet toward him.

The project permissions were open.

His name sat near the top.

OLIVER — SYSTEMS / DRIVE / RECOVERY

Mina touched one control.

His live branch access disappeared.

Ollie stared at the screen.

“What did you do?”

“Removed remote write access until you come back.”

“You can’t do that.”

“I’m project lead this week.”

“You can’t do that to the drive branch.”

“I just did.”

Jonah raised one hand. “I support the coup.”

Ollie stepped closer to the tablet instead of the rover.

“What if the load curve shifts during the review?”

“We know how to read a load curve.”

“What if the compensator starts clipping?”

“We know what clipping looks like.”

“What if—”

“Ollie.”

He stopped.

Mina’s voice had lost the joke.

“Go.”

He looked at the rover.

Six days.

He had pictured watching the regional challenge from Vela if his shift schedule allowed it. He had pictured the team sending him a question at some inconvenient orbital hour because there would obviously be one thing only he remembered.

What he had not pictured was them doing perfectly well.

Without him.

That was an ugly thought.

Not because he wanted them to fail.

He didn’t.

He just wanted his absence to register somewhere.

Mina must have read enough of his face because she leaned against the bench instead of telling him to leave again.

“The rover doesn’t become less yours because we keep working,” she said.

Ollie looked at the permission screen.

“It also doesn’t become more yours because you’re the one holding the tablet.”

“No.”

“If something fails—”

“It’s our failure.”

He looked at her.

Mina tapped her own chest, then pointed at Jonah, then at the empty workstations around the bay.

“Plural.”

Ollie looked back at the rover.

That was the irritating thing about teams. Sometimes they continued existing when you left the room.

His wrist chimed again.

DEPARTURE WINDOW — 00:41:02

Mina pointed toward the door.

Ollie picked up his bag.

At the threshold he turned.

“If the front-left vibration goes above forty-seven millimeters per second squared under combined lateral—”

“Goodbye, Ollie.”

Jonah threw a shop rag at him.

Ollie left.

—

His mother had packed two sandwiches for a trip in which food was included.

Ollie found them in the side pocket of his travel bag while she was checking his medical clearance at the kitchen counter.

“Why are these here?”

“In case you’re hungry.”

“There is food at the transfer center.”

“I know.”

“And in Dublin.”

“I know.”

He looked at the sandwiches.

His mother kept checking the file.

The argument had reached a complete technical dead end.

His father came in carrying the hard case with Ollie’s approved personal kit.

Not actual site tools. Those stayed with the work placement.

The case held a multimeter, calibration tags, an optical inspection clip, two approved interface adapters and a mechanical pencil.

The adapters had needed three separate clearances.

The pencil had needed none.

Ollie respected the pencil.

His father set the case down.

“You signed custody?”

“Yes.”

“Host contact?”

“Yes.”

“Emergency line?”

“Yes.”

“Maker-team escalation?”

Ollie looked up.

His father pointed at himself.

“They have me if something comes up while you’re out of contact.”

“You?”

“Mina asked.”

Ollie looked toward his mother.

She suddenly became very interested in the medical file.

“When did that happen?”

“Yesterday.”

“They can message me.”

“They can,” his father said. “If you’re available.”

“I will be available.”

His father looked at the transfer itinerary on the counter.

Ollie followed his eyes.

Orbital prep. Ascent. Dublin transfer layers. Vela arrival. Host check-in. Placement induction.

There were several places where “available” was clearly not a decision Ollie got to make.

He picked up the equipment case.

“Fine.”

His father nodded.

The nod was much too neutral.

Ollie disliked the entire arrangement.

Which was probably why everyone else liked it.

His mother finally closed the medical file.

“You will message when you reach Dublin.”

“Yes.”

“And after orbital transfer.”

“Yes.”

“And when you reach Vela.”

“Yes.”

“And if your host changes.”

“Yes.”

“Ollie.”

“I said yes.”

“I know. I’m checking whether you know what it means.”

He looked at her.

“I’ll message.”

She nodded.

His room was visible through the open hall door behind her.

Old rover wheel on the shelf. Three failed manipulator joints. A cracked drone housing signed by everyone after a crash nobody admitted had been avoidable.

Things from the version of his life where every project was close enough to bike to.

His mother touched the travel-bag strap.

“You excited?”

“Yes.”

“Scared?”

Ollie opened his mouth.

His father looked at him.

Ollie closed it again.

“A little.”

His mother smiled. “Good.”

“Why is that good?”

“It sounds accurate.”

His wrist chimed.

DEPARTURE WINDOW — 00:22:18

His younger sister called from the hallway, “He’s going to miss orbit because of a wheel.”

“I am not.”

“You missed Thanksgiving dinner because of a wheel.”

“I arrived during dessert.”

“That is missing dinner.”

“It depends which part you value.”

His mother laughed.

Then she hugged him.

He hugged her back.

His father was next. One hand on Ollie’s shoulder.

“Do the boring work,” he said.

Ollie looked at him.

“That is exactly what Mina said.”

“I know.”

“You people are coordinating.”

“Go to orbit.”

Outside, the pickup vehicle was waiting.

Ollie stepped onto the front walk and looked up once.

Cold sky. Ordinary stars.

For most of human history, everyone who looked at them had been standing on a planet.

That was not true anymore.

His mother opened the door behind him.

“Ollie.”

“I’m going.”

He got in the car.

—

Ethan was already at the transfer hub.

Of course he was.

He stood under the DEPARTURES — ORBITAL / LUNAR CONNECTIONS sign with one backpack, one equipment case and the expression of someone who had personally arranged public transportation.

Aoife sat on her suitcase beside him with headphones around her neck and a recorder resting in her palm.

Samir had a sports bag.

Maya had too much luggage.

Ollie stopped in front of her largest case.

He looked at the baggage tag.

Then at the case dimensions.

Then at Maya.

“No.”

“You don’t know what’s in it.”

“It exceeds what I personally would want to move through three transfer stages.”

“That is not the same as too much.”

“It is to me.”

Samir adjusted the strap on his sports bag.

Ollie looked at it.

“Futsal?”

“Local variant. Maybe.”

“That seems worth bringing.”

Maya stared at both of them.

“Interesting.”

Aoife lifted the recorder but did not switch it on.

“You’re late,” Ethan told Ollie.

Ollie checked the departure board.

“I am twenty-three minutes early.”

“You said thirty.”

Ollie thought about explaining Mina, branch access and the wheel vibration.

He looked at Ethan’s face.

“Maker bay.”

Ethan nodded immediately.

That was enough.

Ollie set down his equipment case.

For the first time since leaving the workshop, something in him loosened.

The title the program used for them was still ridiculous.

Experimental Human Youth Cohort — Limited Access.

Maya called it the longest possible way to say supervised teenagers.

But the five of them had already been through enough together that standing under bad terminal lighting with snacks and bags felt almost normal.

Ethan looked around.

“Okay. First document check in twenty-one minutes. Dublin transfer windows are synced. Vela host assignments changed this morning because Ring Three maintenance shifted one central connection, so I made—”

Maya said, “No.”

Ethan stopped. “You don’t know what I made.”

“A schedule.”

“It is not just a schedule.”

Aoife looked up from her recorder. “Did you schedule any of my work?”

Ethan hesitated.

Aoife closed her eyes.

Samir took out his phone and checked his own calendar.

Ollie leaned slightly toward Ethan’s screen.

“What are the assumptions on transfer time?”

Ethan brightened. “Exactly. See? Ollie understands.”

Ollie looked at the colored blocks.

He read three of them.

Then four.

Then he noticed Aoife watching him.

“What?”

“Nothing,” she said.

He looked back at the schedule.

Ethan had put all five people into the same dinner block every night despite three different placement zones, Samir’s sport trial and a host obligation Maya had already mentioned twice.

Ollie pointed at the screen.

“This breaks on day one.”

Ethan’s face changed.

Maya smiled.

“That,” she said, “is why he’s my favorite engineer.”

“I am not your engineer.”

“No one said you were.”

The boarding call sounded before Ethan could revise anything.

They moved toward document control.

For less than a minute they were physically one group again, bags rolling beside them, Maya still defending her luggage, Ethan trying to explain that the schedule was a draft.

Then the first checkpoint divided them.

Different health-clearance queues.
Different equipment screening.
Different transfer prep.
Different host codes.

Aoife went left.

Samir straight ahead.

Maya was redirected because of something in one of her bags she refused to identify.

Ethan asked whether they could regroup past screening and was told they could regroup if their routes happened to permit it.

Ollie was sent right with the equipment case.

He looked back once.

Aoife caught his eye from the other line and tilted her head toward Ethan, who was still asking the agent a question.

Ollie understood the gesture a moment late.

He smiled anyway.

Then a door closed between them.

His wrist updated.

DUBLIN TRANSFER — STAGE 1

Below that:

VELA RESIDENCY
HABITAT SYSTEMS / MAINTENANCE PLACEMENT
REPORT 06:10 LOCAL STATION TIME

A technician at the next station held out her hand for his equipment case.

Ollie gave it to her.

For half a second his fingers stayed on the handle after hers had closed around it.

Then he let go.
