# 2. CHAPTER 2 — YOUR CALL

## PATCH 2A — hold-scope list compression

The chapter needs the scope, but it should not feel like the reader is being trained for certification.

### Replacement principle

Keep all five substantive trigger categories, but move from isolated policy-style enumeration into one paragraph attached to Ethan's reading experience:

> The hold was narrow enough to be real. He could use it for an unreviewed participant-safety risk, a scope violation, a material mismatch between declared and inferred preference, failure of required participant override, or a restricted action outside the approved youth condition. Everything else remained outside his authority.

**Protect:** what the hold does *not* stop; that cost is necessary for later climax fairness.

## PATCH 2B — accountability turn

Keep in full scene-time:

- Voss: a correct hold can still cause harm;
- Ethan: `So if we use it, we own those consequences.`
- Voss distinguishes responsibility for justified decision from sole causation;
- Samir asks what happens if Ethan does nothing;
- no-hold is also logged.

This is the focal point. Do not compress to summary.

# 8. BLOCK PROBLEM THAT MUST BE FIXED FOR CH7–10

Chapters 2, 3, 4 and 6 are all professional/institutional test environments. Individually they work; together they risk creating one repeated discourse rhythm:

`briefing → model → test → discussion → lesson`.

Therefore Chapters 7–10 must not simply add four more audits.

Mandatory alternation:

`PUBLIC/SOCIAL CONSEQUENCE → AUDIT RESPONSE → ORDINARY-LIFE COLLISION → LIVE SYSTEM BENEFIT/COST → DECISION`.

At least one chapter must place the Lattice problem into a youth arena where reputation, friendship, family, school, transport, money, media or ordinary obligation is at stake.

No filler: the ordinary-life event must generate evidence or change the audit.
