## PATCH A — FIRST HUMAN COHORT REJECTION → LESSON ZERO CHOICE

Rowe glanced at something on her screen. “You mentioned in your message that you were interested in the educational material, not only professional placement.”

“I am.”

“Then you may want to look at the youth developmental-access layer. Public modules are already available. Supervised modules require separate consent and eligibility.”

“Is that the thing called Lesson Zero?”

Her eyes returned to him. “Among other things.”

“What is it?”

“If I explain it before you take it, I’ll reduce its value.”

“So it’s a test.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You didn’t say it wasn’t.”

For the first time, Rowe smiled slightly. “You are persistent.”

Ethan heard Aoife shift outside the open door, but she did not interrupt. Rowe waited, giving him no hint that there was a better question hidden somewhere behind the obvious ones. He looked back at the screen.

“Lesson Zero. Can I take it?”

“If you meet the public youth-access requirements, yes.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.”

“No recommendation? No special invitation?”

“No.”

Good. That should have made it better. Instead the answer landed with an unexpected little drop in his stomach: there was no exception, but there was also no sign that anyone had noticed him enough to make one.

The call ended four minutes later.

Aoife came in carrying two vending-machine coffees that tasted like warm roof insulation and set one in front of him.

“So?”

“So no.”

“And Lesson Zero?”

Ethan peeled the plastic lid from the cup and looked at the gray-brown liquid. “It’s a youth module.”

“It’s not the cohort,” Aoife said.

“No.”

“It might be basic.”

“Could be.”

He wanted her to talk him into it. She did not, which was irritating mainly because he knew exactly why she was refusing.

By six, all five of them were at their usual table in the café across from the station, because apparently being rejected from humanity’s first professional inter-civilizational cohort did not exempt anyone from buying fries. Maya had Lesson Zero open on her tablet; Ollie was reading over her shoulder while Samir ate fries Ethan had technically paid for and everyone else had technically stolen.

“It says adaptive collaborative entry module,” Maya said.

“It doesn’t tell us what we actually do,” Ollie said.

Maya was already opening the access requirements.

Samir reached for another fry. “So take it.”

Ethan stared at the page. “I don’t want a children’s version of the thing I actually wanted.”

“Then don’t.”

Aoife looked at Samir, then back at Ethan. She was going to make him answer for himself too. Apparently this was now a group policy.

Samir shrugged. “You wanted to learn how they train people.”

“Yes.”

“Here is a thing where they train people.”

“Kids.”

“You are a kid.”

“I am seventeen.”

“That’s a kind of kid.”

Maya pointed at Samir. “Careful. I’m fifteen.”

“You are also a kid.”

Maya opened her mouth, reconsidered the argument, and stole one of Ethan’s fries instead.

Samir took another. “You wanted the learning or the badge?”

Ethan almost answered too quickly. That was the annoying part: Samir had found the exact place where the argument stopped being about the Administration.

He looked around the table. Maya was already scrolling through access times. Ollie had found the technical requirements. Aoife was pretending not to watch him decide. Samir had salt on his sleeve.

The adult cohort was still gone; no interpretation fixed that. But the door in front of him was real, and refusing it would not make the other one reopen.

“When?” Ethan asked.

Maya grinned. “Saturday.”

### Patch rationale

- Preserved all causal beats.
- Merged isolated narration that represented one continuous cognitive unit.
- Kept dialogue short where turn-taking is the rhythm.
- Replaced generic emphasis fragments with Ethan-specific interpretation: exception, recognition, rules, argument structure.
- Kept one decisive paragraph break before `When?` so the choice retains weight.

## PATCH B — INSERT 7C: SIMILARITIES AND DIFFERENCES

“What was the fifth thing?” Maya asked.

“I don’t know yet.”

“Excellent card.”

Ethan ignored her. The point was to remove the names that made his brain jump ahead: no relational-state mapping, no Lesson Zero, no Insert 7C, no Confederation. Just behavior they could defend from the records.

Ollie had the old diagram beside him and their permitted Lesson Zero participation record open on a tablet. The record exposed no system internals—only states they had personally encountered, their own interactions, and the completion log. Rowe had been explicit about the boundary.

“We are not reverse-engineering restricted educational infrastructure,” she had said.

Ethan had told her he understood. She had looked at him for several seconds, so he had repeated it with more sincerity.

Now Maya moved two cards around. “The old insert is simpler.”

“Obviously.”

“No, I mean actually simpler. Look.” She put ACTOR ORDER MATTERS beside the copied 7C diagram, then beside their Lesson Zero log. “In this, there are six named states and two participant positions. It’s basically a representation. In Lesson Zero the room changed what each of us could even see.”

Ollie nodded. “Different implementation category.”

“Which does not mean different idea,” Ethan said.

“Also does not mean same idea,” Aoife said.

“I know.”

She raised an eyebrow.

“I do.”

Samir had been quiet, looking between the cards and the completion log. Ethan waited longer than felt natural, which he was beginning to recognize as something Samir occasionally required from him.

Samir tapped the second card.

“Available state depends on prior participant action?” Ethan asked.

“Yes.”

“What about it?”

Samir pulled the Lesson Zero tablet closer without changing screens. “When Maya crossed the line the first time, the room changed for her. When she did almost the same thing after Ollie changed the block, it changed for everybody differently.”

Ethan remembered the sequence. “State depended on order.”

“And who had already done something.”

“Right.”

Samir pointed at the 7C diagram. “This is doing that on paper.”

Nobody spoke for a moment. There it was—not proof, not even a match, but a real structural resemblance stated without any of the words that had made the resemblance exciting. Ethan felt his pulse speed up anyway and wrote the fifth card:

**STATE IS RELATIONAL, NOT ONLY INDIVIDUAL.**

Aoife watched him finish. “Now write the differences.”

He looked at her. “Why are you like this?”

“Professional commitment.”

Maya took three more cards before he could complain. “Lesson Zero adapts in real time.”

Ollie added, “7C is a research representation in the material we have. No evidence it controls an environment.”

Aoife wrote:

**LESSON ZERO USES MULTIPLE INFORMATION CHANNELS; 7C DOCUMENT DOES NOT SHOW THAT.**

Samir said, “We don’t know if the old method changed based on physiology.”

Ethan added:

**NO EVIDENCE 7C DOES.**

Soon the table had two clusters: similarities and differences. The similarities survived the comparison. So did the differences. Ethan preferred that, even though it was less convenient. If every new fact had pointed in the same direction, he would have trusted the pattern less, not more.

Ollie rotated the old photocopy under the reading lamp. “There’s something under the revision line.”

Bell looked up from the desk. “Do not change the lamp angle.”

Ollie removed his hand immediately. “I wasn’t.”

“You were thinking about it.”

Aoife covered a smile. Bell came over and adjusted the lamp herself.

### Patch rationale

- Preserved the fair-evidence mechanism: similarities survive, differences survive.
- Converted `Not proof. / Not even a match.` from repeated isolated emphasis into one controlled sentence while retaining the epistemic restraint.
- Made waiting for Samir a developing character/relationship beat rather than neutral filler.
- Made Ethan’s approval of counterevidence part of his systems-oriented POV rather than an authorial comment.
- Maintained short standalone card text because graphic separation is functional, not accidental chop.

## PATCH C — GLOBAL APPLICATION RULE FOR THE NEXT FULL ASSEMBLY

Do not run a blind `join short paragraphs` operation.

For each narration break classify:

`SPEAKER CHANGE | NEW ACTION UNIT | FOCUS SHIFT | TIME/SPACE SHIFT | IMPACT | REVELATION | GRAPHIC/OBJECT TEXT | RHYTHMIC RELEASE | ACCIDENTAL CHOP`

Only `ACCIDENTAL CHOP` is automatically merged. The replacement paragraph must then receive a syntax-variation pass so it does not become merely a long chain of simple sentences.
