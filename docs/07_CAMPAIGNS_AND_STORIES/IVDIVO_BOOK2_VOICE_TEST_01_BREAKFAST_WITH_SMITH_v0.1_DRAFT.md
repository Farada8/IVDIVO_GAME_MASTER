# PROTOTYPE SCENE

Mara’s father had three rules about breakfast: sit down, eat something with protein, and do not pretend a message had failed to arrive when he could see the notification reflected in the microwave door.

She hated the third one most.

“I was going to answer her.”

Daniel Keene slid a plate across the island without looking at Mara’s phone. Egg, toast, sliced pear. The exact breakfast she had eaten willingly twice in the last month, which apparently qualified it as a preference worth preserving indefinitely.

“I didn’t say you weren’t.”

“You made the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you decide silence is more efficient than accusing me of something.”

He considered that. Her father considered almost everything, which was one of the reasons arguing with him felt like filing a complaint against a building.

“I was making coffee.”

“You can do two things.”

“I can do several.”

That was probably a joke. With him, it was sometimes hard to tell.

Mara sat because if she stayed standing he would eventually ask whether she was late, and then she would have to admit she was not late, she was avoiding breakfast, which would turn avoiding breakfast into a topic.

The kitchen wall was running the morning feed with sound low. A reporter stood inside one of the new orbital residential cylinders, hair lifting slightly whenever she moved too quickly in the unfinished gravity section. Behind her, workers in pale pressure coveralls guided a prefabricated garden frame into place.

ASTRA HABITAT THREE — FIRST FAMILY RESIDENTIAL SPINE OPENS IN NOVEMBER

The lower caption changed.

CONFEDERATION MATERIALS SUPPORT PACKAGE CLEARS HUMAN SAFETY REVIEW

Then, because apparently the world had decided not to be subtle anymore:

APPLICATIONS FOR SECONDARY-SCHOOL TRANSFER PROGRAM OPEN MONDAY

Mara reached for her toast.

Her phone buzzed again.

**Mom:** I am not asking you to decide today. I am asking you to look at the campus tour before deciding you hate it.

A second bubble appeared.

**Mom:** Also this is me NOT PRESSURING YOU, in case your father is narrating it differently.

Mara smiled before she could stop herself.

Daniel set his coffee down.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Your mother?”

“Do you have cameras in my face now?”

“You smile differently at messages from your mother.”

That killed the smile.

He noticed.

Of course he noticed.

“I’m not monitoring you, Mara.”

“You just identified who texted me from my mouth.”

“I raised you.”

“For sixteen years. People with normal dads still get privacy after sixteen years.”

“You have privacy.”

She looked at him.

He looked back.

There was no challenge in his expression. That almost made it worse. Other fathers got defensive. Other fathers said ridiculous things like while you live under my roof. Other fathers lost their temper and had to apologize later.

Daniel Keene simply waited as if privacy were a term they could define correctly if given enough time.

Mara stabbed a piece of pear with her fork.

“Sloane’s party moved.”

“I know.”

Her fork stopped halfway to her mouth.

He took a drink of coffee.

“How?”

“Her mother sent the updated address to the parent list at 22:14.”

Mara lowered the fork.

“There is a parent list?”

“There are parents.”

“Not the same thing.”

“It is from a safeguarding form your school required after the Contact Week incident last spring.”

“That was one broken window.”

“It was seven.”

“Only one was near us.”

“That is an interesting standard.”

She hated when he said things were interesting. It meant he had already decided they were wrong but wanted to give them enough room to become wrong on their own.

The feed changed again. A Confederation representative was answering questions beside a human transport minister. The representative’s body had been designed for Earth gravity and atmosphere; everyone at school knew that much. Half the comments online were still arguments about whether “designed body” meant suit, clone, robot, temporary person, or all four depending on who was talking.

Mara had watched three explainer videos and understood less afterward.

A ticker ran below them.

COGNITIVE LIBERTY BILL RETURNS TO COMMITTEE AFTER MENTAL-INFLUENCE HEARING

She looked away before her father could notice her reading it.

“Can I still go?” she asked.

“Yes.”

That came too easily.

“To the party.”

“I understood the question.”

“Until?”

“One.”

“Sloane said two.”

“Sloane does not set your return time.”

“She sets the party time.”

“Then you will miss the last hour.”

Mara stared at him. “You know you could occasionally pretend to think about things before saying no.”

“I said yes.”

“You built the no into the yes.”

He almost smiled.

Almost.

She caught it anyway, because she was good at catching small changes in him. She had needed to become good at it. With her mother, emotion arrived in weather systems. With Daniel, it appeared in millimeters.

“One-thirty.”

Mara blinked.

“What?”

“One-thirty. Location sharing stays active. If the venue changes again, you tell me before you move. If anyone brings neural recreational equipment, you leave.”

“That is not a thing people say.”

“It is a thing people should say.”

“Nobody calls it neural recreational equipment.”

“What do you call it?”

“Depends what it is.”

“Then my term has the advantage of including all of it.”

She laughed despite herself.

He looked faintly satisfied, which annoyed her enough to stop.

“Fine. One-thirty.”

“Good.”

“And you are not coming to pick me up unless I ask.”

“If you are safe and transportation remains available.”

“That is not the same as yes.”

“It is more accurate than yes.”

There he was.

Mara picked up her phone and finally opened the link from her mother.

A clean white page filled the screen.

**ASTRA THREE — YOUTH RESIDENTIAL CAMPUS**

A virtual model unfolded above the glass: school modules, a sports ribbon, gardens, a long curved residential street under an artificial sky. The promotional camera swept upward until the curvature of the habitat became visible and houses seemed to rise in the distance instead of vanishing behind a horizon.

It was beautiful.

She resented it immediately.

Her mother had been working on orbital habitation law for eleven months. At first it had meant late calls and travel. Then it meant an office at the launch coordination center. Then, two weeks ago, it meant an offer.

Two years on Astra Three.

Family residence included.

Mara could finish school there.

Her mother had said it like that was an ordinary sentence now.

Finish school in orbit.

At school, people were already dividing themselves into categories no one had used five years ago. Earthbound. Transfer. Contact-track. Habitat-born, even though almost nobody their age actually was yet. Sloane called everyone who acted superior about getting orbital clearance “upstairs people,” despite the fact that her own family had been approved before anyone else in their group.

Mara rotated the model with one finger.

“You looked at it,” Daniel said.

She locked her phone.

“That was the deal.”

“I did not make a deal.”

“Mom did.”

He nodded once.

That was all.

Mara waited.

Nothing.

“You don’t care if I go?”

He looked at her now.

The question had changed the room. She could tell because he put the cup down instead of answering with it in his hand.

“I care.”

“Very moving.”

“You asked whether I care. I answered.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes.”

“Then answer that.”

He was quiet long enough that she wished she had not asked.

“You should see it before you decide,” he said.

“That is Mom’s answer.”

“It is a reasonable answer.”

“So you want me to go.”

“I want you to have enough information to choose.”

Mara pushed the plate away.

“Sometimes I think you would be happier if I were a form.”

His expression changed.

Not much.

Enough.

She regretted it immediately and refused to show that she regretted it, which was a skill she had inherited from neither parent and perfected entirely on her own.

Daniel looked toward the dark window over the sink.

The kitchen lights reflected in the glass. So did he.

For a second Mara saw something behind his reflection.

Not a person.

Not exactly a shadow either.

A dark vertical distortion seemed to stand a fraction wider than his shoulders, as though the glass had remembered a different outline and failed to erase it completely.

Mara turned.

There was nothing behind him except the refrigerator and the pale wall.

When she looked back at the window, the reflection was normal.

Her father was watching her.

“What?” she said.

“You stopped breathing.”

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“For how long?”

“Approximately three seconds.”

“Normal people say ‘a second.’”

“It was closer to three.”

She rubbed at one eye.

“Headache?” he asked.

“No.”

“Visual disturbance?”

Mara’s hand stopped.

It was the speed of the question that bothered her.

Not concern first. Classification first.

“No.”

He watched her for another moment.

She stood, picked up her bag and took the toast because otherwise he would point out she had eaten almost nothing.

“I’m leaving.”

“You have eleven minutes before you need to.”

“I know how clocks work.”

“Your coat.”

She had already reached the hall.

Mara came back, grabbed the coat from the chair and pulled it on hard enough to make the zipper catch.

Daniel did not move to help.

Good.

At the door she looked back.

He was clearing her plate.

Ordinary kitchen. Ordinary father. Confederation news muttering from the wall. An orbital city turning slowly above the counter on her abandoned phone screen.

Nothing wrong anywhere.

Then the kettle’s black glass caught his reflection as he crossed behind it.

For less than a blink, the reflection moved after he did.

Mara opened the door.

She was halfway down the front steps before she realized she had left her phone on the kitchen island.

Behind her, the door opened.

Her father held it out to her.

He had not needed to call her back.

He had known she would notice.

“Thanks,” Mara said.

Daniel Keene gave her the phone.

“Have a good day.”

She took it without touching his hand.

## VOICE-TEST NOTES

**What this scene tests:**

- ordinary father/daughter friction before plot;
- Contact and Orbit as normal background, not exposition;
- Mara can be funny without becoming quip-machine;
- Smith is competent, controlled, attentive and unsettling without acting like a cartoon villain;
- early orbital move is emotionally personal through mother;
- first anomaly is small and deniable;
- Smith's immediate diagnostic question hints at professional knowledge;
- no explanation of Matrix, reincarnation, Hierarchy or Smith's true age.

**Red Team watch:** If Mara's sarcasm density stays this high in every scene, voice will flatten. Later scenes need embarrassment, warmth, silence, desire, physical activity and moments where she is simply wrong.

**Next prototype:** School social map + Sloane reveals the orbital move is no longer hypothetical.
