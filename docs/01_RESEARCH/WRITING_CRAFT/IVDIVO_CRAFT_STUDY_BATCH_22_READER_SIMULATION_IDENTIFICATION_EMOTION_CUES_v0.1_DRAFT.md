# 1. SOURCE PASSPORT

Oatley treats fiction as a **guided simulation of human intentions, plans, actions, emotions and relationships**. The reader does not receive a complete world; the writer supplies cues from which the reader constructs a workable model.

Book architecture:

1. Fiction as Dream — model/world/simulation
2. Childhood Play — entrance into fictional worlds
3. Creativity — imagined worlds
4. Character, Action, Incident — mental models of persons and doings
5. Emotions — imagined scenes and concerns
6. Writing Fiction — cues for the reader
7. Effects of Fiction
8. Talking About Fiction — interpretation

Professional role in IVDIVO: **reader psychology / character causality / emotional engagement / worldbuilding selection / anti-exposition control**.

# 2. FICTION AS SELECTIVE SIMULATION

Oatley's central model is not `fiction = copy of reality`. Fiction is a selective world-model that foregrounds human intentions and plans and therefore naturally organizes itself around actions and incidents that arise from those actions.

## IVDIVO translation

A scene should not merely display a future technology or institution. It should let the reader simulate:

`PERSON WANTS SOMETHING`
→ `PERSON FORMS / FOLLOWS A PLAN`
→ `WORLD OR OTHER PERSON PUSHES BACK`
→ `ACTION OCCURS`
→ `RESULT CHANGES OPTIONS`
→ `EMOTION CHANGES BECAUSE A CONCERN WAS HELPED / HURT`
→ `NEXT PLAN`.

### Failure mode

`WORLD INFORMATION WITHOUT HUMAN PROJECT` becomes encyclopedia, demonstration copy or lore-tourism.

### Book Two validation

Rivergate works when technology is encountered through goals and friction: Ethan wants to understand/use bounded authority; Maya tests whether choice is real; Samir tests model/person boundaries; Aoife tests framing/consent; Ollie tests physical fallback. Rivergate is therefore stronger as enacted infrastructure than as a descriptive future-city chapter.

# 3. IDENTIFICATION IS GOAL-SIMULATION, NOT LIKING

Oatley proposes that identification occurs when readers temporarily suspend their own immediate goals and run a character's goals/plans/actions through their own planning machinery. Emotional response then tracks success, failure and consequences of those simulated plans.

This is more operationally useful than the weak instruction `make the hero likable`.

## IVDIVO Identification Gate

Before asking whether a protagonist is sympathetic, ask:

`CAN THE READER STATE THE CHARACTER'S CURRENT GOAL?`
`CAN THE READER SEE WHY IT MATTERS NOW?`
`CAN THE READER PREDICT WHAT SUCCESS/FAILURE WOULD FEEL LIKE?`
`DOES THE CHARACTER ACT TOWARD IT?`

A morally imperfect or irritating character can still hold reader identification if the goal is legible and the reader is allowed to mentally run the plan.

### Book Two fit

Ethan does not need to become softer or less ambitious to remain engaging. The early book works because his desire is concrete: he finally has legitimate authority and wants to prove he can carry it. The dramatic engine is then whether his actions under that goal create justified responsibility or repeat the old conversion of competence into control.

# 4. EMOTION AS EVENT × CONCERN

Oatley's emotion discussion repeatedly connects emotional response to what an event means for a person's active concern, goal or relation.

## Operational model

`EVENT`
→ `WHOSE CONCERN IS TOUCHED?`
→ `APPRAISAL: HELP / HARM / UNCERTAINTY / LOSS / GAIN / THREAT`
→ `EMOTION`
→ `ACTION TENDENCY`
→ `NEXT DECISION`.

### Anti-GPT consequence

Do not add emotional language merely because a scene is important. If no live concern is identifiable, the emotion will feel author-applied.

Conversely, a small event can carry strong emotion when it strikes a high-value concern.

Example in Book Two: `Priya says “Good” after Ethan uses the stop` matters not because the word is dramatic but because it touches Ethan's long-running concern: whether real authority can be legitimately entrusted to him.

# 5. CHARACTER → GOALS → PLANS → ACTIONS

Oatley's character model aligns strongly with Storr, McKee, Egri and Herman: character is not a static profile. Character differences generate different goals, plans and tactics, which generate different plots.

## IVDIVO causality test

For each core character:

`TRAIT / MODEL / HISTORY`
→ `WHAT THEY NOTICE`
→ `WHAT THEY WANT`
→ `DEFAULT TACTIC`
→ `WORLD RESPONSE`
→ `SPECIFIC CONSEQUENCE`.

If swapping two character names leaves the same plan and consequence intact, characterization is not causally deep enough.

### Book Two validation

The first three chapters currently pass this well in several places:
- Ethan attends to authorization scope, thresholds and responsibility;
- Maya attacks coercive framing and false choice;
- Samir separates person from model and declared from inferred relation;
- Aoife questions who wrote the optimization policy and what context a record implies;
- Ollie asks for cost, physical fallback and graceful degradation.

This cognitive division should remain an active scene-design rule, not just a character-bible description.

# 6. SHADOW / SUBSTANCE — BEHAVIOR IS NOT EXPLANATION

Oatley's Shakespeare discussion distinguishes visible action from what may lie beneath it without claiming that surface behavior is simply false. The artistic value lies in the relation between outer behavior and deeper motive/meaning.

This strengthens Herman's recent IVDIVO rule:

`OBSERVED / PREDICTED BEHAVIOR ≠ REASON ≠ CONSENT`.

For Lattice, the system may correctly predict accommodation while being wrong to treat that behavior as a complete model of why the person yields or whether the burden is legitimately allocatable.

# 7. RECOGNITION AS PAYOFF

Oatley emphasizes recognition as a recurring narrative effect: a person, relation or situation understood one way is later re-seen under a changed model.

## IVDIVO Recognition Ladder

A strong reveal should often do more than add a fact:

`EARLY MODEL`
→ `OBSERVED ANOMALY`
→ `PRESSURE / CONTRADICTION`
→ `RECOGNITION`
→ `OLD EVENTS CHANGE MEANING`
→ `NEW DECISION`.

### Mystery implication

A clue ladder should preserve enough distance between anomaly and recognition for the reader to participate. If the manuscript explains the pattern at first sight, recognition collapses into exposition.

This directly reinforces the existing Book Two reveal-ownership rule: Chapter 3 may establish `actor-order transition is familiar`, but the explicit R-14 comparison belongs later, after controlled comparison.

# 8. WRITER SUPPLIES CUES; READER BUILDS MODEL

Oatley's model of fiction as collaborative construction supports a strict anti-exposition principle.

## Cue Sufficiency Gate

For any explanatory paragraph, ask:

1. What model does the reader need **now**?
2. Which observable cue can let the reader construct it?
3. What can safely remain unspoken?
4. What uncertainty is productive?
5. What exact misunderstanding would occur if the cue is omitted?

The goal is neither obscurity nor maximal explanation. It is **minimum sufficient cueing for active reader construction**.

### Book Two examples already working

- the laminated authority badge;
- a route line splitting two companions;
- `COMPANION CONTINUITY: MAINTAINED` despite divergent instructions;
- a stop causing `EXPLICITLY COUPLED` mode;
- fallback routes becoming thicker and slower.

These enact the ethics of the system more effectively than abstract claims about agency.

# 9. CROSS-SOURCE COMPARISON

## Oatley + Herman
Herman: readers attribute reasons to persons in ecological/social context.  
Oatley: readers simulate goals/plans/actions and emotionally experience their success/failure.

**Synthesis:** reader attachment improves when the manuscript exposes enough of a person's active project for the reader to run it, while preserving uncertainty about deeper reasons where appropriate.

## Oatley + Storr
Storr: character's model of control generates characteristic tactics and plot.  
Oatley: character flows into goals/plans/actions; readers identify by running those plans.

**Synthesis:** character-specific causality is simultaneously a plot mechanism and a reader-engagement mechanism.

## Oatley + McKee
McKee: action is purposeful behavior in pursuit of desire; scenes turn values through conflict.  
Oatley: reader emotion arises as simulated plans succeed/fail relative to concerns.

**Synthesis:** scene value change should be traceable not only structurally but through a character concern the reader is currently simulating.

## Oatley + Rosenfeld
Rosenfeld: dramatize emotion through cues and embodied action.  
Oatley: cues prompt reader-constructed simulation and fresh emotion.

**Synthesis:** emotional specificity should be distributed across action, perception, image, speech and consequence rather than narrated as labels.

# 11. NEW PRODUCTION GATES

### Gate IU — Reader Simulation Legibility
For every major scene, the reader must be able to identify at least one active character goal/plan whose outcome can be mentally simulated.

### Gate IV — Identification Before Likability
Do not solve attachment problems by cosmetic likability until goal, stakes, plan and action legibility have been tested.

### Gate IW — Concern-to-Emotion Trace
Important emotion must be traceable to a live concern, relationship, goal or threatened self-model.

### Gate IX — Emotion Changes Action
Strong emotional beats should alter attention, tactic, decision or later behavior; otherwise test whether they are decorative.

### Gate IY — Character-Causal Plot
Core character differences must generate different plans/tactics and therefore different consequences.

### Gate IZ — Shadow/Substance Gap
Visible behavior may be accurate while its meaning/reason remains unresolved; do not collapse observation into explanation.

### Gate JA — Recognition Requires Prior Model
A reveal/recognition should revise an earlier reader/character model, not simply provide a first-time encyclopedia fact.

### Gate JB — Recognition Spacing
Do not spend anomaly, comparison, interpretation and proof in the same early beat when later inquiry depends on their separation.

### Gate JC — Minimum Sufficient Cue
Prefer concrete cues that let readers construct needed world/character models; add exposition only when a specific required inference would otherwise fail.

### Gate JD — Active Reader Construction
Productive uncertainty is allowed when the reader has enough cues to form a provisional model and a reason to want revision.

### Gate JE — Emotional Salience Through Participation
Relationship meaning should preferably arise from things characters do to/with each other, not from detached reports about the relationship.

### Gate JF — Simulation-to-Payoff Continuity
Climax payoff should complete or overturn concerns/plans the reader has actually been allowed to simulate earlier.
