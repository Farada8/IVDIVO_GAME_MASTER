## Primary contribution

Fiction is usefully treated as a **guided simulation** built jointly by writer and reader. It selectively models human intentions, plans, actions, incidents, emotions and relationships rather than reproducing a whole world.

The reader's identification is tied to taking on character goals/plans/actions and experiencing their successes and failures, not merely to liking the character.

## High-value mechanisms extracted

- selective world simulation;
- goal/plan/action reader identification;
- emotion as event appraised against concern;
- character → goals/plans/actions → plot;
- visible behavior vs deeper reason/meaning;
- recognition as revision of a prior model;
- writer supplies cues, reader constructs model;
- emotional salience increases through personally consequential interaction rather than detached description.

## Limits / failure modes

1. Oatley is not a complete prescriptive story-structure method.
2. Psychological findings and theoretical claims vary in evidential strength; craft rules must not be falsely presented as universal empirical laws.
3. Identification through goal simulation does not mean every POV must expose every motive.
4. `reader builds model` cannot justify deliberate vagueness; sufficient cues remain necessary.
5. Social/empathy effects of fiction are probabilistic and should not become promises about reader transformation.
6. Simulation terminology is a useful model, not a literal claim that narrative experience is computationally identical to a software simulator.
