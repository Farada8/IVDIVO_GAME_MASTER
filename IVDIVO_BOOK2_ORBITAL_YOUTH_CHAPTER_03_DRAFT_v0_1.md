# ORBITAL YOUTH
## BOOK TWO
### CHAPTER THREE — ARRIVAL WITHOUT A FLOOR

**Status:** WORKING DRAFT v0.1 / NOT CANON

Maya's first mistake in orbit lasted less than three seconds.

Unfortunately, twelve people saw it.

The transfer corridor from Dublin had delivered them into a broad arrival chamber that seemed, at first glance, designed by somebody who did not believe floors deserved exclusive rights to being underneath people.

There was a floor.

Mostly.

A pale band of it curved upward along the far wall and continued until it became, by degrees, something Maya's body insisted was a wall even while people walked across it.

Not climbed.

Walked.

Families, commuters and freight staff moved through the curve with the calm indifference of people crossing a road they had crossed too many times to admire.

Maya stopped just beyond the transfer gate.

Her stomach arrived a fraction of a second later.

A sign over the lane shifted into English as she approached.

ROTATIONAL ENTRY — BLUE BAND

MATCH WALKING PACE

DO NOT STOP IN TRANSITION

That last instruction appeared while Maya was already stopped.

Someone behind her said, "Seriously?"

She moved.

Too fast.

The floor changed under her before her balance did.

There was no dramatic drop, no weightlessness, nothing cinematic enough to justify what happened next. Her left foot came down where her body expected it to. Her right foot tried to correct for a sideways pull that was not exactly sideways. She stepped across the edge of the blue lane, clipped a painted divider and forced an older man with a shopping carrier to twist around her.

His carrier compensated better than Maya did.

A girl coming the other way caught Maya by the forearm.

"Blue means keep pace," she said.

Maya recovered immediately.

"I know."

The girl looked at where Maya was standing.

Then at Maya.

"Apparently not."

She let go.

Maya felt heat climb into her face.

The older man had already continued. So had everyone else.

That should have helped.

It did not.

"First day?" the girl asked.

She was about Maya's age, maybe a little older. Dark hair cut short at the jaw. Loose gray trousers. A jacket with three narrow reflective strips at one shoulder and a local transit patch Maya did not recognize.

Maya looked at the transfer gate behind her.

"What gave it away?"

The girl considered that.

"Stopping in the transition lane."

"Right."

"And then saying you knew."

Maya stared at her.

The girl smiled—not cruelly, which was somehow worse—and stepped into the curve.

Within four paces she was walking at an angle Maya's inner ear still refused to accept.

Then she was gone into the traffic above.

Maya stood very carefully inside the blue band.

Her wrist buzzed.

ETHAN: Transfer complete?

She typed:

MAYA: obviously

A second message arrived before she sent anything else.

AOIFE: define complete

MAYA: in orbit

OLLIE: same

SAMIR: where

Maya looked around.

The chamber had seven exits, three visible levels, two lanes that became walls, and one central shaft through which passenger capsules moved without any obvious concern for what direction counted as horizontal.

MAYA: somewhere extremely intuitive

ETHAN: send route tag

Maya did not.

Not because she needed help.

Because she had been on the station for approximately four minutes and the idea of Ethan locating her through a route tag felt unbearable.

She opened the city map instead.

VELA TRANSFER HUB — ARRIVAL RING

HOST RESIDENCE: EAST RESIDENTIAL 4

RECOMMENDED ROUTE: SPINE C / TRANSFER 3 / ER4 LOCAL

ESTIMATED TIME: 31 MIN

Maya zoomed out.

The map did not become simpler.

Vela looked like several circles that had disagreed about sharing a center and compromised by attaching themselves to a long central spine. Some rings were full circles. Others were partial arcs. Transit lines connected them at points that moved relative to one another on the live map.

The recommended route went away from East Residential 4 before curving back toward it.

Maya frowned.

That was stupid.

She zoomed farther.

A shorter connection was visible through a neighboring transfer hub.

Nine minutes less by distance.

The route system marked it:

NOT RECOMMENDED FOR CURRENT ADAPTATION PROFILE

Maya read it twice.

Not forbidden.

Not closed.

Not restricted.

Not recommended.

She smiled.

---

Ten minutes later, Maya discovered that "not recommended" was an extremely flexible phrase.

It could mean: you are allowed.

It could also mean: nobody is going to stop you from doing something irritating to yourself.

The shorter route began well.

That was part of the problem.

She found the connector without help, entered a narrow local transit cabin with six other passengers and watched the transfer clock count down from forty seconds.

The cabin doors closed.

A woman beside her was arguing softly with someone on a call about whether a child had remembered a sports harness.

A boy in school clothes was eating something green from a folded paper tray.

A man with a maintenance case leaned against the wall with his eyes closed.

Nobody appeared concerned that the cabin was about to leave one rotating structure and cross into another.

Maya decided not to be concerned either.

The cabin moved.

For the first few seconds there was only vibration.

Then the pressure through her feet reduced.

Her body lightened.

Not weightlessness. Training had taught her the difference, and Maya intended to respect at least one thing she had learned today.

The cabin crossed the central spine.

For seven seconds, the floor stopped making a convincing argument that it was down.

The boy with the green food hooked one shoe under a rail without looking.

The woman on the call shifted her stance half a step.

The maintenance man did not open his eyes.

Maya gripped a handle a little harder than necessary.

Then weight returned.

Different.

Her knees adjusted badly.

The doors opened.

A local sign read:

TRANSFER 3 — OUTER ARC

ADAPTATION HOLD MAY APPLY

Maya walked out before her body had finished recalculating.

A soft tone sounded behind her.

Her wrist flashed amber.

ADAPTATION CHECK

PLEASE PAUSE IN MARKED ZONE

She kept walking two more steps.

The amber became red.

PLEASE PAUSE.

"Fine," Maya muttered.

A rectangular patch of floor lit beside the lane.

She stepped into it.

Thirty seconds, she thought.

Maybe a minute.

The system checked her balance, pupil response, pulse, recent transfer history and whatever else the Youth Cohort medical profile allowed it to check.

A countdown appeared.

03:40

Maya stared.

"No."

The countdown did not negotiate.

A group of local students came through the corridor laughing and walked directly past the adaptation zone.

None of them stopped.

One was the girl from the blue band.

Maya looked away before the girl could see her.

Then looked back because that was worse.

The girl had seen her.

She paused.

For one terrible second, Maya expected recognition.

Instead the girl looked at the red adaptation border, then at Maya's transfer jacket.

"You took Three?"

Maya folded her arms.

"Yes."

"From arrival?"

"Yes."

The girl glanced at the route board overhead.

"Why?"

"It's shorter."

The girl looked at her for another second.

Then she laughed.

Not loudly. Just once.

"Okay."

She started walking again.

Maya called after her.

"It is shorter."

The girl turned while still moving.

"Geometrically."

"That's usually what shorter means."

"Not here."

And then she was gone again.

Maya watched the corridor swallow her.

The countdown read 02:51.

Her wrist buzzed.

ETHAN: We are trying to regroup before host dispersal.

MAYA: busy

ETHAN: with what

Maya looked at the red adaptation border.

MAYA: local culture

Samir sent a single question mark.

Aoife sent:

AOIFE: record it

Maya typed:

MAYA: absolutely not

The adaptation zone released her after three minutes and forty seconds exactly.

Her route system recalculated.

ESTIMATED ARRIVAL: 36 MIN

Maya stopped.

The recommended route from the original transfer hub had been thirty-one.

She had spent eleven minutes proving that nine minutes was less than fourteen.

There were moments when mathematics became personally insulting.

---

By the time Maya reached East Residential 4, she had learned three things.

First: in Vela, a map showed where things were, not how long they took to become accessible to you.

Second: locals used phrases like "nearby" with a moral flexibility that should have been illegal.

Third: nobody cared that she had come from Earth.

That one surprised her most.

Not nobody, literally.

At the residence intake desk, a small boy had stared at the Earth transfer mark on her luggage and asked if the ocean really moved all the time.

Maya had said yes.

He had asked why.

She had said gravity.

He had looked disappointed.

But nobody had gathered around. Nobody had asked about Lesson Zero. Nobody had recognized the Youth Cohort patch unless they were already part of the host system.

In Northern Virginia, Contact had made them locally visible almost by accident.

In Vela, the station contained so many kinds of normal that Maya's particular normal had lost market value.

She was still deciding whether this was insulting or wonderful when her host opened the door.

"You're late," the woman said.

Maya's wrist showed that she was eleven minutes past the assigned arrival window.

"The route changed."

The woman looked at her route history.

Not secretly. The host system displayed the permitted arrival verification between them.

"You changed it."

Maya took off her jacket.

"Technically, yes."

"Technically is usually where the interesting part lives."

She stepped aside.

"I'm Nara. Come in before you make the corridor responsible too."

Maya liked her immediately and decided not to show it.

The residence was smaller than the pictures had made it look.

Not cramped.

Dense.

Every surface seemed to have more than one job. The entry wall stored shoes, outer layers, emergency masks and two folding seats. A narrow table extended from the kitchen partition only when Nara touched a control. The main room curved gently enough that Maya noticed only because a cup left on the table did not align quite the way her eye expected with the doorway.

There were plants along one wall.

Real ones.

A strip of darker material ran beneath them and disappeared into the floor.

"Food garden?" Maya asked.

"Humidity buffer," Nara said. "And basil."

"Mostly basil?"

"Emotionally, yes."

A second voice called from farther inside.

"She took Transfer Three, didn't she?"

Maya closed her eyes briefly.

Nara smiled.

"My son. Sixteen. He has developed a statistical interest in planetary arrivals."

"He can stop."

"I'll alert the journal."

A boy appeared from the corridor carrying a sports bag.

He looked at Maya, at her Earth luggage, then at Nara.

"Three?"

"Go away," Maya said.

He grinned.

"I'm Kellan."

"Still go away."

"Good start."

He left through another door before she could decide if that had been an insult.

Nara pointed down the corridor.

"Your room is the last one. Bathroom is shared with Kellan. Kitchen is shared with everyone. Quiet cycle begins at twenty-three thirty unless we agree otherwise. Environmental overrides are not toys. If you change your sleep schedule, tell the house system. If you need more privacy, ask before assuming there isn't a way to create it."

Maya walked toward the room.

"What do I have to report?"

Nara leaned against the wall.

"To me?"

"To you, the host system, whoever is pretending not to supervise us."

"That's three different questions."

"Fine. Start with you."

Nara considered her.

Maya had expected the usual adult calculation: age, risk, responsibility, how much trouble could this teenager cause if given three meters of unsupervised corridor.

Instead Nara asked, "What do you want to do?"

The question was almost suspicious.

"Go out."

"Obviously."

"Late."

"Also obviously."

"Without asking permission every time I change locations."

"Good. Anything difficult?"

Maya stopped walking.

"You're serious."

"Very."

"My parents were told the host rules were strict."

"They are."

"This does not sound strict."

"You think strict means I choose where you go. I think strict means we agree what information becomes necessary when your choices can affect other people's response."

Maya's guard went up immediately.

"So tracking."

"No."

"Location reporting."

"Sometimes."

"Which is tracking."

Nara shook her head.

"The house does not need to know which table you're sitting at, who you're flirting with, what you bought, what music you're hearing or whether you ignored your messages because you were angry. I do not need live coordinates."

Maya waited.

There was always a second half.

"I need to know which residential or public zone you're in if you are not coming back when you said you would. I need to know whether you're sleeping here or in another registered host residence. If a transit segment closes, I need a truthful route condition from you—not a story designed to keep adults calm."

Maya looked at her.

"That's it?"

"No."

There it was.

"If you enter a controlled work environment, medical zone or restricted transfer layer, different rules apply because those are not my rules. If you are injured, the system will share what it is legally required to share. If you vanish during an emergency, people will look for you. You don't get a privacy exemption from being missing."

Maya almost smiled.

"Fair."

"I know."

"You say that confidently."

"I have teenagers."

"One teenager."

"Kellan has friends. It multiplies."

Maya reached her room.

The door opened to her host profile.

The space beyond was narrow but actually private.

A bed folded partly into the wall. Storage compartments were built into the curve. One section of the ceiling showed a live exterior view: not a window, but a low-latency wall surface linked to a camera on the outer residential skin.

Earth was not visible.

Stars were.

Maya put her bag down.

She tried not to react.

Nara noticed anyway and had the decency not to comment.

"Your parents asked for a twenty-two thirty check-in," Nara said.

Maya turned.

"Absolutely not."

"I told them that was unrealistic."

Maya blinked.

"You did?"

"Yes."

"What did they say?"

"Your father asked whether orbital transport was really operating after twenty-two thirty."

Maya covered her face with one hand.

"Please tell me you didn't answer."

"I sent him the public transit schedule."

"That's worse."

"I enjoyed it."

Maya lowered her hand.

"What did you agree?"

"First week: message if your return changes by more than an hour. Tell me where you will sleep. Keep your zone status truthful during service disruptions. No live tracking unless there is a specific safety condition and you agree or emergency law requires it."

Maya stared at her.

"They agreed to that?"

"Eventually."

"How long did eventually take?"

"Forty-eight messages."

"I love you."

Nara's eyebrows lifted.

Maya pointed at her.

"That was administrative."

"Obviously."

"Do not make it weird."

"I'm a host parent. Making things weird is part of the certification."

Maya laughed before she could stop herself.

Nara pushed away from the wall.

"Dinner in twenty minutes. Kellan has training after. You don't have to come with us later, but if you want to see the local social spine before orientation, he'll probably pretend he doesn't want to show you."

From the other room, Kellan shouted, "I can hear you."

"Excellent," Nara called back.

Maya sat on the edge of the bed.

Her wrist buzzed again.

There were nineteen group messages.

Ethan had finally managed to identify everybody's host zone.

OLLIE: mine is not near any of you

AOIFE: define near

ETHAN: I am not doing this again

SAMIR: you are doing it now

Maya scrolled.

Ethan had proposed dinner.

Aoife had an intake recording block.

Samir had sport orientation.

Ollie had an early maintenance handover the next morning and a host meal he was expected to attend.

Maya had nothing yet.

That bothered her more than it should have.

Then Kellan appeared in the doorway.

"My friends are going to the inner social level after training," he said.

Maya looked up.

"Did your mother order you to invite me?"

"Yes."

"Good to know."

"She also said I should lie better, but I think we're building trust."

Maya liked him slightly more.

"What is the inner social level?"

He leaned against the frame.

"Food. Music. Courts. People pretending they're not waiting for the Night Transit Festival bookings to open."

"What kind of courts?"

"Depends which gravity section is free."

Maya looked at the group chat again.

Ethan had typed:

ETHAN: 19:00 tomorrow seems possible if everyone protects it now.

Aoife replied:

AOIFE: do not say protects it like we're negotiating a ceasefire

Ollie:

OLLIE: depends on handover

Samir:

SAMIR: practice until 18:30 outer ring

Maya typed:

MAYA: maybe

Ethan responded immediately.

ETHAN: What does maybe mean?

Maya looked at Kellan.

"How late?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"Whether you take Transfer Three."

She stared at him.

He smiled.

Maya looked back at the chat.

MAYA: it means maybe

She muted the group for one hour.

That felt fantastic.

---

Dinner was not alien.

Maya had somehow expected it to be.

Not because Nara or Kellan were nonhuman. They were not. Because every documentary about orbital life eventually showed somebody eating something grown in a wall under blue light while a narrator explained resource loops.

Nara made noodles.

Kellan burned part of the sauce because he was arguing with somebody through an audio channel about a team reservation.

Maya cut vegetables.

The basil from the humidity wall went into the food.

Nobody narrated it.

The kitchen system warned that one pan was heating unevenly.

Nara ignored it.

The system warned again.

"It's fine," Nara said.

Maya looked at the pan.

"Is it?"

"No."

"Then why—"

"Because I am cooking dinner, not commissioning a habitat."

Kellan laughed.

Nara pointed the spoon at him.

"You have lost sauce privileges."

"I made the sauce."

"Exactly."

Maya's wrist buzzed against her leg.

She did not look.

It buzzed again.

Kellan noticed.

"Planet emergency?"

"Ethan."

"Same thing?"

Maya looked at him.

"You don't know Ethan."

"I know the type."

"No, you don't."

"Schedules people?"

"That's not a type."

"It's absolutely a type."

Maya checked the message despite herself.

ETHAN: tomorrow 19:00 — hold unless work/sport changes

Then privately:

ETHAN: You okay? You vanished after arrival.

Maya's irritation weakened.

She hated when he did that.

Not the checking.

The part where the checking was sometimes actually about whether she was okay.

She typed:

MAYA: host family normal. station annoying. alive.

She considered adding:

I got lost.

Deleted it.

Then added:

MAYA: took a stupid route

Ethan's answer took longer this time.

ETHAN: you chose it yourself didn't you

MAYA: irrelevant

ETHAN: glad you're alive

Maya put the wrist down.

Kellan was looking at her.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You did a face."

"Apparently that's contagious."

Nara set the noodles on the table.

"Eat before the station rotates again."

Maya looked at her.

Nara held the expression for two seconds.

Then smiled.

"That was a joke."

"I knew that."

Kellan made a choking sound into his drink.

Maya pointed at him.

"I am going out with you only because I need local intelligence."

"Of course."

"Not because I need a guide."

"Definitely not."

"And if you tell anyone about Transfer Three—"

"I don't remember it."

Maya paused.

"What?"

"Transfer Three. I don't remember it."

He took another bite.

Maya narrowed her eyes.

"You weren't the girl."

Kellan stared at her.

Nara stared too.

There was a beat of silence.

Then Kellan said, very carefully, "No."

Maya closed her eyes.

Nara began laughing.

Not politely.

Kellan leaned back in his chair.

"There was a girl?"

"No."

"At Transfer Three?"

"Eat your noodles."

"Did she rescue you?"

"Nobody rescued me."

"Did you fall?"

"No."

"Almost?"

Maya stood up.

"I am changing host families."

Nara wiped her eyes.

"You just told your parents you loved me administratively."

"Administrative love is revocable."

Kellan was still smiling.

"What did the girl look like?"

Maya threw a napkin at him.

He caught it.

For the first time since the transfer gate, the embarrassment loosened enough to become funny.

Only a little.

But enough.

---

Later, after dinner, Maya stood alone in her room with the exterior view turned on.

She had expected stars to look different from orbit.

They did not.

That was almost disappointing.

The black around them did.

There was no atmosphere softening anything. No city haze. No horizon at the bottom of the image telling her where the universe stopped and home began.

Just points of light and, far off to one side, a piece of Vela's structure crossing the view like a dark line.

Her wrist showed the host agreement Nara had sent for acknowledgement.

FIRST-WEEK RETURN CONDITION:

— no continuous location feed required

— zone update required if return changes beyond agreed window

— sleeping location must remain truthful

— route condition must remain truthful during service interruption

— emergency location request subject to local safety law / explicit notification

Maya read it twice.

Then a third time.

She had spent months arguing for more independence at home.

There had always been an adult on the other side of the argument.

No, because you're sixteen.

No, because we don't know those people.

No, because the train stops running.

No, because we said so.

This was different.

Nara had said yes.

Yes, go.

Yes, stay out.

Yes, decide.

Just do not make other people act on false information when the system changes around you.

Maya should have loved that without qualification.

Instead it made the responsibility feel heavier.

She approved the agreement.

The room registered her acknowledgement and disappeared the text.

A message from Kellan arrived.

KELLAN: leaving in 12

Then:

KELLAN: blue lane

Maya stared at it.

She typed:

MAYA: die

He reacted with a heart.

Maya changed out of her transfer clothes.

She opened the group chat.

Ethan had added tomorrow's dinner to the shared calendar.

Aoife had renamed the event:

ETHAN ATTEMPTS SOCIAL CONTROL THROUGH FOOD

Samir had accepted.

Ollie had marked tentative.

Maya hovered over ACCEPT.

Then selected MAYBE.

She put on her jacket and left the room.

At the end of the corridor, Nara called from the kitchen.

"Which zone?"

Maya opened the door.

"Inner social level. Back before one unless I message."

"Sleeping here?"

"Yes."

"Good."

That was all.

No interrogation.

No tracker request.

No warning speech.

Maya stepped into the corridor.

The station curved away in both directions.

Somewhere inside it, four friends were unpacking into four other homes.

Somewhere below—or inward, or whatever direction Vela used when normal words stopped cooperating—Kellan was waiting.

And somewhere between the arrival hub and Transfer Three was a girl who had corrected Maya twice and apparently considered both events too ordinary to remember.

Maya found this intolerable.

She started walking.

This time, when the floor markings changed, she watched what the locals did first.

Then she chose her own lane.
