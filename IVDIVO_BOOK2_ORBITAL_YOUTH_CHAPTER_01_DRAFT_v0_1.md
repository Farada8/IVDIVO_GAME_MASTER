# ORBITAL YOUTH
## BOOK TWO
### CHAPTER ONE — THE LAST GROUND

**Status:** WORKING DRAFT v0.1 / NOT CANON

Ollie had been told three times not to touch the rover.

The first time had been polite.

The second time had included his name.

The third time Mina stood between him and the open service panel with both hands on her hips and said, “If you reach past me, I am calling your mother.”

Ollie looked at the rover.

The rover looked unfinished.

This was not because it was unfinished. It had passed drive, obstacle, comms and recovery tests yesterday. The new wheel assemblies were balanced. The left manipulator no longer twitched under load. The rescue cradle had survived Ethan standing in it while Maya drove the prototype over the practice ramp, which had not been part of the official test plan and had resulted in six minutes of shouting.

Technically, the rover was ready.

This was worse.

“There’s a vibration at forty-two percent front-left load,” Ollie said.

“There was a vibration.”

“There is still a vibration.”

Mina did not move.

Around them, the maker bay at Jefferson Regional Technical Center had the Sunday-evening emptiness of a place that belonged to teenagers only because the adults trusted them enough to leave the lights on. Half the workbenches were covered in sleeping projects. A climbing drone hung upside down from a gantry. Somebody had abandoned three lengths of printed structural foam on the floor with a note that said NOT TRASH, which meant they would remain there until graduation.

The rover sat on its maintenance blocks in the middle of Bay Four.

Its chassis was ugly in the deliberate way useful machines often were. Four independently articulated wheel units. Flat central body. Fold-out rescue cradle. Two manipulators. Sensor mast. Emergency beacon. No smooth white shell pretending it belonged in an advertisement about the future.

Ollie liked it more because of that.

Mina held up her tablet.

“Forty-two percent front-left load,” she said. “We tested it.”

“You repeated the curve?”

“I repeated the curve.”

“With the old compensator values?”

“With the new compensator values.”

“And the lateral bias?”

“Ollie.”

“I’m just asking.”

“You are standing six centimeters from the access panel.”

He looked down.

That was true.

He stepped back.

Mina lowered the tablet.

“You leave in fifty-three minutes.”

“Fifty-eight.”

“Your mother messaged me.”

Ollie stared at her.

Mina’s expression did not change.

“That is a hostile escalation.”

“You created the conditions.”

On the other side of the bay, Jonah laughed without looking up from a parts crate.

Ollie pointed at him.

“You are not involved in this.”

“I’m completely involved in this. I have money on whether you miss the orbital transfer because of a wheel vibration.”

“How much?”

“Fourteen dollars.”

“Who took the other side?”

“Your mother.”

Ollie looked at Mina again.

“She did not.”

“No,” Mina said. “But you believed it, which is enough.”

He put both hands in his jacket pockets.

This was tactical. It removed temptation.

Mina watched him do it and nodded as if confirming a successful containment procedure.

“There,” she said. “Growth.”

“I hate this team.”

“No, you don’t.”

“No.”

That was the problem.

The regional autonomous rescue challenge was in six days.

He would be in orbit.

Not metaphorically. Not watching a feed from some youth Contact center. Not in a training room where a Confederation interface adjusted itself around five teenagers and then refused to explain what it was doing.

Actually in orbit.

The first operational group of the Experimental Human Youth Cohort had been given a six-week supervised residency across several human orbital environments. Education, placements, shared projects, host assignments, cultural exchange, too many safety briefings and—according to Ethan—“the first serious opportunity to understand how a post-Contact human civilization is actually going to work.”

According to Maya, Ethan had practiced that sentence.

According to Ethan, he had not.

According to everyone else, that meant he had.

Ollie had been assigned to habitat systems and maintenance in Vela.

That part had been his first choice.

The rover had also been his first choice.

The universe had apparently decided to test whether he understood calendars.

Mina turned back to the workbench.

“We ran the full load series.”

Ollie did not answer.

“We ran the stair transfer.”

He still did not answer.

“We ran the asymmetric casualty load.”

“That was Ethan.”

“He was available.”

“He complained the whole time.”

“He improved realism.”

Jonah laughed again.

Mina picked up a torque driver, checked the bit and placed it in the tool tray.

“The vibration is inside tolerance. We logged it. If it changes, we inspect. If it doesn’t, we don’t take apart a working wheel assembly six days before competition because you are emotionally attached to disassembling things.”

“I am not emotionally attached to disassembling things.”

Mina looked at him.

Ollie considered the sentence.

“Not only emotionally.”

“That I believe.”

His wrist chimed.

A reminder appeared.

DEPARTURE WINDOW — 00:47:31

He dismissed it.

Mina saw him dismiss it.

“You know what your actual problem is?” she asked.

“I have several, apparently.”

“You think if you can still see a problem, you still own it.”

Ollie looked at her.

She said it casually. That made it worse.

He looked back at the rover.

“It is our machine.”

“Yes.”

“I built half the drive logic.”

“Yes.”

“I designed the rescue cradle release.”

“Yes.”

“So if it fails—”

“It will be our failure.”

Ollie stopped.

Mina tapped her own chest, then pointed at Jonah, then toward the empty workstations of the rest of the team.

“Plural.”

He did not like how reasonable that was.

She turned her tablet toward him.

The project permissions were open.

His name sat near the top.

OLIVER — SYSTEMS / DRIVE / RECOVERY

Below it was a row of access controls.

Mina touched one.

His live branch permissions disappeared.

Ollie stared at the screen.

“What did you do?”

“Removed your remote write access until you come back.”

“You can’t do that.”

“I’m project lead this week.”

“You can’t do that morally.”

“I can do it technically, which is the category we recognize in this room.”

Jonah raised one hand.

“I support the coup.”

Ollie took his hands out of his pockets.

Mina pointed at them.

He put them back.

“That is not funny.”

“It is extremely funny.”

“What if you need me?”

“We have your documentation.”

“What if the documentation is wrong?”

“Then we will discover a fascinating lesson about documentation.”

“What if the load curve changes?”

“We know how to read a load curve.”

“What if—”

“Ollie.”

He stopped.

Mina’s voice changed just enough that he knew she was done joking.

“Go.”

He looked at the rover again.

Six days.

He had imagined watching the challenge from Vela if the schedule allowed it. He had imagined sending advice between orbital placement shifts. He had imagined the team needing him in some small but decisive way, because of course they would.

It had not occurred to him until this exact moment that they might simply do well.

Without him.

That was an ugly thought.

Not because he wanted them to fail.

He absolutely did not want them to fail.

He just wanted his absence to have shape.

That was worse.

Mina must have seen something on his face because she stopped smiling.

“You’re allowed to leave,” she said.

“I know.”

“The rover does not become less yours because we keep working.”

“I know.”

“And if we win, I will still tell everyone you were unbearable.”

“That helps.”

“I thought it might.”

He walked to the rover and put one hand on the flat edge of the chassis.

Not dramatic.

Just metal and composite, cool under his palm.

A machine they had built because a rescue platform in a collapsed building should be able to go where a person could not.

A machine that did not care who got credit for the wheel vibration.

His wrist chimed again.

DEPARTURE WINDOW — 00:41:02

Mina pointed toward the door.

Ollie picked up his bag.

At the threshold, he turned.

“If the front-left load vibration goes above—”

“Goodbye, Ollie.”

“Forty-seven millimeters per second squared—”

“Goodbye.”

“Under combined lateral—”

Jonah threw a shop rag at him.

Ollie left.

—

His mother had packed food for a journey in which food was included.

This had not surprised him.

What surprised him was the quantity.

He found two wrapped sandwiches in the side pocket of his travel bag while she was standing at the kitchen counter checking his medical clearance for the third time.

“Why are these here?” he asked.

“In case you’re hungry.”

“There is food at the transfer center.”

“I know.”

“And in Dublin.”

“I know.”

“And during ascent.”

“I know.”

“So what scenario are the sandwiches for?”

“The one where you are hungry.”

Ollie looked at the sandwiches.

This was difficult to argue with because it was technically complete.

His father came in carrying the small hard case that contained Ollie’s placement tools.

Not the real tools. Those stayed with the site.

The approved personal kit contained a multimeter, calibration tags, optical inspection clip, two interface adapters that had been checked by three different systems because apparently a teenager bringing an unregistered electronic connector into orbital infrastructure was how civilizations ended, and a mechanical pencil.

The pencil had required no clearance.

It was therefore Ollie’s favorite object.

His father set the case down.

“You signed the custody form?”

“Yes.”

“Host contact?”

“Yes.”

“Emergency line?”

“Yes.”

“Earth-side liaison?”

“Yes.”

“Your mother?”

Ollie looked at him.

His father kept a straight face for almost two seconds.

“Very funny.”

His mother closed the medical file.

“You will message when you reach Dublin.”

“Yes.”

“And after orbital transfer.”

“Yes.”

“And when you reach Vela.”

“Yes.”

“And if your host changes.”

“Yes.”

“And if—”

“Mom.”

She stopped.

Ollie softened his voice.

“I will.”

She nodded once.

He zipped the bag.

His room looked strange behind him.

Not because he was leaving for six weeks.

He had left before. Camps. Competitions. Contact programs. Overnight labs. The kind of things Ethan put into calendars and Maya forgot to tell people about until the day before.

This was different because the room was suddenly full of objects that would not matter where he was going.

The old rover wheel on the shelf.

Three failed manipulator joints.

A cracked drone housing signed by everyone after the crash nobody admitted was avoidable.

A gravity demonstration souvenir from the First Contact festival months ago.

Things from the version of his life that had all happened under one sky.

His mother touched the strap of his travel bag.

“You excited?”

“Yes.”

“Scared?”

“No.”

His father made a sound.

Ollie looked at him.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You made a sound.”

“I breathed.”

“You breathed judgmentally.”

His father smiled.

Ollie looked back at his mother.

“Fine. A little.”

“That sounds more like a person.”

“I was already a person.”

“Debatable.”

His wrist chimed.

DEPARTURE WINDOW — 00:22:18

His mother looked at the timer.

“You’re going to be late.”

“I am never late.”

From the hall, his younger sister said, “You missed Thanksgiving dinner because you were testing a wheel.”

“That was not lateness. I arrived during dessert.”

“That is the definition of lateness.”

“It depends what part of dinner you value.”

His mother laughed despite herself.

Then she hugged him.

Ollie had expected it.

That did not make it easier.

He hugged her back.

His father was next. Shorter. Harder. One hand on the back of Ollie’s shoulder.

“Do the boring work,” he said.

Ollie pulled back.

“That is terrible advice.”

“It got you the rover.”

Ollie thought of Mina blocking the service panel.

“That is disturbingly coordinated.”

“No conspiracy,” his father said.

His mother looked innocent.

Ollie did not believe either of them.

At the door, his father handed him the hard case.

“Bring back stories,” his mother said.

“Not components,” his father added.

“That feels targeted.”

“It is.”

The pickup vehicle was waiting outside.

Ollie stepped onto the front walk, stopped, and looked up.

The sky was cold and clear.

No visible ships.

No dramatic streak of light announcing that the world had changed.

Just stars.

For most of human history, every person who had ever looked at them had been standing on a planet.

That was not true anymore.

He should probably have thought something larger than that.

Instead he wondered whether Mina had rechecked the front-left load curve after disabling his access.

Then his mother opened the door behind him.

“Ollie.”

“I’m going.”

He got in the car.

—

Ethan was already at the transfer hub.

Of course he was.

He stood under the DEPARTURES — ORBITAL / LUNAR CONNECTIONS sign with one backpack, one approved equipment case and the expression of someone who had personally arranged the existence of public transportation.

Aoife sat on her suitcase beside him with headphones around her neck and a recorder in her hand.

Samir had a sports bag.

Maya had too much luggage.

Ollie stopped walking.

He looked at Maya’s luggage.

Then at Maya.

Then at the luggage again.

“No.”

Maya folded her arms.

“You don’t know what’s in it.”

“I don’t need to.”

“There are six weeks.”

“There are stores in orbit.”

“You don’t know that.”

“There are cities in orbit.”

“Cities can have bad stores.”

Ethan looked at Ollie’s hard case.

“You brought tools.”

“Approved tools.”

“Exactly.”

“That is different.”

Maya pointed at her largest bag.

“Approved shoes.”

“How many?”

“That question is hostile.”

Samir shifted the sports bag higher on his shoulder.

Ollie looked at it.

“What’s that?”

“Shoes.”

Maya turned toward him with both palms open.

“Thank you.”

“For sport,” Samir said.

“Traitor.”

Aoife lifted the recorder.

“Can everyone repeat the last thirty seconds but with better timing?”

“No,” Ethan said.

She smiled.

Ollie set down his bag.

For the first time since leaving the maker bay, the tight place behind his ribs loosened.

The five of them had been in stranger rooms than this.

Not physically.

Physically, this was a transfer terminal in Northern Virginia with gray flooring, charging points, badly designed seats and a ceiling high enough to make everyone feel slightly processed.

But months earlier they had sat together waiting to discover whether a system they barely understood would let them continue.

They had argued with adults who were right about some things and wrong about others.

They had broken trust, repaired parts of it, found records nobody wanted to explain, made choices they had not been ready for and somehow finished the year with an official title that sounded far more impressive than the five people holding snacks under fluorescent lighting.

Experimental Human Youth Cohort — Limited Access.

Maya called it “the longest possible way to say supervised teenagers.”

Ethan had argued that this was unfair.

Maya had asked which word was inaccurate.

He had stopped arguing.

Aoife switched off the recorder.

“You’re late,” Ethan told Ollie.

Ollie looked at the departure board.

“I am twenty-three minutes early.”

“You said thirty.”

“I was detained by authoritarian project governance.”

“Mina?” Samir asked.

“She removed my branch access.”

Samir nodded solemnly.

“Good.”

“You are all terrible friends.”

“We’ve discussed this,” Aoife said.

Maya kicked one of her bags toward him.

“Help me with that.”

“No.”

“You said friends.”

“I said terrible friends.”

Ethan looked around the group.

“Okay. We have twenty-one minutes before first document check. Dublin transfer windows are already synced. Vela host assignments updated this morning. There was a route adjustment on arrival because Ring Three maintenance changed the central spine connection, so—”

Aoife closed her eyes.

Maya groaned.

Samir took out his phone.

Ollie watched Ethan keep going.

Something about host sectors. Something about transit tokens. Something about how they should establish a group check-in window every evening so nobody disappeared into work and forgot everyone else existed.

Ollie almost interrupted.

Then Ethan said, “I made a shared schedule.”

All four of them spoke at once.

“No.”

Ethan looked offended.

“You haven’t seen it.”

“We don’t need to,” Maya said.

“It accounts for shift variation.”

“No.”

“Transit time.”

“No.”

“Host commitments.”

Aoife pointed at him.

“Especially no.”

Ethan looked at Samir.

Samir shook his head.

He looked at Ollie.

Ollie considered the situation.

“I would like to see the schedule.”

Ethan brightened.

“Thank you.”

“So I know what not to do.”

Aoife laughed.

Ethan stared at him.

“That was cheap.”

“It was efficient.”

The boarding call sounded before Ethan could answer.

The five of them moved toward security and document control.

For forty seconds they were a group again, bags rolling beside them, voices overlapping, Ethan trying to direct traffic nobody had asked him to direct.

Then the first checkpoint split them.

Different health-clearance queues.
Different equipment screening.
Different transfer prep.
Different host codes.

Aoife went left.

Samir straight ahead.

Maya was redirected because of something in one of the bags she refused to identify.

Ethan tried to ask whether they could regroup past screening and was told, politely, that they could regroup if their routes happened to permit it.

Ollie was sent right with his equipment case.

He looked back once.

Ethan was still talking to the agent.

Aoife caught Ollie’s eye from the other line and made a small circling gesture beside her head.

Ollie nodded.

Then a door closed between them.

His wrist updated.

DUBLIN TRANSFER — STAGE 1

Below that:

VELA RESIDENCY
HABITAT SYSTEMS / MAINTENANCE PLACEMENT
REPORT 06:10 LOCAL STATION TIME

Ollie read the last line twice.

Maintenance placement.

Real work.

The thought landed differently now that the rover was already continuing without him.

He tightened his hand around the equipment case.

A technician at the next station looked up.

“You planning to repair the terminal?” she asked.

Ollie loosened his grip.

“No.”

“Good. We just had it serviced.”

She scanned his clearance and waved him forward.

For the first time in his life, Ollie walked toward a place where nothing he had built was waiting for him.

He found that he liked the feeling.

He found that he hated it.

Both things were true long enough to get him through the door.
