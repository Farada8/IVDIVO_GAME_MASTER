# ORBITAL YOUTH
## BOOK TWO
### CHAPTER TWO — DUBLIN VERTICAL

**Status:** WORKING DRAFT v0.1 / NOT CANON

Aoife’s first usable recording of Dublin was a man arguing about pears.

Not the launch towers.

Not the vertical transit chambers sunk beneath the old city.

Not the upper transfer structures visible between preserved roofs where, a generation earlier, the skyline had ended.

Pears.

“They’re bruised,” the man said.

A woman answered, “They were inspected.”

“They were inspected before somebody stacked medical cargo on them.”

“They’re not under medical cargo.”

“They were.”

Aoife stood six meters away near a load-routing barrier with the recorder low against her coat.

The man wore a green logistics jacket with one sleeve rolled to the elbow. The woman had a tablet tucked under her arm and the expression of someone who had spent the morning being told things were not where they were supposed to be.

Between them sat three gray freight modules marked for orbital transfer.

One had a handwritten produce sticker stuck across an official mass declaration.

Aoife smiled.

This was good.

Not because bruised pears mattered historically.

Because they mattered now.

Behind the argument, Dublin kept moving.

People crossed the old stone concourse with orbital luggage cases, school bags, work kits, prams, cargo harnesses and takeaway cups. A group of tourists had stopped under the surviving arch of an older station entrance to photograph the transparent lift columns that descended through the floor farther than Aoife could see.

Above them, through a cut in the roofline, one of the city’s vertical transfer structures rose into low cloud.

Nobody nearby was looking at it.

That was better too.

Aoife adjusted the recorder gain.

The logistics woman said, “If they’re bruised, file spoilage.”

“I’m filing reality.”

“Reality needs a code.”

“That’s the whole problem.”

Aoife nearly laughed into the microphone.

A notification appeared on her wrist.

ETHAN: WHERE ARE YOU?

Then, immediately below it:

ETHAN: Never mind I can see you.

Aoife looked up.

Across the concourse, Ethan was standing beside a column with Samir and two luggage carts.

He pointed at her.

She pointed at the pears.

He looked at the freight modules.

Then back at her.

His face asked a question.

Aoife shook her head.

Not now.

Ethan accepted this for almost four seconds.

Then her wrist chimed again.

ETHAN: Our transfer starts in 17 minutes.

Aoife typed back:

AOIFE: Pear emergency.

She saw him read it.

He did not look amused.

Samir did.

Aoife switched off the recorder and walked toward them.

“You have twelve minutes,” Ethan said.

“You just said seventeen.”

“Twelve until I want us moving.”

“That is not how time works.”

“It is how buffers work.”

Samir pushed one luggage cart with his sports bag balanced on top.

“Were the pears okay?” he asked.

“No.”

He nodded. “That’s bad.”

“Thank you.”

Ethan looked between them.

“Is this going into the documentary?”

“Maybe.”

“Why?”

Aoife looked around the concourse.

“What would you record?”

Ethan glanced upward through the open roofline.

The transfer structure disappeared into cloud.

“That.”

“Everyone records that.”

“It’s extraordinary.”

“It’s already in forty thousand arrival videos.”

“It is still extraordinary.”

Aoife looked at him.

He was right, which was inconvenient.

The structure was extraordinary.

Future Dublin had not erased the city beneath it. That was what made the place strange.

Stone walls remained where they had been. Streets older than most nations still crossed the surface. Churches, Georgian brick, shopfronts, pubs and weather-darkened facades had not been flattened to make room for a generic future.

The future had gone around them.

And under them.

And very, very far above them.

The result was less like an old city upgraded and more like somebody had discovered Dublin was only the ground floor.

Aoife took out the recorder again.

Ethan watched.

“You’re recording now?”

“Ambient.”

“You said everyone records that.”

“I changed my mind.”

He smiled.

“Growth.”

“Don’t ruin it.”

They crossed the concourse together.

Samir stopped at a glass section of floor.

Below them, a transit shaft dropped through several lit levels. Passenger capsules moved vertically and then vanished sideways into connecting tunnels.

Farther down, freight platforms carried containers in organized silence.

Samir looked for several seconds.

“Where’s Ollie?”

“Equipment processing,” Ethan said.

“Maya?”

“Baggage review.”

Aoife looked at him.

“What did she bring?”

“She won’t say.”

“That makes it worse.”

“I know.”

Samir stepped off the glass.

“Do we meet them after?”

Ethan opened the itinerary.

“That was the plan.”

Aoife heard the phrase and looked at him.

Ethan noticed.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You did a face.”

“I did not.”

“You absolutely did.”

“I’m just enjoying your relationship with the word plan.”

“My relationship with plans is healthy.”

Samir said, “You made us a dinner schedule before we left Earth.”

Ethan looked at him.

“You too?”

“I didn’t say it was bad.”

“Thank you.”

“I didn’t say it was good.”

Aoife lifted the recorder toward Samir.

He pointed at it.

“No.”

She lowered it.

That part had become easier since the first year after Contact.

Not asking.

Not assuming.

People liked to talk about recording as though the ethical question began when the red light came on.

Usually it began earlier.

At the point where you decided something belonged to you because you had noticed it.

Aoife pushed the thought away before it could become a sentence she would hate later.

The concourse widened around an older civic wall preserved inside the transfer complex.

A row of departure gates had been built behind it without touching the stone. Their surfaces changed language and interface density depending on who approached.

One panel shifted from Irish to English as Aoife came near, then offered a smaller line of trade glyphs she could not read without assistance.

A family in front of them stopped at the same gate.

Two adults. One child. One older woman using a mobility support frame.

The gate changed shape.

Not physically.

The information hierarchy reconfigured.

Large route prompts moved lower. Audio guidance became more prominent. A handrail indicator lit beside the mobility frame.

The older woman did not look impressed.

She looked annoyed that the family ahead of them was taking too long.

Aoife recorded the gate tone.

That was useful.

Ethan watched her again.

“No narration?”

“Not yet.”

“You usually narrate.”

“I usually regret it later.”

The gate opened.

Their group moved forward.

A staff member checked Aoife’s residency clearance and pointed left.

“Media/documentation track, lower prep corridor.”

Aoife looked at Ethan.

He was being directed straight ahead.

Samir went right.

Ethan frowned.

“We reconnect at ascent staging?”

The staff member checked the system.

“If your preparation windows align.”

“They should.”

The staff member looked at Ethan in the neutral way adults looked at teenagers who had mistaken preference for infrastructure.

“Then they will.”

Aoife smiled.

Ethan saw.

“Don’t.”

“I said nothing.”

“You’re enjoying this.”

“A little.”

Samir had already started toward his corridor.

Ethan turned after him.

“Message when you clear prep.”

Samir lifted one hand without turning around.

Aoife took two steps toward her own corridor.

Ethan said, “Aoife.”

She looked back.

He hesitated.

Not long.

Just enough.

“Don’t miss the window.”

There was something else under it.

She could have made him say it.

Instead she tapped the recorder against her coat.

“Try surviving twenty minutes without coordinating me.”

“I can do that.”

“Document it.”

She walked away before he answered.

—

The media/documentation prep corridor was not for media.

That became clear after six minutes.

It was for equipment with enough sensors to annoy flight-safety systems.

Aoife’s recorder was ordinary by orbital standards, which did not prevent it from being inspected, registered and temporarily limited during specific transfer stages.

The technician assigned to her had short gray hair and a Dublin accent so familiar Aoife almost forgot they were twenty meters below a transit complex connected to orbital ascent.

“Independent project?” the technician asked.

“Yes.”

“Commercial?”

“No.”

“School?”

“Also no.”

The technician looked at her.

Aoife said, “It started as school.”

“That sounds dangerous.”

“It became worse.”

“Usually does.”

She scanned the recorder.

The device displayed its registration state.

“Passive recording allowed through ground prep. Disabled during high-interference stage. Restores automatically after transfer confirmation.”

“Can I override if it doesn’t restore?”

“No.”

Aoife looked at her.

The technician looked back.

“No,” she repeated.

“Right.”

“Write that down if you like.”

Aoife almost did.

Instead she asked, “Do people complain?”

“About losing recording for forty minutes?”

“Yes.”

“Constantly.”

“What do you tell them?”

“That if forty minutes is enough to destroy the story, they probably didn’t have one.”

Aoife stared at her.

The technician handed back the recorder.

“That one was free.”

Aoife smiled.

“Can I quote you?”

“No.”

“Of course.”

The corridor opened into another transfer level.

This one had no visible old stone.

The walls were darker, the ceilings lower and the passenger flow faster. People moved toward numbered lift chambers in timed groups.

Aoife checked her route.

DUBLIN ORBITAL TRANSFER
PREP STAGE B
CHAMBER 14

Below it:

GROUP CONNECTION NOT GUARANTEED

She laughed once.

Ethan was going to hate that sentence.

Her wrist showed three messages.

MAYA: they confiscated nothing

MAYA: this is a victory

Then:

MAYA: temporary hold does not count

Aoife typed:

AOIFE: What did you bring?

Three dots appeared.

Then disappeared.

No answer.

Samir had sent a picture of his sports bag going through a scanner with the caption:

Apparently this is suspicious.

Ethan had sent:

I am at B-11. Anyone nearby?

Aoife checked the chamber numbers around her.

B-14.

She typed:

AOIFE: Close enough to be emotionally supportive.

ETHAN: Physically?

AOIFE: No.

She put the wrist down before he could respond.

The chamber doors opened.

Aoife entered with eleven other passengers.

There were no windows.

A child beside her complained immediately.

“Why can’t we see?”

His father said, “Because there’s nothing to see yet.”

“There’s always something to see.”

Aoife liked him.

A status band illuminated around the chamber wall.

PREP TRANSFER — 02:10

The floor moved.

Not dramatically.

That was the strange part.

Aoife felt the change in pressure through her knees before she understood they were descending.

The chamber dropped through Dublin’s transfer stack.

At one point her ears clicked.

At another, the wall display updated their relative position.

Surface level disappeared above them.

Then old transit.

Then logistics.

Then deep transfer.

The city did not end at the ground.

The child beside her pressed both palms to the wall as if he could feel the layers pass.

Aoife resisted the recorder.

She had already been told it was permitted.

That was not the same as needing it.

The chamber slowed.

Doors opened onto a long staging hall where the ceiling disappeared into structural darkness.

Large passenger transfer vehicles waited behind pressure barriers.

Somewhere beyond them, a cargo mover passed with a low mechanical hum.

Aoife stepped out and stopped.

For the first time that morning, nobody was arguing about pears.

People were quieter here.

Not solemn.

Just focused.

This was the line between city and ascent.

She raised the recorder.

The display flashed:

RECORDING SUSPENDED — TRANSFER SAFETY MODE

Aoife looked at it.

Then at the staging hall.

The technician’s voice came back to her.

If forty minutes is enough to destroy the story—

She put the recorder away.

—

Ollie saw the cargo problem before he saw Earth through glass.

This was not deliberate.

The equipment-processing line had delivered him to an ascent staging section two decks away from Aoife and one deck below Ethan.

His approved tool case had reappeared on a tracked cart with a new routing seal.

Two carts ahead of it, a service module stopped.

A technician frowned at a display.

Another technician came over.

They both looked at the module.

Ollie looked at it too.

The side panel showed a mass declaration, destination, environmental requirement and transfer priority.

Nothing obviously wrong.

The first technician scanned it again.

The display rejected the route.

“Why?” the second asked.

“Center allocation.”

“It’s within mass.”

“Not with the medical unit behind it.”

“Move the medical unit.”

“That changes sequence on three.”

“Then move—”

They stopped because they had noticed Ollie watching.

Ollie looked away.

A window occupied the far end of the staging section.

Beyond it, Dublin spread under cloud.

Old surface districts looked impossibly small from here. Above and beyond them rose structures that did not fit the city Ollie remembered from photographs: vertical transfer lines, high platforms, atmospheric traffic lanes, distant silhouettes half-hidden by weather.

He should have gone to the window.

Instead he kept listening to the cargo technicians.

The first said, “If we shift service two behind passenger six, center stays clean.”

“Passenger six is already locked.”

“Then split the service module.”

“It’s one unit.”

“Not internally.”

The second technician looked at the routing panel.

Then smiled.

“Fine.”

They opened the service module and separated two internal cargo cassettes onto different carts.

The routing warning disappeared.

Problem solved.

No speech.

No artificial intelligence announcing a breakthrough.

Two people who knew the system looked at the thing that was wrong until it stopped being wrong.

Ollie liked that.

A voice beside him said, “You planning to apply?”

He turned.

A maintenance technician in a blue work jacket was watching him.

“For what?” Ollie asked.

“The cargo shift.”

“No.”

“You’ve been staring at it for five minutes.”

“I was waiting to see what they changed.”

The technician nodded toward the window.

“Most first-timers stare at Dublin.”

Ollie glanced at it.

“It’s still there.”

The technician smiled.

“You’re on the wrong side of the glass if you want to be useful.”

Ollie looked back at the cargo carts.

Then at his own case.

A new route status appeared.

TRANSFER VERIFIED
VELA RESIDENCY — HABITAT SYSTEMS / MAINTENANCE

His wrist buzzed.

A group message from Ethan:

ETHAN: I have lost everyone.

MAYA: dramatic

SAMIR: I’m in C-4

AOIFE: emotionally nearby

Ollie typed:

OLLIE: Equipment line. Still alive.

Ethan answered immediately.

ETHAN: We regroup on orbital transfer side.

Maya sent:

MAYA: unless the universe protects us

Ollie smiled.

The staging call sounded.

Passengers began moving toward the transfer vehicle.

He picked up his equipment case.

The technician in blue nodded toward it.

“Tools?”

“Mostly adapters.”

“Good.”

“Why?”

“People with too many tools usually think the first job is to open something.”

Ollie looked at the case.

Mina would have liked this person.

That was not encouraging.

He joined the line.

Only when the doors began to close did he turn toward the window.

Dublin was still there.

Old roofs. New towers. Cloud. Transfer lines disappearing upward.

No single image explained what the city had become.

Ollie was glad Aoife’s recorder was probably off.

She would have hated an explanation that easy.
